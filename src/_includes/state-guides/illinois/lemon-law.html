The New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act

Illinois's lemon law is the New Vehicle Buyer Protection Act, 815 ILCS 380. It's a short statute with only eight sections, and its protection window is brief: the first year or 12,000 miles after delivery, whichever comes first. During that window, if the seller can't make a new vehicle match its express warranty after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must replace it or buy it back.

The Attorney General's lemon law fact sheet warns that many owners have lost their remedy by waiting too long, believing they were already pursuing a lemon claim through the dealer. A claim doesn't start at the dealership. It starts with the manufacturer.

Who and what is covered

A "consumer" under § 380/2 is an individual who buys a new vehicle, or leases one for at least a year, mainly for personal, household or family transportation. Fire departments, fire protection districts and township fire departments that buy or lease a new vehicle for a year or more also qualify. The law covers:

  • Passenger cars
  • Second Division vehicles (light trucks and vans) weighing under 8,000 pounds
  • Vehicles bought by a fire department, fire protection district or township fire department
  • Recreational vehicles, except camping or travel trailers that don't fit the Vehicle Code definition

The Attorney General's fact sheet says the law doesn't cover used cars, altered or modified vehicles, motorcycles or boats, and that it applies to new vehicles bought in Illinois.

The "seller" includes the manufacturer, its agents, its distributors and its authorized dealers. When a new vehicle has been modified by a converter, the converter and its dealers are sellers too. If a converted part causes the warranty failure, responsibility falls on the company that did the conversion.

What counts as a defect

A "nonconformity" is a failure to meet any express warranty that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety. The manufacturer can defend a claim by showing that abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications caused the problem.

Four repairs or 30 business days

Under § 380/3(b), the law presumes the seller has had a reasonable number of attempts when, inside the one-year or 12,000-mile period, either of these is true:

  • The seller, its agents or its dealers have tried to fix the same nonconformity four or more times, and it still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs of nonconformities for a total of 30 or more business days

Both conditions have to happen within the statutory period. A vehicle that reaches its fourth failed repair at month 13 doesn't fit the presumption. The clock pauses when repair service isn't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

Illinois adds one more condition in § 380/3(h). The presumption doesn't apply against the manufacturer or its dealers unless the manufacturer has received direct written notice from you, or from someone acting for you, and has had a chance to correct the defect. Because the notice must reach the manufacturer directly, telling only the dealer may not satisfy this requirement. Send a letter to the manufacturer's address in your owner's manual or warranty booklet, describe the defect and each repair visit, and keep proof of delivery.

The manufacturer's dispute program comes first

Section 380/4 says the refund-or-replace remedy doesn't apply until you've used the manufacturer's informal dispute settlement procedure, provided three things are true:

  • The manufacturer has set up such a procedure
  • The procedure substantially follows the Federal Trade Commission rules in 16 CFR part 703 and its decisions tell you that you can still go to court
  • The seller has given you adequate written notice that the program exists, which can be done by including it in the written warranty

The Attorney General describes these as industry third-party dispute resolution programs and says the details are usually in your owner's manual. To start, contact the manufacturer representative designated for your vehicle, who will send you the forms. The fact sheet says complaints must be filed within the 12 months after purchase, so it's worth opening a case as soon as the repairs start piling up.

If the board rules for you, you can expect a replacement of like or similar value, or a buyback minus a deduction for miles driven. According to the Attorney General, the manufacturer can't dispute the board's decision. You can. If you're dissatisfied with the decision or how it's carried out, you can file a civil suit under § 380/4(b), and the board's decision is admissible as evidence. The time your case spent in the program is added to the filing deadline.

What a buyback or replacement includes

If you qualify, the manufacturer must either give you a new vehicle of the same model line (or a comparable vehicle if that isn't available) or take the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price or lease cost. For a lease, the cost includes deposits, fees, taxes, down payments, periodic payments and anything else you paid the seller. Collateral charges are included in a purchase refund, but the sales taxes you paid are not. Instead, the retailer that sold the vehicle can claim a tax credit under the Retailers' Occupation Tax Act. Refunds go to you and to any lienholder according to each party's interest.

The manufacturer may deduct a reasonable allowance for your use. Illinois doesn't use a mileage formula for this. The allowance is the wear and tear from driving before you first reported the defect, plus driving during later periods when the vehicle wasn't in the shop. Days spent waiting for repairs don't add to the deduction. When the manufacturer gives you a replacement, the law treats that as a new sale to you.

Filing deadline and other remedies

A lawsuit under the Act must be filed within 18 months of the vehicle's original delivery, under § 380/6, plus any days spent in the dispute program. If you choose to proceed and settle under this Act, § 380/5 bars a separate claim under the Uniform Commercial Code. Before you choose a path, the Attorney General recommends talking with a private attorney about the other federal and state warranty laws that might apply.

Section 380/7 requires the seller, at delivery, to give you a written statement that clearly sets out your refund and replacement rights and the four-repair and 30-day presumptions. Keep that statement with your purchase papers.

Keeping records

The Attorney General stresses good records. Keep every receipt and repair order, note the reason and date of each visit, and write down how long the car stayed in the shop. Days out of service count only in business days, so a log noting drop-off and pickup dates makes the 30-day presumption much easier to prove.
