EV infrastructure fees in 2026

Vermont charges plug-in vehicles an EV infrastructure fee on top of the regular registration fee, under 23 V.S.A. § 361. For a battery electric pleasure car, the annual fee equals the $89 base registration fee, and the two-year fee is twice that amount. For a plug-in hybrid, the fee is half the annual base fee, or the full annual fee for a two-year registration. The DMV's fee table shows the combined totals: $178 a year or $341 for two years for a battery electric car, and $135.50 or $256 for a plug-in hybrid.

Until the start of 2027, the statute sends these fees to the Transportation Fund for Agency of Commerce and Community Development programs that expand access to Level 1 and Level 2 charging ports at workplaces and multiunit dwellings.

The mileage-based user fee from January 1, 2027

Act No. 168 of the 2025 adjourned session added Chapter 43 to Title 23, effective January 1, 2027. On that date, the battery electric infrastructure fee in § 361(b) is repealed, and battery electric pleasure cars start paying a mileage-based user fee (MBUF). Plug-in hybrids keep paying a fee under § 361(c), equal to half the annual base fee, with the money going to the Transportation Fund.

Under § 4302, the rate is $0.014 per mile, and the fee for a mileage reporting period can't exceed $178. A reporting period generally runs between required annual inspections, so the odometer reading taken at inspection under § 1222 feeds the calculation. Owners and lessees choose one of three payment options:

  • An annual lump sum, which the Commissioner calculates and mails within 14 days after the reporting period ends
  • Pay-as-you-go installments based on odometer reports, if the Commissioner chooses to offer that option, reconciled at the end of each period
  • A flat $178 per period, which requires no mileage reporting

A newly registered vehicle pays through pay-as-you-go or the flat rate during its first year. Payments are due by the next registration renewal, the end of the vehicle's Vermont registration, or its sale or lease termination, whichever comes first. You can switch options by notifying the Commissioner.

The fee doesn't apply to vehicles owned or operated by the federal government or the State of Vermont, or to short-term rental vehicles. Under § 4304, if the Commissioner can't determine your miles because you didn't file a report or get an inspection, you're charged a default of $178 at your next renewal, with a credit if an inspection within 60 days after that renewal shows you owed less. You can appeal an assessment to the Commissioner within 45 days after it's mailed. Section 4305 lets the Commissioner suspend or refuse to renew a registration, after notice and a chance for a hearing, for missing reports, misrepresentations or unpaid amounts.

State incentive programs

The U.S. Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) lists Vermont's state programs. Several consumer programs now appear there as archived, including the Agency of Transportation's EV incentive for low- and moderate-income buyers, the MileageSmart used high-efficiency vehicle incentive, and the Replace Your Ride program. Programs the AFDC still lists include:

  • The Department of Environmental Conservation's Automotive Emissions Repair Assistance Program, with vouchers of up to $2,500 to repair vehicles that failed the OBD II inspection, for households at or below 185% of the federal poverty level
  • Department of Housing and Community Development grants of up to $100,000 for Level 1 and Level 2 chargers at multifamily housing and at workplaces
  • Grants for chargers at locations open to the general public, up to $56,000 for Level 2 chargers and up to $160,000 for DC fast chargers
  • Vermont State Infrastructure Bank loans at a 1% fixed rate, up to $100,000, for public EV chargers and natural gas fueling stations
  • Diesel Emissions Reduction Grants for projects on heavy-duty vehicles, including buses and Class 5–8 trucks

Act 41 of 2023 requires investor-owned utilities to fund programs that supplement their EV incentive and charger grant programs, with priority for low-income communities. Act 144 of 2023 lets electric utilities use state funding between 2024 and 2026 to aim those programs at drivers with high gasoline or diesel use or with low to moderate incomes.

Other EV rules

  • Under 30 V.S.A. § 203, an entity that supplies electricity to the public only to charge EVs isn't a public utility, and it can charge by the kilowatt-hour.
  • Vermont has a goal of at least one public DC fast charger port within one mile of every interstate and highway exit and within 25 miles of other fast chargers along state highways, under 19 V.S.A. § 2906.
  • Section 304 of Title 23 required the DMV to begin issuing special license plates for EVs and plug-in hybrids by July 1, 2026, so first responders can identify them.
  • Vermont has adopted California's vehicle emissions standards, including the zero-emission vehicle sales requirements for vehicles up to 14,000 pounds and the Advanced Clean Trucks rules starting with model year 2026.
  • Neighborhood electric vehicles, designed for speeds up to 25 mph, may be driven on roads with posted limits up to 35 mph.
