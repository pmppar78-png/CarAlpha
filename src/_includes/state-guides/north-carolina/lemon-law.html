The New Motor Vehicles Warranties Act

North Carolina's lemon law is the New Motor Vehicles Warranties Act, Article 15A of Chapter 20 of the General Statutes (N.C.G.S. §§ 20-351 through 20-351.10). Enacted in 1987, it provides state and private remedies against manufacturers whose new vehicles don't conform to their express warranties. The text is on the General Assembly's website. The act doesn't set up a state arbitration program, so claims are resolved with the manufacturer, through a qualifying dispute program if the warranty requires one, or in court.

Who and what is covered

Under § 20-351.1, a "consumer" is a buyer who didn't purchase for resale, a lessee who leased from a commercial lender, lessor, manufacturer or dealer, and anyone else the express warranty lets enforce it. A covered "motor vehicle" is one sold or leased in North Carolina. House trailers and vehicles over 10,000 pounds are excluded.

A "new motor vehicle" is one whose certificate of origin has never been supplied to a consumer, or one that the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer says in writing is being sold as new.

Minimum warranty and repair duty

Section 20-351.2 requires express warranties on new vehicles to last at least one year or 12,000 miles. If the vehicle doesn't conform to the warranty during one year after delivery, or during the warranty term if that's longer, and you report it to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer within that period, the manufacturer has to make the needed repairs. That's true even if the work is done after the warranty runs out. A mileage-based warranty starts counting from the odometer reading on the day of delivery.

The presumption

Under § 20-351.5, the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if:

  • The same nonconformity has been presented for repair four or more times and still exists, or
  • The vehicle was out of service, either in repair or waiting for it, for a cumulative 20 or more business days during any 12-month period of the warranty.

The presumption also requires that you notified the manufacturer directly in writing and gave it a reasonable period, no more than 15 calendar days, to fix the problem. The manufacturer has to disclose this requirement clearly and conspicuously in the warranty or owner's manual, along with the name and address for the notice. If it doesn't, you don't have to give the notice.

The presumption is one way to prove your case. Section 20-351.5(b) also lets you show substantial impairment in other ways. The warranty term, the one-year period and the 20-day count are extended for any time repairs aren't available because of war, a strike or a natural disaster.

Refund or replacement

If the manufacturer can't fix a defect that substantially impairs the vehicle's value to you, and the defect showed up no later than 24 months or 24,000 miles after original delivery, § 20-351.3 gives you the choice of a comparable new vehicle or a refund. For a buyer, the refund includes:

  • The full contract price, including undercoating, dealer preparation, transportation, installed options and the non-refundable parts of extended warranties and service contracts
  • Collateral charges such as sales tax, license and registration fees and similar government charges
  • Finance charges incurred after you first reported the problem, and
  • Incidental and monetary consequential damages.

A lessee gets back the lease payments and amounts paid to enter the lease, including any capitalized cost reduction, plus incidental and consequential damages. The lessor receives the lease price plus 5%, less 85% of what you paid it. The lease ends without penalty.

The deduction for use

Under § 20-351.3(c), the refund is reduced by a reasonable allowance for use. It counts the miles you drove up to the third repair attempt for the same problem, or the 20th cumulative business day out of service, whichever came first. Those miles are multiplied by the purchase price, or the lessor's lease price, and divided by 120,000.

Manufacturer defenses

Section 20-351.4 makes it an affirmative defense that the nonconformity resulted from abuse, neglect, odometer tampering by the consumer, or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Key time limits at a glance

  • Repair duty: one year after delivery or the express warranty term, whichever is longer (§ 20-351.2).
  • Refund or replacement: the defect must have occurred within 24 months or 24,000 miles of delivery (§ 20-351.3).
  • Days out of service: 20 business days in any 12-month period of the warranty (§ 20-351.5).
  • Final repair chance: up to 15 calendar days after your written notice to the manufacturer (§ 20-351.5).
  • Before suing: written notice of intent at least 10 days ahead (§ 20-351.7).

Building your file

The presumption depends on dates, so ask for a dated repair order every time the vehicle goes in, including visits where the shop couldn't find the problem. Each order should describe your complaint in your own words and show when the car arrived and when it was ready. Look in the warranty booklet or owner's manual for the address the manufacturer gives for written notice, and send the letter in a way that proves delivery. Keep copies of the sales or lease contract, the finance agreement and receipts for towing, rentals and other costs, since § 20-351.3 lets you recover incidental and consequential damages.

Notice before suing

Section 20-351.7 lets an injured consumer sue the manufacturer, but you must first give it written notice of your intent to sue at least 10 days before filing. A manufacturer can require you to use an informal settlement procedure before going to court if the procedure substantially complies with the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act and its rules, and if the requirement is written clearly and conspicuously in the warranty and its instructions.

What a court can award

Under § 20-351.8, a court can grant an injunction or other equitable relief and award money damages. A jury may count every item that would be refundable under § 20-351.3. The damages are tripled if the manufacturer unreasonably refused to make repairs under § 20-351.2 or to provide a refund or replacement under § 20-351.3. The prevailing party's attorney fees can be shifted to the losing side if the court finds the manufacturer unreasonably failed to resolve the matter, or the person who sued knew or should have known the case was frivolous and malicious.

The Attorney General may also bring a civil action under § 20-351.6 when the public interest requires it.

Dealers and other remedies

Section 20-351.9 protects authorized dealers. A manufacturer can't hold a dealer liable for refunds or replacements unless there's evidence the dealer's repairs were substantially inconsistent with the manufacturer's instructions, and the act doesn't give consumers a claim against dealers. Section 20-351.10 keeps every other remedy available under other laws.

Resale of returned vehicles

A manufacturer, agent or dealer that resells a vehicle returned under this act, or a similar law in another state, must tell the next buyer before the sale that it was returned and what defects substantially impaired its value. That applies even if no court ever found a defect. A buyer who purchases it for resale with notice of the return must pass the same disclosures on.
