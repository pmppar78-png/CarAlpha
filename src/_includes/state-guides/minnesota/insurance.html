Minnesota's no-fault system

Minnesota is a no-fault state. Under the Minnesota No-Fault Automobile Insurance Act, Minn. Stat. §§ 65B.41 to 65B.71, every vehicle registered or principally garaged here needs a "plan of reparation security." That policy pays your own medical bills and lost income after a crash, whoever caused it, and also carries liability coverage for harm you cause to others. The Department of Commerce regulates auto insurers and publishes an Auto Insurance Guide that lists the required coverages.

Required coverage

Every Minnesota policy must include four parts:

  • Personal injury protection (PIP): $40,000 per person per accident, under Minn. Stat. § 65B.44.
  • Liability: $30,000 for injury to one person, $60,000 for injury to two or more people, and $10,000 for property damage, under § 65B.49, subd. 3.
  • Uninsured motorist: $25,000 per person and $50,000 per accident.
  • Underinsured motorist: $25,000 per person and $50,000 per accident.

The liability limits are often written as 30/60/10. The uninsured and underinsured coverages come from § 65B.49, subd. 3a. Collision and comprehensive are optional unless a lender or lessor requires them.

What the $40,000 in PIP covers

The statute splits PIP benefits into two buckets for each injured person:

  • $20,000 for medical expenses. This covers reasonable, necessary medical, surgical, dental, chiropractic and rehabilitative care, prescriptions, hospital and nursing care, and travel to treatment.
  • $20,000 for everything else. That's income loss, replacement services, funeral expenses and survivors' losses combined.

Income loss benefits pay 85% of lost gross income, up to $500 a week. They're reduced by any income from substitute work you do, or reasonably could have done. Missing work to get treatment for crash injuries counts as being unable to work. The law also bars no-fault insurers from steering claimants into managed-care networks.

PIP is primary for your own injuries. Uninsured motorist coverage, by the Commerce guide's description, picks up your medical expenses once PIP is used up when an at-fault driver has no insurance. Underinsured coverage adds to PIP when the at-fault driver's liability limits aren't enough.

How uninsured and underinsured limits work

Section 65B.49, subd. 3a, makes every owner keep uninsured and underinsured coverage as separate coverages, and it sets some limits on how they pay:

  • An insurer doesn't have to sell uninsured or underinsured limits higher than your bodily injury liability limit.
  • These coverages don't repay losses that PIP already paid or would pay.
  • If you're hurt in a vehicle, that vehicle's limit applies first. Your own policy may add excess protection, but only by the amount its limit is higher.
  • If you're hurt as a pedestrian, you can pick the limit for any one vehicle on a policy that covers you.
  • Limits for two or more vehicles can't be stacked together for one accident.

Where liability coverage applies

Minnesota's residual liability coverage pays damages you're legally obligated to pay for injury and property damage from owning, maintaining or using a motor vehicle. That includes a vehicle you're driving with permission. It applies anywhere in the United States, its territories, or Canada. Once an injury happens, the insurer's obligation to the injured person is fixed and can't be canceled by a later agreement with you.

Driving without insurance

Under Minn. Stat. § 169.797, an owner who drives or lets someone drive a vehicle that the owner knows lacks the required coverage commits a crime. So does a driver who knows the owner has no coverage, and so does an owner who falsely claims coverage when registering. The penalties:

  • A first violation is a misdemeanor.
  • A violation within ten years of the first of two prior convictions is a gross misdemeanor.
  • An uninsured driver who causes or contributes to a crash that kills someone or causes substantial bodily harm commits a gross misdemeanor.
  • The court must impose a fine of at least $200, though community service may replace it if you're indigent.

If you're the owner and had coverage but couldn't show it at the stop, you won't be convicted if the court administrator receives proof of insurance by your first court appearance date.

What drivers pay

The NAIC's 2024 Auto Insurance Database supplement lists Minnesota's combined average premium for liability, collision and comprehensive at $1,372.61. In 2020 it was $972.91. The average expenditure, which counts what drivers actually bought, was $1,231.57 in 2024. The Insurance Research Council estimates that 11.3% of Minnesota drivers were uninsured, ranking the state 34th.

Choosing coverage

A serious injury can use up $20,000 in medical PIP quickly. Optional added PIP and higher uninsured and underinsured limits are worth pricing. Liability of $30,000 per person and $10,000 for property damage won't go far in a crash with a newer vehicle or multiple injuries, and any shortfall can come back to you personally.
