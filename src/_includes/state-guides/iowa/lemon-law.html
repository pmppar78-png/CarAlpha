How Iowa's lemon law works

Iowa's lemon law is Iowa Code chapter 322G, titled "Defective Motor Vehicles." It covers vehicles bought or leased on or after July 1, 1991. The legislature's statement of intent in § 322G.1 calls a car "a major consumer acquisition," says a good-faith warranty complaint should be resolved by the manufacturer within a set period, and gives consumers a way to get a replacement or a full refund when a vehicle can't be made to match its warranty. The Attorney General enforces the law and writes its rules.

Iowa's process has a built-in final step. After a set number of failed repairs, you send the manufacturer a written notice by certified or registered mail, or by overnight service, and the manufacturer gets one final chance to fix the problem. Keep proof that the notice was received, because several deadlines are counted from that date.

What and who is covered

Section 322G.2 defines a motor vehicle as a self-propelled vehicle bought or leased in Iowa and designed mainly to carry people or property on public roads. These are excluded:

  • Mopeds, motorcycles and autocycles
  • Motor homes
  • Vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating over 15,000 pounds

A "consumer" is the buyer or lessee of a new or previously untitled vehicle, other than for lease or resale, or anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty during the lemon law rights period. A lessee counts if the written lease runs one year or more and makes the lessee responsible for repairs. Under § 322G.15, the law also covers vehicles first bought or leased in another state, as long as you live in Iowa when you assert your rights.

A "nonconformity" is a defect, malfunction or condition that makes the vehicle fail to conform to the warranty. It doesn't include problems caused by an accident, abuse, neglect, or modification by someone other than the manufacturer or its authorized service agent. To "substantially impair" means to make the vehicle unfit, unreliable or unsafe for ordinary use, or to significantly lower its value.

The lemon law rights period

The rights period ends at the earliest of these: the end of the manufacturer's written warranty, two years after original delivery, or the first 24,000 miles you drive. If you report a nonconformity during that period, the manufacturer must make the needed repairs even if they happen afterward. If a reported problem still isn't cured when the period ends, the presumption window under § 322G.4(3) extends up to two years after delivery or 24,000 miles, whichever comes first. Iowa counts days as calendar days.

The final repair attempt

Section 322G.4(1) lets you send the manufacturer written notice in any of these situations:

  • After three attempts to repair the same nonconformity that substantially impairs the vehicle
  • After one attempt to repair a nonconformity likely to cause death or serious bodily injury
  • After the vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a total of 20 or more days

After a repair-attempt notice, the manufacturer has 10 days to offer you a reasonably accessible repair facility. Once you deliver the vehicle there, it has 10 more days to fix it. If the manufacturer misses either deadline, it loses its right to a final attempt. After a 20-day notice, the manufacturer gets 10 more cumulative out-of-service days to fix the vehicle.

When the law presumes enough attempts

The law presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts if any of these happens during the rights period:

  • The same substantially impairing nonconformity has been examined or repaired at least three times, plus a final attempt if one was made, and it still exists
  • A nonconformity likely to cause death or serious injury has been examined or repaired at least once, plus a final attempt if one was made, and it still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair of substantially impairing nonconformities for a total of 30 or more days, not counting routine maintenance

The 30 days can be extended for any time repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or natural disaster. A manufacturer or service agent may not refuse to examine or repair a nonconformity to avoid liability.

Your repair paperwork

Each time the vehicle comes back from warranty work, § 322G.3 requires a fully itemized repair order. It must list the problem you reported, any diagnosis, the parts and labor, the odometer reading, and the dates the vehicle was dropped off and the work was finished. If you ask, the manufacturer or dealer must also give you diagnostic computer printouts and any technical service bulletins for your year and model. At the time of sale, the manufacturer must give you a written statement of your rights, prepared by the Attorney General, plus the address and phone number of its regional office where you can file a claim.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer hasn't fixed a substantially impairing nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts, it must replace or repurchase the vehicle within 40 days after receiving payment of a reasonable offset for use. You have an unconditional right to choose a refund instead of a replacement. Either way, the manufacturer must pay collateral charges, such as dealer-installed items, earned finance charges, use tax and title charges, and incidental charges like towing and alternative transportation. Incidental charges don't include loss of use, lost income or personal injury. If you choose a replacement, the manufacturer must give you a substitute vehicle to use until it arrives.

The offset for use counts your miles up to the earliest of these: the third repair attempt on the same nonconformity, the first repair attempt on a dangerous one, or the 20th cumulative day out of service. Those miles are multiplied by the purchase price and divided by 120,000. On a $36,000 purchase with 8,000 qualifying miles, the offset would be $2,400. If you choose a refund larger than the offset, you don't pay the offset up front; the manufacturer deducts it instead. For a lease, you get back your deposit and payments minus the offset, the lease ends without an early-termination penalty, and the lessor is paid separately.

Certified dispute programs

Under § 322G.6, a manufacturer may ask the Attorney General to certify its informal dispute program. If the program is certified and the manufacturer told you how and where to file, you must use the program before you can claim a refund or replacement. A certified program can't charge you, must offer an oral presentation, and must decide within 60 days of your notification, extended by seven days if you hadn't already notified the manufacturer. If you win, you have 25 days to accept. The manufacturer then has up to 30 days to comply.

Going to court

Under § 322G.8, you can sue in district court within one year after the rights period ends, or after its extension, in either of two cases: a certified program didn't decide in time, or the manufacturer has no certified program and you sent the required notice. If you accepted a program decision and the manufacturer didn't carry it out, you have six months after its deadline to sue. Either side may appeal a program decision for a new trial within 50 days of receiving it, or within 25 days of the consumer's acceptance, whichever comes first.

A consumer who wins recovers pecuniary losses, the refund or replacement, reasonable attorney's fees and costs. When a court upholds a program decision in your favor, it adds $25 a day for each day past the 25-day period after the manufacturer received your acceptance. The award is doubled, and may be tripled, if the manufacturer appealed in bad faith or to harass. The Attorney General may also seek up to $1,000 a day from a manufacturer that neither complies with nor appeals a decision you accepted.

Dealers, waivers and resale

Franchised dealers generally can't be sued under the chapter, and manufacturers can't charge lemon-law costs back to them without a court finding of improper repairs. Any agreement that waives your lemon-law rights is void. A violation by a manufacturer is an unfair or deceptive trade practice under § 714.16. Returned vehicles get a title designation, and the defect must be clearly disclosed to the next buyer or lessee.
