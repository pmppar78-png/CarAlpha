Virginia's lemon law

Virginia's lemon law is the Virginia Motor Vehicle Warranty Enforcement Act, Chapter 17.3 of Title 59.1 of the Code of Virginia, §§ 59.1-207.9 through 59.1-207.16:1. In § 59.1-207.10, the General Assembly calls a motor vehicle a major consumer purchase and says a defective one creates a hardship for the consumer. Its stated intent is for the manufacturer or its agent to resolve a good-faith warranty complaint within a specified period, and to give consumers a statutory path to a replacement or a full refund when a vehicle can't be brought into conformity with the manufacturer's express warranty. The chapter doesn't limit rights or remedies you have under any other law.

Who and what is covered

Under § 59.1-207.11, a "consumer" is the purchaser, other than for resale, or the lessee of a motor vehicle used in substantial part for personal, family or household purposes. Anyone the vehicle is transferred to for those purposes while a warranty applies is also covered, as is anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty.

The chapter defines "motor vehicle" as these vehicles only, using the definitions in § 46.2-100:

  • Passenger cars
  • Pickup and panel trucks
  • Motorcycles and autocycles
  • The self-propelled motorized chassis of motor homes
  • Mopeds
  • Demonstrators and leased vehicles issued with a warranty

"Warranty" covers any implied warranty, any written manufacturer's warranty, and any affirmation of fact or promise the manufacturer made in the sale or lease that became part of the basis of the bargain. It addresses materials, workmanship, and fitness for ordinary use or reasonable intended purposes through the lemon law rights period.

The lemon law rights period

The lemon law rights period ends 18 months after the original delivery of the new vehicle to the consumer. That's the window in which you can report a nonconformity to the manufacturer and pursue your rights under the chapter. The definition sets no mileage limit.

Section 59.1-207.13(C) extends the period if the manufacturer has been notified but hasn't effectively repaired the nonconformity by the time the period ends.

Under § 59.1-207.12, a nonconformity you report to the manufacturer, its agents or an authorized dealer during the manufacturer's warranty period has to be repaired, even if the repairs happen after the warranty expires.

What counts as a nonconformity

A nonconformity is a failure to conform to a warranty, or a defect or condition, that significantly impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety. That includes problems that don't affect driveability. "Significant impairment" means the problem makes the vehicle unfit, unreliable or unsafe for ordinary use or reasonable intended purposes.

A "serious safety defect" is a life-threatening malfunction or nonconformity that impedes your ability to control or operate the vehicle for ordinary use, or that creates a risk of fire or explosion.

When the presumption applies

Under § 59.1-207.13(B), the law presumes that the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts and that the vehicle is significantly impaired if, during the lemon law rights period, any of the following happens:

  • The same nonconformity has been repaired three or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or its authorized dealers, and it still exists.
  • A serious safety defect has been repaired one or more times, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative total of 30 calendar days.

The 30-day test doesn't apply if repairs couldn't be performed because of conditions beyond the control of the manufacturer, its agents or dealers, such as war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

Notifying the manufacturer

Before you use the remedies in § 59.1-207.13, subsection (E) makes you, or your representative, responsible for notifying the manufacturer that the nonconformity needs correction or repair, unless the manufacturer has already been notified. If you mail the notice to an authorized dealer, the dealer must forward it to the manufacturer.

The chapter counts the manufacturer as notified in any of these situations:

  • A written complaint about the defect has been mailed to it.
  • It has responded to you in writing about a complaint.
  • A factory representative has inspected the vehicle or met with you or an authorized dealer about the nonconformity.

If the manufacturer hadn't been notified and one of the presumption conditions already exists, it gets one more opportunity, of no more than 15 days, to correct or repair the problem.

Subsection (D) requires the manufacturer to disclose clearly and conspicuously, in the warranty or owner's manual, that written notification is required before you can qualify for a refund or replacement. It must also include the name and address where you send that notice.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer, its agents or dealers can't fix a nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts during the rights period, § 59.1-207.13(A) requires the manufacturer to do one of two things:

  • Replace the vehicle with a comparable motor vehicle acceptable to you
  • Take the vehicle back and refund the full contract price, including all collateral charges and incidental damages, to you, any lessor and any lienholder as their interests appear

You have the unconditional right to choose a refund over a replacement, and you can keep driving the vehicle until you receive the replacement or the refund. A comparable vehicle is one identical or reasonably equivalent to yours as it was when you bought or leased it.

Collateral charges include sales tax, license, registration and title fees, finance charges and interest, transportation and dealer preparation charges, and charges for service contracts, undercoating, rustproofing or installed options that can't be recovered from someone else. In a lease, they also include capitalized cost reductions, trade-in credits, fees paid to obtain the lease, and insurance or other costs the lessor paid for your benefit. You can also recover mileage, expenses and reasonable loss of use caused by the repair attempts.

The manufacturer can subtract a reasonable allowance for use, for either a refund or a replacement, covering your use up to the date of the first notice of nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agents or dealer. That allowance can't exceed half of the IRS business mileage rate per mile, plus an amount for any loss in fair market value from damage beyond normal wear and tear that wasn't caused by the nonconformity.

For a leased vehicle, the vehicle goes back to the manufacturer and the lessor ends your lease without penalty. The lessor transfers title to the manufacturer as needed, whichever remedy you choose.

Under § 59.1-207.13(G), it's an affirmative defense that the alleged nonconformity doesn't significantly impair use, market value or safety, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration by the consumer.

Informal dispute settlement

Section 59.1-207.15 leaves it to you whether to use a manufacturer's informal dispute settlement procedure before exercising your rights under the chapter. If you use one and the decision awards a refund or a comparable vehicle, the manufacturer has 40 days from receiving your acceptance of the decision, or from the date of a court order, to comply.

If the manufacturer fails to comply and you sue, the court may triple the value of the award and grant other equitable relief, including additional attorney's fees.

Going to court

Under § 59.1-207.14, a consumer who suffers a loss because of a violation of the chapter can file a civil action. A consumer who wins recovers reasonable attorney's fees, expert witness fees and court costs. A defendant in a frivolous action brought by a consumer recovers the same.

Section 59.1-207.16 requires you to file within the lemon law rights period. If you tried in good faith to settle through the manufacturer's informal procedure and it didn't resolve the matter, you have the longer of 12 months from the manufacturer's final action in that procedure, if you used it within the rights period, or the original rights period.

Resale of returned vehicles

Under § 59.1-207.16:1, when a vehicle returned under this chapter, or by a judgment, decree or arbitration award in any state, is transferred to a Virginia dealer, the manufacturer or distributor must disclose that to the dealer. Before the vehicle is resold or leased again, the manufacturer must give the dealer a separate written disclosure in ten-point capital type stating that it was returned, the nature of the defect, and its condition at transfer. The dealer must share that notice with any prospective buyer or lessee and pass it on to the next one. A manufacturer or distributor that violates this section is guilty of a Class 3 misdemeanor.
