West Virginia's lemon law

West Virginia's lemon law is Article 6A of the West Virginia Consumer Credit and Protection Act, W. Va. Code §§ 46A-6A-1 through 46A-6A-9, covering new motor vehicle warranties. In § 46A-6A-1, the Legislature says the article's purpose is to make manufacturers meet their obligations under the express warranties they give consumers in the state, and that the manufacturer bears the total cost of doing so.

The same section protects dealers. Any agreement between a dealer and a manufacturer that shifts a duty under the article, or any part of its cost, to the dealer is against public policy, void and unenforceable. That includes arrangements that charge the dealer for, or cut the reimbursement due for, warranty work and parts the article requires.

Who and what is covered

Under § 46A-6A-2, a "consumer" is someone who buys a new motor vehicle, other than for resale, and uses it mainly for personal, family or household purposes. Anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while an express warranty is in effect also counts, as does anyone else entitled by the warranty's terms to enforce it.

A "motor vehicle" under the article is either of these:

  • A passenger automobile bought in West Virginia or registered and titled in the state, including a pickup truck or van registered as a Class A vehicle and the self-propelled chassis of a motor home registered as Class A or Class B
  • A self-propelled vehicle designed primarily for and used in farming, with 20 or more horsepower

Farm vehicle buyers are consumers too, along with later transferees during the warranty. A "manufacturer" is a business that, under normal conditions, makes, assembles or distributes at least 10 new motor vehicles to dealers in a year. The "manufacturer's express warranty" is its written warranty of the vehicle's condition and fitness for use, including any conditions for enforcing it.

The duty to repair

Under § 46A-6A-3(a), if your new vehicle doesn't conform to its express warranties, report the nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer. Do this during the warranty term or within one year after the original delivery, whichever period is longer. The manufacturer, agent or dealer then has to make the repairs needed to conform the vehicle, even if the repairs happen after the warranty expires.

If they can't fix a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use or market value after a reasonable number of attempts, § 46A-6A-3(b) requires the manufacturer to replace it with a comparable new vehicle that conforms to the warranties.

Authorized dealers aren't liable to the manufacturer for refunds or replacements unless there's evidence their repairs were done in a way substantially inconsistent with the manufacturer's instructions. The section doesn't give consumers a cause of action against a dealer.

The presumption of a reasonable number of attempts

Section 46A-6A-5 sets two ways to presume that a reasonable number of attempts has been made. The window for both is the express warranty term or one year after original delivery, whichever ends earlier:

  • The same nonconformity has been subject to repair three or more times and still exists, or the vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative 30 or more calendar days.
  • A nonconformity that's likely to cause death or serious bodily injury if the vehicle is driven has been subject to repair at least once and still exists.

Note the difference between the two clocks. The repair duty in § 46A-6A-3 runs for the longer of the warranty term or one year, while the presumption uses the earlier date. Miles don't affect either period.

The presumption applies against a manufacturer only if it has received prior written notice from you or on your behalf and has had at least one opportunity to cure the defect. The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30-day period are all extended for any time repair services weren't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

The written notice at purchase

At the time of purchase, § 46A-6A-6 requires the manufacturer, directly or through its agent or dealer, to give you a written statement on a separate piece of paper in ten-point all-capital type. In substance, it says that if the vehicle is defective, you may be entitled under state law to replacement or compensation, but first you must notify the manufacturer of the problem in writing and give it a chance to repair the vehicle.

Separately, § 46A-6A-3a requires authorized dealers to disclose in writing any repairs made after the vehicle left the manufacturer, including transit damage, when those repairs have a retail value of 5 percent of the manufacturer's suggested retail price. Identical replacement of stolen or damaged accessories, tires or antennae doesn't need to be disclosed. A vehicle that's been titled before, or damaged badly enough that its value and usability would be substantially impaired if left unrepaired, isn't a new motor vehicle for this section.

Third-party dispute resolution

Under § 46A-6A-8, the Attorney General writes the rules for third-party dispute mechanisms for warranty disputes. These mechanisms are supervised by the Consumer Protection Division of the Attorney General's office and must meet or exceed the federal informal dispute settlement requirements under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act.

If a qualified process exists and you get timely written notice of it, with a description of how it works and what it does, you have to use it before suing under § 46A-6A-4. You can go to court if no qualified process exists, if you're dissatisfied with the decision, or if the manufacturer, agent or dealer doesn't promptly carry it out. Limitation periods under federal and state law are paused from the day you file with the process until the decision, or until the deadline for complying with it, whichever is later.

The Consumer Protection Division also takes motor vehicle complaints, including warranty violations. It reviews each complaint, contacts the business, and works toward a voluntary resolution through mediation. You can file online, by email at consumer.complaint@wvago.gov, or by mail to P.O. Box 1789, Charleston, WV 25326. The consumer protection hotline is 1-800-368-8808.

Suing the manufacturer

Under § 46A-6A-4, you have a cause of action against the manufacturer in the circuit court of any county with venue. You can sue if a nonconformity substantially impairs the vehicle's use or market value and the manufacturer hasn't replaced it, or if the nonconformity remains after a reasonable number of attempts. The court can award all or part of the following:

  • Revocation of acceptance and a refund of the purchase price, including sales tax, license and registration fees and other reasonable purchase expenses, or damages for diminished value if there's no revocation
  • The cost of repairs reasonably needed to conform the vehicle to the warranty
  • Damages for loss of use, annoyance or inconvenience, including reasonable replacement transportation costs
  • Reasonable attorney fees

The manufacturer has two affirmative defenses: the nonconformity doesn't substantially impair use or market value, or it resulted from abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications or alterations by someone other than the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer. You have to file within one year after the express warranty term ends, and the action is available only against the manufacturer. Section 46A-6A-9 keeps any other right or remedy you or a dealer have under other law.

Resale of returned vehicles

A vehicle returned under § 46A-6A-3, or a similar law in another state, can't be resold in West Virginia unless the manufacturer corrects the nonconformity. The buyer must also get a separate written statement, in ten-point capitals, saying the vehicle was returned because it didn't conform to the express warranty and wasn't cured within a reasonable time under West Virginia law. Under § 46A-6A-7, a manufacturer can't require its dealers, directly or indirectly, to accept such a vehicle for resale.
