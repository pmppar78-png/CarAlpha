Oklahoma's lemon law

Oklahoma's lemon law is a single statute, 15 O.S. § 901, in the chapter on manufacturers' warranties. It makes a manufacturer repair a new vehicle that doesn't conform to its express warranty, and it requires a refund or replacement when a defect that substantially impairs the vehicle's use and value can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts. The Attorney General publishes a consumer guide, "Oklahoma's Lemon Law: Your Rights and Responsibilities," and § 901(G) requires the dealer to hand you that statement when you buy a new vehicle.

Who and what is covered

Section 901(A) defines a "consumer" three ways:

  • the buyer of a motor vehicle, other than for resale
  • anyone the vehicle is transferred to while an express warranty still applies to it, and
  • anyone else entitled by the warranty's terms to enforce it.

A "motor vehicle" is any motor-driven vehicle that must be registered under the Oklahoma Motor Vehicle License and Registration Act, excluding vehicles over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight. A recreational vehicle, as defined in 47 O.S. § 1-152.1, counts as a motor vehicle regardless of its weight.

The Attorney General's guide puts the definition of a lemon this way: a new vehicle may be a lemon if a defect covered by the warranty substantially impairs its use and value, and the defect isn't the result of abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

The written report comes first

Under § 901(B), the duty to repair is triggered when you report the nonconformity directly and in writing to the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer. The report has to be made during the express warranty term or during the one year after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, whichever date comes earlier. Once you've reported in time, the manufacturer, agent or dealer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity, even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period has ended.

The guide's advice on this point is "Act fast!" It also tells you to take the vehicle back so the manufacturer, its agent or the dealer gets a "reasonable number of attempts" to fix it.

When the law presumes enough attempts were made

Section 901(D) presumes that a reasonable number of repair attempts have been made if either of these happens within the express warranty term or the one year after original delivery, whichever ends first:

  1. the same nonconformity has been subject to repair four or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers, and it still exists, or
  2. the vehicle is out of service for repair for a cumulative total of 30 business days.

The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30-day count are all extended for any time repair services aren't available to you because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The presumption has a condition attached. Under § 901(C), it can't be used against a manufacturer unless the manufacturer itself received prior direct written notification from you or on your behalf and had an opportunity to cure the defect. A letter to the dealer may start the repair duty, but the presumption depends on notice reaching the manufacturer.

Informal dispute settlement

Section 901(F) applies when a manufacturer has set up an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies in all respects with the Federal Trade Commission's rules at 16 C.F.R. Part 703. In that case, the refund and replacement provisions of § 901(C) don't apply to a consumer who hasn't first used the procedure. The Attorney General's guide tells you to read your warranty or owner's manual to find out whether the manufacturer has one.

The guide also answers whether a state agency enforces the law: it says no state agency has enforcement authority over it and suggests contacting a private attorney if you think your rights have been violated.

Refund or replacement

If the defect can't be corrected after a reasonable number of attempts, § 901(C) gives the manufacturer two choices:

  • accept the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price, including all taxes, license and registration fees and similar government fees, but excluding interest, less a reasonable allowance for your use, or
  • replace it with a comparable new model acceptable to you.

If the two of you can't agree on a comparable model, the purchase price is refunded, less the use allowance. Refunds go to you and to any lienholder as your interests appear.

How the use allowance is calculated

The statute fixes the formula. Take the purchase or lease price of the new vehicle and multiply it by a fraction. The numerator is the miles directly attributable to your use beyond 15,000 miles, and the denominator is 120,000 miles. The first 15,000 miles you drive aren't counted.

The Attorney General's guide works an example. A consumer pays $15,000 for a car and drives it 17,000 miles. The 2,000 miles over 15,000 go in the numerator, so the fraction is 2,000/120,000, or 1/60. One-sixtieth of $15,000 is $250, and that's the allowance.

The manufacturer's defenses

Section 901(C) gives the manufacturer two affirmative defenses to any claim:

  • the alleged nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use and value, or
  • the nonconformity results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Going to court

Under § 901(J), a consumer who prevails in a civil action under the section recovers all costs and reasonable attorney fees as the court determines them. Section 901(E) says nothing in the law limits rights or remedies you have under any other law.

Excise tax credit on a replacement vehicle

Oklahoma's motor vehicle excise tax law, 68 O.S. § 2103(D), allows a credit when a new vehicle replaces a defective new original vehicle that you returned to the seller within six months of purchase, as certified by the manufacturer. The credit equals the excise tax paid on the original vehicle and is applied to the tax due on the replacement. It can't be refunded.

What happens to returned vehicles

Under § 901(H), a vehicle returned under the law may not be resold in Oklahoma unless one of these conditions is met:

  • the manufacturer provides the same express warranty it gave the original buyer, though the term can be limited to 12,000 miles or 12 months after resale, whichever comes earlier, or
  • the manufacturer, through the licensed dealer, gives the buyer a written statement on a separate piece of paper that clearly discloses why the vehicle was reacquired.

Section 901(I) bars resale altogether when a vehicle was returned under this law, or a similar statute in another state, because of a complete failure of the braking or steering system likely to cause death or serious bodily injury if the vehicle is driven.

The Attorney General's guide adds that the manufacturer must have reacquired vehicles that were registered in Oklahoma retitled, and must ask the Oklahoma Tax Commission to put a "Lemon Law Buyback" notation on the new title. That has to happen before the vehicle is sold, leased, transferred or exported to another state for sale, lease or transfer.

Protecting yourself

The guide recommends these habits from the day you buy:

  • Read the owner's manual and warranty, and follow the maintenance guidelines.
  • Keep detailed, dated maintenance records, including warranty work.
  • Write down the problems you notice and hand the list to the technician at each visit, even when you're bringing the vehicle back for the same problem.
  • Get and keep copies of repair orders, and make sure they show the work done and how long the vehicle was in the shop.
  • Keep records and receipts for related expenses, such as towing.
