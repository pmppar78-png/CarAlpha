South Dakota's lemon law

South Dakota's lemon law is chapter 32-6D of the Codified Laws, titled "Manufacturer's Warranty." Enacted in 1993, it requires a manufacturer to repair a new vehicle that doesn't conform to its express warranty and, when repeated attempts fail, to replace it or buy it back. The consumer chooses which of those two remedies to take.

Who and what is covered

Under SDCL 32-6D-1, a "consumer" is someone who buys a new or previously untitled vehicle, other than for resale, uses it in substantial part for personal, family or household purposes and is entitled under the warranty to enforce it.

A "motor vehicle" is any self-propelled vehicle intended mainly for use on public highways. The definition also takes in all-terrain vehicles with four or more wheels and a combustion engine of at least 200 cubic centimeters. It leaves out:

  • Electric bicycles
  • Multi-passenger quadricycles
  • Motor homes
  • Vehicles with a manufacturer's gross vehicle weight rating of 15,000 pounds or more

The law works through the manufacturer's "express warranty," defined as a written warranty, labeled as such, that the manufacturer of a new vehicle issues, including any terms or conditions that must be met before its obligations can be enforced.

The "manufacturer" is the business that makes, imports or distributes vehicles to dealers for retail sale. An "authorized dealer" operates under a dealer agreement with the manufacturer and holds a license under chapter 32-6B.

What counts as a defect

A "nonconforming condition" is one that doesn't meet the manufacturer's express warranty and significantly impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety. It must arise from ordinary use. Conditions caused by abuse, neglect, or modification or alteration the manufacturer didn't authorize are excluded, as is damage from an accident after the dealer delivered the vehicle.

A "notice of a nonconforming condition" is a written statement delivered to the manufacturer that describes the vehicle, the problem and every earlier repair attempt, naming who made each attempt and when.

The lemon law rights period

The lemon law rights period ends one year after the vehicle was first delivered to the consumer or after its first 12,000 miles, whichever comes first. Under SDCL 32-6D-2, if you deliver the vehicle to the manufacturer or an authorized dealer and give notice of the problem during that period, the manufacturer must make the needed repairs.

The repair duty continues after the rights period ends, as long as you first gave notice during the period. It stops at 24 months after delivery or 24,000 miles, whichever comes first.

When repair attempts are presumed reasonable

SDCL 32-6D-5 presumes you've allowed reasonable attempts to fix the problem if, within 24 months or 24,000 miles of delivery, whichever comes first, either of these happened:

  • The same problem was the subject of four or more repair attempts by the manufacturer or its authorized dealers, with at least one during the rights period, plus a final attempt by the manufacturer, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle was out of service in the custody of the manufacturer or an authorized dealer for repairs, including the final attempt and with one attempt during the rights period, for a cumulative 30 calendar days.

The 30-day count doesn't apply when repairs couldn't be performed because of conditions beyond the control of the manufacturer or dealers, such as war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer or its dealer can't fix a problem that first appeared during the rights period after reasonable attempts, SDCL 32-6D-3 requires the manufacturer to act through its authorized dealer. At your option, it must either:

  • Replace the vehicle with a comparable new vehicle and refund all collateral charges, including excise tax, license and registration fees and similar government charges, or
  • Take the vehicle back and refund the full contract price, including charges for undercoating, dealer preparation, transportation and installed options, the nonrefundable portions of extended warranties and service contracts, all collateral charges, finance charges you incurred after first reporting the problem, and incidental damages such as the reasonable cost of alternative transportation while you were without the vehicle.

Under SDCL 32-6D-4, refunds go to you and any lienholders as their interests appear. The manufacturer can offset a reasonable allowance for your use of the vehicle before you first reported the problem: the full purchase price multiplied by the miles driven before that first report, divided by 100,000. The allowance is limited to the amount directly attributable to your use before that first report, so miles driven afterward while the problem was being repaired don't add to it.

Final notice before a lawsuit

SDCL 32-6D-6 lets a consumer who is harmed by a manufacturer's failure to meet these duties bring a civil action. Before filing, you must send the manufacturer notice of the nonconforming condition by certified mail and demand that it be corrected or repaired. Because the law uses its defined term here, the letter should describe the vehicle, the problem and every earlier repair attempt, with who made each one and when.

If the presumption of reasonable attempts has already arisen when you send that notice, the manufacturer gets one final chance to fix the vehicle. Within seven calendar days of receiving the notice, it must tell you of a reasonably accessible repair facility. After you deliver the vehicle there, it has no more than 14 calendar days to correct the problem.

Informal dispute settlement

If the manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure conducted within South Dakota that complies with federal rules, you must first use up any remedy it offers before you can sue under the lemon law. The chapter doesn't set up a state-run arbitration board.

Defenses, fees and deadlines

Under SDCL 32-6D-7, a manufacturer can defend a claim by showing the problem doesn't significantly impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or modification or alteration by the consumer that the manufacturer didn't authorize.

If the manufacturer breached its obligations, you may also recover reasonable attorney fees (SDCL 32-6D-8). Any lawsuit must start within three years after the vehicle was first delivered to you (SDCL 32-6D-11).

Dealers aren't liable under the chapter, and consumers can't sue dealers under it. Manufacturers can't charge dealers back for any refund, replacement or other cost arising from the law (SDCL 32-6D-10).

Resale of returned vehicles

A vehicle returned to the manufacturer under South Dakota's law or a similar law in another state, whether through a lawsuit or an informal dispute proceeding, can't be resold in South Dakota unless the manufacturer tells the next buyer in writing that it was returned and describes the warranty problem. The manufacturer must also send the title to the Department of Revenue with an application for title in its own name. The department brands that title and every later one with the statement: "This vehicle was returned to the manufacturer because it did not conform to its warranty."
