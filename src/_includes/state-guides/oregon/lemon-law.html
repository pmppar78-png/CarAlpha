Oregon's lemon law

Oregon's lemon law is ORS 646A.400 to 646A.418, in the Trade Regulation chapter. It covers new passenger vehicles that don't conform to the manufacturer's express warranty. If a defect that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must replace the vehicle or take it back and refund the price.

Who and what is covered

Under ORS 646A.400(2), a "consumer" is:

  • the buyer or lessee, other than for resale, of a new motor vehicle normally used for personal, family or household purposes
  • anyone the vehicle is transferred to for those same purposes while an express warranty still applies, and
  • anyone else the warranty entitles to enforce it.

A "motor vehicle" is a passenger motor vehicle as defined in ORS 801.360 that's either bought in Oregon or bought elsewhere and registered in Oregon. The law also reaches motor homes, meaning new or demonstrator units built on or permanently attached to a self-propelled chassis, chassis cab or van and designed as temporary living quarters. Trailers and campers aren't motor homes. Neither are vans or vehicles built by a company whose main business is making other kinds of motor vehicles.

For a motor home, the living facility components are excluded. The statute lists flooring, plumbing fixtures, appliances, the water heater, fabrics, door and furniture hardware, lighting fixtures, generators, roof heating and air conditioning units, cabinets, countertops, furniture and audio-visual equipment.

When the remedy is available

ORS 646A.402 sets three conditions:

  1. the new vehicle doesn't conform to the manufacturer's express warranties
  2. you report each nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer for repair during the two years after original delivery, or before the odometer reaches 24,000 miles, whichever comes first, and
  3. the manufacturer has received direct written notification from you or on your behalf and has had an opportunity to correct the defect.

The statute says a request for the manufacturer's informal dispute settlement procedure counts as that notification.

The presumption of a reasonable number of attempts

ORS 646A.406(1) presumes a reasonable number of attempts were made if, within the same two-year or 24,000-mile period, any of these is true:

  • the manufacturer, its agent or a dealer has repaired the same nonconformity three or more times and had an opportunity to cure it, and it still exists
  • the vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative 30 or more calendar days, or 60 or more for a motor home, or
  • a nonconformity likely to cause death or serious bodily injury has been repaired at least once, the manufacturer has made a final repair attempt, and the problem still exists.

A repair that has to happen after the period ends still counts. The mileage period, the two years and the 30-day count are extended for any time repair services aren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

Under ORS 646A.406(4), the presumption can't be used against the manufacturer unless it received prior direct written notice from you or on your behalf and had an opportunity to cure the defect.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer can't correct the defect after a reasonable number of attempts, ORS 646A.404(1) requires the manufacturer to either:

  • replace the vehicle with a new motor vehicle, or
  • accept its return and refund the full purchase or lease price and the collateral charges you paid, less a reasonable allowance for your use.

Refunds go to you and any lienholder as your interests appear.

"Collateral charges" are defined in ORS 646A.400(1). They include:

  • sales, property or use tax
  • license, registration and title fees
  • finance charges
  • prepayment penalties
  • charges for undercoating, rust-proofing, and factory or dealer installed options
  • aftermarket items bought within 20 days after delivery

For an aftermarket item, the manufacturer can choose to remove it and give it back to you, provided it comes off without damage, instead of refunding its cost.

The use allowance

ORS 646A.404(3) sets the formula. Multiply the vehicle's mileage by the cash or lease price plus any collateral charges you paid, then divide by a fixed number that depends on the vehicle:

VehicleDivisor
Passenger vehicle (other than a motorcycle or motor home)120,000
Motorcycle25,000
Motor home90,000

The mileage is the odometer reading when the manufacturer replaces or repurchases the vehicle, less 10 miles for travel during any period when you didn't have the vehicle because it was being repaired.

Affirmative defenses

Under ORS 646A.404(4), the manufacturer can defend a claim by showing that the alleged nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety, or that it results from abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Informal dispute settlement

If a manufacturer sets up or takes part in an informal dispute settlement procedure that substantially complies with 16 C.F.R. part 703 and makes sure you're told about it, ORS 646A.408 requires you to use that procedure before the refund or replacement remedy applies. An arbitration decision under the procedure binds the manufacturer. It doesn't bind you.

Manufacturers must keep records of every case submitted and give them to the Department of Justice on request. ORS 646A.410 lets the Department review those records to check whether arbitrators are following the law.

Going to court

Under ORS 646A.412, if a court grants you replacement or refund and finds that the manufacturer didn't act in good faith, you're also awarded up to three times your damages, capped at $50,000 above the amount owed under ORS 646A.404. A court may award reasonable attorney fees, expert witness fees and costs to a consumer who prevails. If a consumer sues in bad faith or to harass, the court may award the manufacturer its attorney fees. In a case involving a motor home, fees and costs may go to whichever side prevails.

ORS 646A.416 sets the deadline. You must file within one year after the earliest of these: the date the vehicle reaches 24,000 miles, the end of the two years after original delivery, or the end of any extension under ORS 646A.406(3).

The law gives you no claim against the dealer, except under the resale rules in ORS 646A.405. A manufacturer can't add the dealer as a party or try to collect damages from the dealer. Other legal remedies stay available under ORS 646A.418. If you pursue another remedy in court, though, you can't recover more than this law allows unless you prove fault causing greater damages.

Buyback titles and resale

A manufacturer that replaces or repurchases a vehicle must ask the Oregon Department of Transportation to title it in the manufacturer's name and to inscribe "Lemon Law Buyback" on the title and in the Department's records (ORS 646A.405). Anyone who later sells, leases or transfers it, and knows or should know of the buyback or the notation, must give the next buyer a written notice. The notice says the vehicle was repurchased under Oregon's consumer warranty law because of a defect and that its title is permanently inscribed "Lemon Law Buyback." The buyer must sign to acknowledge receiving and understanding it. Failing to follow these rules is an unlawful trade practice under ORS 646.608.
