Electric vehicles in Nebraska

The U.S. Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center doesn't list a Nebraska rebate or tax credit for drivers who buy an EV. Its Nebraska entries include a state loan program that can finance alternative fuel vehicles, an annual EV registration fee, and new charging rules passed in 2024, including a tax on charging electricity that begins in 2028.

The alternative fuel registration fee

Under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 60-3,191, each vehicle powered by an alternative fuel pays a fee on top of the regular registration charges. The county treasurer collects it every time the vehicle is registered or renewed, and it goes to the Highway Trust Fund. The amounts are:

  • $150 for an electric or hydrogen fuel cell passenger vehicle or truck.
  • $75 for a plug-in hybrid or a motorcycle.
  • Three times the $150 fee ($450) for a commercially registered alternative fuel vehicle over 7,500 pounds that isn't registered under the International Registration Plan.

The AFDC says the fee covers vehicles running on electricity, solar power or any other energy source not otherwise taxed under the state's motor fuel laws. Compressed natural gas, liquefied natural gas and propane aren't included. The registration certificate records whether the vehicle uses an alternative fuel and what type (§ 60-390). Nebraska doesn't require emissions testing, so an EV owner has no inspection fee to weigh against the registration fee.

Dollar and Energy Saving Loans

The Nebraska Energy Office runs the Dollar and Energy Saving Loan Program. The AFDC says it offers low-cost loans for alternative fuel projects, including buying new alternative fuel vehicles, replacing conventional vehicles with them, converting vehicles, and building or buying fueling stations or equipment. It lists a maximum loan of $500,000 per borrower at 5% interest or less. Check the program's website for current terms and participating lenders before you apply.

Charging rules passed in 2024

Nebraska's 2024 legislation set several rules for EV charging:

  • Not a utility: a person or company operating an EV charger isn't a public utility. Commercial operators are still subject to the local utility's interconnection requirements, rates and service rules (§§ 70-1001 and 70-1002).
  • Charging tax: beginning January 1, 2028, electricity sold at retail for EV charging is subject to an excise tax of 3 cents per kilowatt-hour (§ 77-27,241).
  • Certified installers: chargers funded or authorized by a Nebraska state agency or the federal government must be installed by a contractor with Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Training Program certification.
  • Sanctions certification: entities that get state money to buy or install commercial chargers must certify that no component was made or assembled by a person or entity on the U.S. Treasury's sanctions lists (§ 81-121).

Where utilities can build fast chargers

Section 70-1002 limits when an electric utility can develop, own or operate a DC fast charger. For now, the site has to be at least 15 miles from an existing or under-construction private fast charger and within one mile of a federally designated Alternative Fuel Corridor. The utility also has to run a right-of-first-refusal process before construction and offer fair, nondiscriminatory charging rates to all fast charger operators in its territory. Beginning January 1, 2028, the distance drops to 10 miles from a private fast charger that's operating or has a pending building permit and interconnection request. The AFDC's summary of the 2028 rules doesn't repeat the corridor and right-of-first-refusal conditions.

NEVI planning

Under the federal NEVI Formula Program, the Nebraska Department of Transportation had to submit an updated EV infrastructure deployment plan for each of fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The plans are posted on NDOT's NEVI website.

Heavy-vehicle weight allowance

Section 60-6,294 lets vehicles powered mainly by natural gas or battery power exceed the state's gross weight limit by up to 2,000 pounds, to make up for the extra weight of their fuel systems.

Federal credits

Before you count a federal clean vehicle credit in your budget, check its current status with the IRS and on fueleconomy.gov.
