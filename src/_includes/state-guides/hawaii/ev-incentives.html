No state rebate for buying an EV

Hawaii has set clean transportation goals, but it doesn't currently give drivers a rebate or tax credit for buying or leasing an electric car. The state programs listed for Hawaii by the Alternative Fuels Data Center focus on charging infrastructure, parking rules and how EVs pay for roads.

The road usage charge replaces the old EV surcharge

Since July 1, 2025, electric vehicles in Hawaii pay a state road usage charge at registration renewal through the Hawaiʻi Road Usage Charge (HiRUC) Program, under HRS § 249-36. On the first renewal notice after that date, an eligible EV owner picks one of two options:

  • Per-mile: 0.8 cents per mile, or $8 per 1,000 miles, capped at $50 a year
  • Flat: $50 a year, offered through June 30, 2028

Mileage is calculated from the odometer readings recorded at your annual safety check. A new EV that hasn't needed an inspection yet pays $50 at its first renewal, and that amount is credited against the per-mile charge at the second renewal.

HDOT's rules (HAR chapter 19-154) attach some conditions to the choice. If you don't pick an option, you're placed on the per-mile charge. Once you choose per-mile, you can't switch back to the flat fee, and neither can a later owner of that car. Fleets of 25 or more vehicles pay per mile automatically. If the odometer data is missing or wrong, a default charge of $50 applies through June 2028, rising to $80 after that. Beginning July 1, 2028, every EV moves to the per-mile charge.

Only fully electric vehicles take part in HiRUC. Conventional hybrids and plug-in hybrids aren't eligible. If you drive under about 6,250 miles a year, the per-mile option costs less than $50.

Rebates for charging stations

Hawaii's EV charging station rebate program is authorized under HRS §§ 269-72 and 269-73 and funded by three cents from the tax on each barrel of petroleum product sold in the state. Hawaii Energy administers it for the Public Utilities Commission. Rebates cover up to the full project cost, with these maximums:

  • Level 2, one port: $2,000 new, or $1,300 for a replacement or expansion
  • Level 2, two ports: $4,500 new, or $3,000 for a replacement or expansion
  • DC fast charging: $35,000 new, or $28,000 for a replacement or expansion

Affordable housing serving families at or below 100% of area median income can get an extra $5,000 per Level 2 station at existing developments, or $1,500 at new ones. Awards are first-come, first-served while state funds last. Chargers that serve only one person, like a reserved stall or a single-family home, don't qualify, so this program isn't a way to pay for a home charger.

EV parking rules

Under HRS §§ 291-71 and 291-72, public parking facilities with at least 100 spaces must set aside at least one EV space with a Level 2 or DC fast charger. Parking a non-EV in an EV space, or parking any vehicle in a charging space when it isn't actively charging, can bring a $50 to $100 fine plus court costs and a $50 state enforcement fine. Counties can also pass ordinances requiring that public chargers be kept in working order.

If you live in a condo or townhouse, HRS § 196-7.5 lets you install a charger at or near your parking stall. The charger has to follow the association's rules and be registered with it within 30 days. You need the association's consent for a charger in a common area. Associations can restrict where and how chargers are placed but can't ban them or charge a placement fee. The owner is responsible for any damage from installing, maintaining or removing the charger.

Neighborhood electric vehicles

Low-speed neighborhood electric vehicles, which are four-wheeled, electric and under 3,000 pounds GVWR, can travel up to 25 mph, but only on roads with speed limits of 35 mph or less. Each one must carry a permanent notice of those restrictions where the driver can see it.
