South Carolina's lemon law

South Carolina's lemon law is chapter 28 of Title 56 of the Code of Laws, titled "Enforcement of Motor Vehicle Express Warranties." It requires a manufacturer to repair, replace or buy back a new vehicle that it can't bring into line with its express warranty after a reasonable number of attempts. The chapter dates from 1989, and a 2016 amendment brought motorcycles under it.

Who and what is covered

Under § 56-28-10, a "consumer" is a buyer or lessee, other than for resale, of a vehicle normally used for personal, family or household purposes and covered by the manufacturer's express warranty. Anyone else entitled under the warranty to enforce it also counts.

The law covers two kinds of vehicles sold and registered in South Carolina:

  • Private passenger vehicles, as classified by § 56-3-630. The living portion of a recreational vehicle and off-road vehicles are excluded.
  • Motorcycles, including three-wheel motorcycles.

A "manufacturer" is any person, resident or nonresident, who makes, assembles, imports or distributes new vehicles to be sold in the state. A "new motor vehicle" is one sold to a new-vehicle dealer by a manufacturer, used for nothing beyond demonstration, and with no original title yet issued from the dealer. A "nonconformity" is a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety. Defects caused by an accident, or by modification or alteration by someone other than the manufacturer or its authorized service agent, don't count.

The protection period

Section 56-28-30 applies when a new vehicle doesn't conform to its express warranties within the first 12 months after purchase or the first 12,000 miles, whichever comes first, and you report the problem to the manufacturer or its agent during the warranty term. The manufacturer must then make the needed repairs at no cost to you, even if the repairs happen after the term ends.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer, through its agents or authorized dealer, can't fix a defect that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety within that period after a reasonable number of attempts, § 56-28-40 says it must either replace the vehicle with a comparable one or, at its option, take it back and refund the full purchase price as delivered. The refund includes:

  • Finance charges
  • Sales taxes
  • License and registration fees
  • Other similar government charges

The manufacturer can subtract a reasonable allowance for your use of the vehicle. That allowance covers miles driven before you first reported the problem: multiply the full purchase price by the miles driven before that first report, then divide by 120,000. Refunds go to you and to any lienholder, according to their interests on the DMV's record of ownership.

You aren't entitled to a refund or replacement if the problem doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety, or if it resulted from your abuse, neglect, modification or alteration of the vehicle.

The presumption of a reasonable number of attempts

Section 56-28-50(A) presumes that a reasonable number of attempts have been made if either of these happened within the express warranty term:

  • The same nonconformity was repaired three or more times by the manufacturer or its agent and still exists.
  • The vehicle was out of service for repair a cumulative total of 30 or more calendar days.

Those periods are extended by any time repair service wasn't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

Written notice and the final repair attempt

Manufacturers must give information about consumer complaint remedies with each new vehicle. If the manufacturer clearly and prominently told you at the time of sale that written notice is required, you must notify the manufacturer in writing of the need for repair before using the law, giving it a final chance to fix the defect.

Within 10 business days of that notice, the manufacturer must tell you about a reasonably accessible repair facility at a franchised new-vehicle dealer. After you deliver the vehicle there, the manufacturer must attempt the repair immediately, within no more than 10 business days. If it can't fix the vehicle in that period, it must replace it with an identical or reasonably equivalent vehicle, or refund the purchase price under § 56-28-40.

All written notices under § 56-28-50 must go by registered, certified or express mail.

Informal dispute settlement and arbitration

When you tell the manufacturer the vehicle still hasn't been fixed, it must tell you whether it has set up an informal dispute settlement procedure, unless it already gave you that notice. Under § 56-28-60, if the manufacturer has a procedure that substantially complies with the Federal Trade Commission's rules at 16 C.F.R. part 703, or takes part in a consumer-industry appeals, arbitration or mediation panel whose decisions bind the manufacturer, you must use that procedure, or the state alternative described in § 56-28-90, before the refund and replacement remedy applies.

Section 56-28-90 lets the Administrator of the Department of Consumer Affairs set up, by regulation, a five-member state arbitration board. It would review cases involving manufacturers that haven't created a procedure complying with part 703, and the manufacturer would bear its cost.

Going to court

A consumer who finally prevails may be allowed by the court to recover costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time spent, that were reasonably incurred in bringing the case, unless the court decides a fee award would be inappropriate. Any action must start within three years after the vehicle was first delivered to you (§ 56-28-70).

The chapter doesn't make dealers liable or let consumers sue dealers under § 56-28-40. Manufacturers can't charge dealers back for refunds or replacements, unless there's evidence the dealer did the related repairs in a way substantially inconsistent with the manufacturer's published instructions.

Resale of bought-back vehicles

A vehicle repurchased under the lemon law or another warranty law can't be resold in South Carolina, wholesale or retail, unless the manufacturer:

  • Notifies the Administrator of the Department of Consumer Affairs in writing, within 30 calendar days, of the vehicle identification number, the reason for the repurchase and a statement that needed repairs are complete and the vehicle meets acceptable operating standards.
  • Gives the next retail buyer a written warranty of 12 months or 12,000 miles that expressly covers any component related to the repurchase.
  • Discloses the repurchase to any dealer or wholesale buyer.

Each later buyer must be told by the seller that the vehicle was repurchased. A seller who fails to do so faces an administrative penalty of up to $500 per vehicle and may request a contested case hearing at the Administrative Law Court. Manufacturers must also give the Administrator a yearly written summary of vehicles repurchased or replaced, and failing to provide it, or to answer reasonable requests for information, can bring a penalty of up to $1,000 per violation.
