New Jersey's insurance requirement

N.J.S.A. 39:6A-3 requires every owner or registered owner of an automobile registered or principally garaged in New Jersey to carry liability insurance on a form approved by the Commissioner of Banking and Insurance. New Jersey is a no-fault state, so every policy also includes personal injury protection (PIP), which pays medical bills for you and your household after a crash regardless of who caused it. The law gives owners two ways to comply: the Standard Policy, which the DOBI Buyer's Guide says most New Jersey drivers choose, and the lower-cost Basic Policy in N.J.S.A. 39:6A-3.1.

Standard Policy minimums: 35/70/25

P.L.2022, c.87 raised the liability floor in two steps. For a Standard Policy issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2026, the minimums are:

  • $35,000 for injury to or death of one person in any one accident
  • $70,000 for injury to or death of more than one person in any one accident
  • $25,000 for damage to property in any one accident

From January 1, 2023 through the end of 2025 the floor was 25/50/25. Before 2023, the bodily injury minimums were $15,000 and $30,000. The Department of Banking and Insurance's Bulletin 25-06 tells insurers that when a renewal would fall below the new limits, they raise the limits and notify the named insured, and they don't need a new signed coverage selection form to do it. The bulletin also notes the increase doesn't apply to the Basic Policy.

A Standard Policy includes PIP of at least $15,000 per person per accident. DOBI's Standard Policy page says PIP can be bought up to $250,000 or more, and up to $250,000 is available for certain serious injuries whatever limit you pick. Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage is available up to the liability limits you select.

The Basic Policy

N.J.S.A. 39:6A-3.1 lets an owner choose a Basic Policy in place of the Standard Policy. It carries:

  • PIP medical expense benefits of up to $15,000 per person per accident, rising to $250,000 for permanent or significant brain injury, spinal cord injury or disfigurement, and for certain trauma-center care right after the crash
  • $5,000 of property damage liability per accident
  • An optional $10,000 of bodily injury liability for all people injured in one accident

Bodily injury liability isn't included unless you add that $10,000 option, and DOBI's comparison chart shows the Basic Policy has no uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage. Collision and comprehensive may be offered by some insurers. Everyone covered by a Basic Policy is automatically under the limited right to sue. The 2026 Buyer's Guide says the Basic Policy usually costs significantly less but gives limited benefits, and it may suit people with few family responsibilities and few assets to protect, including income from a job.

For drivers enrolled in federal Medicaid with hospitalization, the Buyer's Guide also describes a Special Automobile Insurance Policy. It's a medical-only policy priced at $365 a year. Your agent or DOBI, at 1-800-446-7467, can tell you whether you qualify.

Choosing your right to sue

A Standard Policy buyer must pick a lawsuit option, and the choice also binds a spouse, children and other resident relatives who don't have their own auto policy. Under the Unlimited Right to Sue, you keep the right to sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering for any injury. Under the Limited Right to Sue, you give up that right unless you suffer one of the injuries the Buyer's Guide lists: loss of a body part, significant disfigurement or scarring, a displaced fracture, loss of a fetus, a permanent injury, or death. Either way, you can still sue for economic losses such as medical bills and lost wages. If you make no choice on the coverage selection form, the limited option applies by law.

PIP deductibles and health care primary

The Buyer's Guide explains that PIP medical claims carry a deductible, $250 at the minimum, plus a 20 percent copayment on expenses between the deductible and $5,000. Its example: with $10,000 of medical bills, a driver with the $250 deductible pays $1,200 in total, and a driver who chose a $2,500 deductible for a 25 percent cut in the PIP premium pays $3,000. You can also make your own health insurance the primary payer for crash injuries. Medicare and Medicaid can't be used that way, so check your health plan's terms first. A Standard Policy can add an extra PIP package with income continuation, essential services, a death benefit and a funeral expense benefit.

Penalties for driving uninsured

N.J.S.A. 39:6B-2 sets the penalties for an owner who drives, or lets someone drive, an uninsured vehicle, and for a driver who knows or should know the car is uninsured:

  • First offense: a fine of $300 to $1,000 and community service set by the court. The court may also suspend your driving privilege for up to one year, and may shorten or drop that suspension if you show proof of insurance at the hearing.
  • Later offense: a fine of up to $5,000, a 14-day jail term, 30 days of community service, and a possible suspension of up to two years.

When the suspension ends, you apply to the MVC's Chief Administrator for a license, and the decision depends on how likely you are to drive uninsured again. If you can't produce an insurance card or policy at trial that was in force on the date charged, the court presumes you were uninsured, though you can rebut that. A complaint can be filed in municipal court within six months of the offense.

What New Jersey drivers pay

The NAIC's 2024 Auto Insurance Database supplement puts New Jersey's average combined premium for liability, collision and comprehensive at $1,878.38. The Insurance Research Council estimates 14.1% of New Jersey drivers are uninsured, which ranks 20th among the states. Your own premium will depend on your record, your town and your car, so compare quotes from several licensed insurers.
