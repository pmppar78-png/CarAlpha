Michigan's new vehicle warranty act

Michigan's lemon law is 1986 PA 87, MCL 257.1401 to 257.1410. It took effect June 25, 1986, and applies to new vehicles sold to the original consumer on or after that date. The rights it gives you can't be waived, and it doesn't limit any other remedy you have for breach of an express or implied warranty. Michigan doesn't run a state arbitration board for these cases. A claim that isn't settled goes through the manufacturer's dispute program, if it has a qualifying one, and then to court.

Which vehicles and buyers qualify

Section 1 covers motor vehicles designed as passenger cars or sport utility vehicles, and pickup trucks and vans. It leaves out motor homes, buses, other trucks and anything built to travel on fewer than four wheels, so motorcycles aren't included. A "new motor vehicle" is one bought or leased in Michigan, or by a Michigan resident, that has the manufacturer's express warranty at the time of sale or lease.

You're a "consumer" if any of these describes you:

  • You bought or leased the vehicle for personal, family or household use, and not to resell or re-lease it.
  • You buy or lease fewer than 10 new vehicles a year. That brings in small businesses.
  • You buy or lease 10 or more a year, but only for personal, family or household use.
  • You're otherwise entitled to enforce the express warranty under its terms.

Section 6 excludes problems caused by a modification that wasn't made by or for the manufacturer. It also excludes abuse, neglect, and accident damage that happened after you bought or leased the car.

When you must report the problem

Section 2 covers any defect or condition that impairs the vehicle's use or value to you, or keeps it from conforming to the express warranty. The manufacturer or a dealer for that make must repair it, if you first reported it within whichever of these ends sooner:

  • the term of the manufacturer's express warranty, or
  • one year from delivery to the original consumer.

There's no mileage limit in the statute itself, but a warranty that ends by mileage can end the window early. Repairs for a defect you reported in time must be made even after the warranty expires, as long as they continue the original repair attempt.

Four repairs or 30 days, then written notice

Under section 3(5), a reasonable number of attempts is presumed in either of two situations.

Four repairs. The same defect, one that substantially impairs use or value, has been repaired four or more times within two years of the first repair attempt and still exists. Any time after the third attempt, and before you claim a remedy, you must notify the manufacturer in writing by return receipt service. That gives it a chance to cure the problem.

30 days. The vehicle was out of service for repairs for a total of 30 or more days or parts of days. Those days must fall within the express warranty or the first year after delivery, whichever ends first. Once the vehicle has spent at least 25 days in a repair facility, and before you claim a remedy, you must send the same return-receipt notice.

Either way, the manufacturer must tell you as soon as reasonably possible about a reasonably accessible repair facility. After you deliver the vehicle there, it has 5 business days to fix the defect. The warranty term and the one-year, 30-day and 5-day periods are extended when repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

The notice requirement also appears in a statement that section 8 requires the Secretary of State to include with documents for each new vehicle. It says you must first report the defect in writing to the manufacturer, and you may have to arbitrate first. It also tells you to keep copies of all letters to and from the manufacturer and dealer, and every repair order showing dates and mileage. Finally, it says to follow the warranty's requirements, including using an authorized dealer for repairs.

Refund or replacement within 30 days

If the defect continues after a reasonable number of repairs, the manufacturer has 30 days to act. For a purchased vehicle, it must either replace it with a comparable vehicle currently in production that you accept, or take it back and refund the purchase price. You have the right to demand the refund. For a leased vehicle, you're entitled to a refund of the lease price you paid. You may agree to take a comparable replacement instead, and then the lease can change only to identify the new vehicle. A lessor can't charge an early-termination fee for a lemon-law return.

The purchase price is the vehicle sales price on the buyer's order, including any cash you paid and your trade-in allowance. It also includes sales tax, license and registration fees, and similar government charges you paid. It doesn't include debt from another transaction, such as negative equity rolled in from an old loan, or any manufacturer discount, rebate or incentive applied to the deal. The lease price is defined the same way, using what the lessor paid. Both include the cost of options or modifications installed by or for the manufacturer, and other charges made by or for the manufacturer.

The manufacturer also reimburses towing and reasonable rental costs caused by the defect, if those weren't provided for free.

The mileage deduction

Two amounts can come off the refund. One is any appraised damage not caused by normal use or by the defect. The other is a reasonable allowance for use, figured as the purchase or lease price multiplied by a fraction over 100,000. The top of the fraction counts:

  • the miles you and any earlier consumer drove before you first reported a defect that impairs use or value, plus
  • all the miles you drove beyond 25,000.

Miles driven after the first report and before 25,000 aren't deducted. A court or a qualifying dispute program can also cut the deduction for miles over 25,000. That applies to any stretch during which the vehicle didn't provide reliable transportation for ordinary personal or household use. In deciding, it looks at the number and cost of repairs, the days out of service, and how much the repairs affected your use of the vehicle.

Loans and liens

A lemon-law claim doesn't change your obligations under the loan, sales or lease contract. If you accept a comparable replacement, the lender must agree to move its security interest to the new vehicle. If that can't be done, you must take a refund, which goes to you and the lender according to your interests at that time. The lessor must be told when a lessee gets a refund.

Manufacturer dispute programs and court

Section 5 says that if a manufacturer has, or participates in, an informal dispute settlement procedure, you must use it before you can rely on the act. That applies only if the procedure does all of the following:

  • complies with the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act and the FTC's rule at 16 C.F.R. 703
  • binds the manufacturer to a decision you agree to
  • lets you reject the decision and pursue your remedies under the act
  • requires the manufacturer to start carrying out a final settlement within 30 days

Your warranty booklet will say whether such a program exists. If you go to court and win, section 7 lets the judge award your costs and expenses, including attorney fees based on the actual time spent, unless the court decides a fee award would be inappropriate.
