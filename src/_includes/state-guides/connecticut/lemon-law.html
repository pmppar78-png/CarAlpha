How Connecticut's lemon law works

Connecticut's lemon law is Chapter 743b of the General Statutes, "New Automobile Warranties." The core rules are in C.G.S. § 42-179. What makes Connecticut different is that the state runs the arbitration itself. The Department of Consumer Protection (DCP) operates a Lemon Law Arbitration Program. It costs a consumer $50, it's held online, and DCP says it usually reaches a decision within about 60 days of receiving your request. Most people who use it don't hire a lawyer.

Who and what is covered

The statute protects a "consumer," which includes:

  • anyone who buys the vehicle for a purpose other than resale
  • a lessee
  • anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the express warranty is still in effect
  • anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty.

A "motor vehicle" means a passenger car, a combination passenger-and-commercial vehicle, or a motorcycle, as those terms are defined in § 14-1, that was sold or leased in Connecticut. Motorcycles have been covered since 1997. DCP's program handles only vehicles bought or leased in Connecticut. Its guide lists contacts in Rhode Island, Massachusetts, and New York for cars bought there.

The two-year / 24,000-mile window

If you report a defect within two years of original delivery or the first 24,000 miles, whichever ends first, the manufacturer or its dealer has to repair it to meet the express warranty. That's true even if the repair happens after the period ends (§ 42-179(b)). The mileage limit was raised from 18,000 to 24,000 in 1998.

What counts as a reasonable number of attempts

Under § 42-179(e) and (f), the law presumes the manufacturer has had enough chances when one of these is true during the eligibility period:

  • the same defect has been repaired four or more times and is still there
  • the vehicle has been out of service for repairs for 30 or more calendar days in total, and DCP's guide notes the days can come from any number of unrelated problems
  • a defect likely to cause death or serious injury has been repaired at least twice and is still there. For this rule, the window is shorter: the express warranty term or one year from delivery, whichever ends first.

Time when repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, a fire, a flood, or another natural disaster is added to those periods. You can't bring a claim at all until the manufacturer or dealer has made at least one repair attempt or refused to try (§ 42-179(e)(3)). DCP also notes that you may still qualify with fewer attempts if you can explain why fewer was reasonable. It also counts cases where the dealer said the problem was "normal" or couldn't reproduce it.

The defect has to substantially impair the vehicle's use, safety, or value to you. The manufacturer can defend itself by showing it doesn't, or that the problem came from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes.

Notifying the manufacturer

The statute requires written notice to the manufacturer only if the warranty or owner's manual clearly says it's required. When it does, the manufacturer has to give you the name and address to send it to (§ 42-179(c)). DCP's arbitration program has its own rule, though: you must notify the manufacturer, by mail or email, that you intend to file, and upload a copy of that letter with your application. If you lease, you also have to notify the leasing company by certified or registered mail. The lessor then has 10 days to tell DCP whether it wants to join the case.

What the manufacturer owes

If the defect can't be fixed, the manufacturer has to either give you a new vehicle acceptable to you or take the car back and refund (§ 42-179(d)):

  • the full contract price, including undercoating, dealer prep, transportation, and installed options
  • collateral charges such as sales tax, license and registration fees, and similar government charges
  • finance charges you paid after first reporting the defect and while the car was in the shop
  • incidental damages, such as reasonable costs for transporting or storing the car, efforts to avoid defaulting on the loan, and pursuing other remedies.

The refund is reduced by an allowance for your use: the total contract price multiplied by the miles driven before the manufacturer accepted the car back, divided by 120,000. For a $40,000 car returned with 9,000 miles on it, that's $40,000 × 9,000 ÷ 120,000, or $3,000. That denominator is higher than most states use, so it works in your favor. DCP points out that arbitrators have discretion, though. An arbitrator may or may not apply a mileage deduction, and may award reimbursement for incidental or consequential costs, so keep your receipts.

A manufacturer can't make the dealer pay for a refund or replacement unless the dealer's repairs didn't follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Applying to the state program

Applications are online only, through eLicense, filed as a Consumer Protection general complaint against the manufacturer's "Lemon Law – Manufacturer" license. Have these ready:

  • the year, make, model, and VIN
  • the purchase or lease agreement and the registration
  • proof that you notified the manufacturer, and the leasing company if you lease
  • every repair order, recall notice, and photo or video of the problem.

DCP staff review eligibility, which can take up to two weeks. If your case is accepted, they'll email you instructions to pay the $50 fee, and the manufacturer pays a $250 fee. If you win, the manufacturer may have to reimburse your fee.

Hearings are held on Microsoft Teams. You usually present first, and either side can ask questions. A DCP automotive technical expert advises the arbitrator and may be ordered to inspect the car. The burden of proof is on you, so organize your repair records in date order and come prepared to explain how the defect affects use, safety, or value. If an attorney will present your case, tell DCP at least two days before the hearing. If someone else will present for you, tell DCP at least one day before.

If the manufacturer offers to settle before the hearing, the offer has to be in writing, list every dollar amount, and set a fixed compliance date. DCP has to review it before you sign. Settlements can include extras like floor mats, extended warranties, or maintenance plans, and you may owe an MSRP difference on a replacement.

After the decision

An arbitrator's decision can't be changed by the arbitrator or DCP, and all decisions are made public. Either side can appeal to Superior Court within 30 days, but only on limited grounds. DCP follows up within 10 days after the compliance date. If the manufacturer hasn't complied, the case can be referred to the Attorney General. If you get a "no action" decision, your remaining option is a private lawsuit. If you plan to sue instead of arbitrating, talk to a lawyer before you sign the agreement to arbitrate. A manufacturer's own dispute program counts only if the Attorney General has certified it. If so, you must use that program first. Otherwise, dealers have to post a notice about the state program.

If you sell the car while your case is open, you have to tell the buyer about the case before the sale and notify DCP within five days.

Buyback branding

A car returned under the lemon law, or bought back in a settlement, can't be resold or leased in Connecticut without a written disclosure attached to the car and included in the sale contract. The manufacturer has to stamp "MANUFACTURER BUYBACK-LEMON" on the title in letters at least a quarter inch tall. It must send a copy to the DMV within 30 days and send electronic proof to DCP. DCP can fine a manufacturer up to $10,000 for missing that deadline. These rules also apply to cars bought back in another state and later sold in Connecticut by someone who knows their history.
