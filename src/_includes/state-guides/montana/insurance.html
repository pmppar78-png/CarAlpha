What Montana requires

Under Mont. Code Ann. § 61-6-301, the owner of a motor vehicle registered and operated in Montana must continuously carry liability insurance. The owner can also hold a certificate of self-insurance issued under § 61-6-143. Driving on a road open to the public without a valid policy is unlawful unless you are self-insured or the vehicle is exempt. The law lets a policy exclude a named family member from coverage.

Minimum limits: 25/50/20

Section 61-6-103 sets the minimum limits for each vehicle:

  • $25,000 for bodily injury to or death of one person in an accident.
  • $50,000 for bodily injury to or death of two or more people in an accident.
  • $20,000 for damage to other people's property in an accident.

The policy has to cover you and anyone driving the listed vehicles with your express or implied permission, anywhere in the United States or Canada. An insurer may not attach a reduced-limits endorsement to a policy written to meet these minimums. You can buy more coverage on the same policy, and the extra coverage isn't subject to these rules.

The same section bars an insurer from canceling, not renewing, or raising your premium because of an injury or damage claim unless you violated a traffic law, a court or arbitrator found you negligent or contributorily negligent, or damages were paid to another party. A premium also can't go up during the policy term unless your exposure changes.

Uninsured motorist coverage

Under § 33-23-201, an insurer can't issue a liability policy for a vehicle registered and principally garaged in Montana unless it includes uninsured motorist coverage at the 25/50 bodily injury limits. That coverage pays for your injuries when an at-fault driver has no bodily injury liability insurance or bond. The named insured can reject it. If you rejected it on an earlier policy with the same insurer, the insurer doesn't have to include it in renewals unless you ask in writing.

Carrying proof

Section 61-6-302 requires every owner or operator to carry proof of insurance in the vehicle. That proof can be an insurance card approved by the state and issued by your insurer, or an electronic document from the insurer shown on a phone or other device. Cards for commercial policies or self-insured fleets must say "commercially insured" or "fleet." You have to show the proof when a judge, peace officer, highway patrol officer, or a field deputy or inspector of the Department of Justice asks. If the person driving isn't the owner, the officer can charge the owner instead, served in person or by certified mail.

A charge for not carrying proof can be beaten. You won't be convicted if a check of the state's online insurance verification system shows the vehicle was insured at the time. If that system isn't available, you can bring valid proof of coverage to the court or to the officer's office.

Penalties

Section 61-6-304 sets escalating penalties for driving uninsured or failing to show proof:

  • First conviction: a fine of $250 to $500.
  • Second conviction: a $350 fine. The court takes the registration receipt and plates, and the vehicle's registration is suspended until you prove coverage.
  • Third or later conviction: a $500 fine, up to 10 days in county jail, or both, plus the same loss of registration and plates.
  • Fourth or later conviction: the court also takes your driver's license if the vehicle was registered to you or a member of your immediate family. The license stays suspended until you prove coverage.

Earlier convictions count only if less than 5 years passed between them and the new offense. After a second or later conviction, you can get a restricted registration receipt that lets you use the vehicle only for work. You'll need proof of insurance and the fees for replacement plates, decal and receipt. The restriction lasts 90 days after a second conviction or 180 days after a third. If the vehicle is sold, the new owner can register it.

Exempt vehicles

Section 61-6-303 exempts these vehicles from the insurance requirement:

  • Vehicles owned by the federal government, the state or a local government.
  • Farm implements and special mobile equipment that use the road only incidentally.
  • Vehicles that only cross a highway between properties.
  • Commercial vehicles registered in more than one state and insured under another state's law.
  • Motorcycles and quadricycles.
  • Vehicles moved only by human or animal power.

Motorcycle riders should still look into coverage, because the exemption doesn't protect them from liability after a crash.

What drivers pay

In the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' 2024 Auto Insurance Database supplement, Montana's average combined premium for liability, collision and comprehensive was $1,321.59, up from $1,031.50 in 2020. The NAIC's average expenditure figure for the state was $1,080.94. The Insurance Research Council estimated that 7.2% of Montana drivers were uninsured. Rates depend on the driver, the vehicle and the location, so get quotes from several licensed insurers to see what you would pay.
