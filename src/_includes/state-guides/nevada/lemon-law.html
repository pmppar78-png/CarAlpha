Nevada's lemon law

Nevada's new vehicle warranty law is NRS 597.600 to 597.688, in the Nevada Revised Statutes. When a new vehicle has a defect the manufacturer can't fix after a reasonable number of attempts, the law requires a replacement or refund. Since 2005, it has also set out how a bought-back vehicle has to be labeled and disclosed before anyone resells it. The law sets short deadlines, so it helps to know them before the first repair visit.

Who and what is covered

Under NRS 597.600, a "buyer" is someone who purchases or contracts to purchase a motor vehicle, other than for resale, that is normally used for personal, family or household purposes. The definition also covers anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the manufacturer's express warranty is still in effect, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty under its terms.

"Motor vehicle" takes its meaning from NRS 482.075, the state's vehicle registration chapter. The lemon law excludes motor homes and off-road vehicles. The one exception is NRS 597.680, which requires the manufacturer to reimburse its dealers for warranty repairs at the rate they usually charge the public for similar work.

Report the defect in writing, early

NRS 597.610 is where a Nevada claim starts. If a new vehicle doesn't conform to the manufacturer's express warranties, you have to report the problem in writing to the manufacturer. The report must be made before the express warranty expires or within one year after the vehicle was delivered to the original buyer, whichever comes first. Once you've reported it in time, the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer has to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity, even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period ends.

A trip to the service department isn't the same as a written report to the manufacturer. Send a letter to the address in your warranty booklet or owner's manual, keep a copy, and use a delivery method with proof of receipt. NRS 597.675 makes you responsible for telling the manufacturer if your home address changes, so its replies reach you.

The presumption: four repairs or 30 days

NRS 597.630(2) presumes a reasonable number of repair attempts in either of these cases, within the warranty period or the first year after delivery to the original buyer, whichever ends first:

  • The same nonconformity has been repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agent or authorized dealer, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a cumulative total of 30 or more calendar days.

The 30 days are counted as calendar days, so weekends count. If the needed repairs can't be made for reasons beyond the control of the manufacturer or dealer, the number of days required is "appropriately extended." Under NRS 597.640, every deadline in the law is tolled while repair services aren't reasonably available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The defect must substantially impair the vehicle's use and value to you. It also can't be the result of abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Replacement or refund

When those conditions are met, NRS 597.630(1) requires the manufacturer to do one of two things:

  • Replace the vehicle with a comparable one of the same model with the same features. If that can't be delivered within a reasonable time, a substantially similar comparable vehicle can be substituted.
  • Refund the full purchase price, including all sales taxes, license fees, registration fees and similar government charges, minus a reasonable allowance for your use. The refund goes to you and any lienholder according to your interests.

The use allowance is the amount directly attributable to your use before you first reported the problem to the manufacturer, agent or dealer, plus any later period when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repairs. The statute doesn't set a mileage formula.

Manufacturer dispute programs

NRS 597.600 to 597.688 don't create a state-run arbitration program. Under NRS 597.620, if the manufacturer has established or designated an informal dispute procedure that substantially complies with the Federal Trade Commission rules in 16 C.F.R. part 703, you must submit your replacement or refund claim to it before filing suit under NRS 597.630. The warranty booklet should say whether such a program exists and how to start it.

The 18-month lawsuit deadline

NRS 597.650 requires any lawsuit under the lemon law to be filed within 18 months after the vehicle was first delivered to the buyer. Since the reporting window can run up to a year, and a manufacturer program may take time, the deadline can arrive quickly. Track it from the delivery date on your purchase paperwork, and keep every repair order showing the dates the vehicle was in and out of the shop.

Under NRS 597.660, any agreement that has you waive your lemon law rights is void. NRS 597.670 keeps any other remedies you have under other laws or your contract, such as warranty claims.

Lemon Law Buyback titles

NRS 597.682 to 597.688 cover what happens to a vehicle after it's bought back. Before a manufacturer, agent or dealer resells, leases or transfers a vehicle it reacquired under NRS 597.630, or exports it to another state for sale, it must:

  • Retitle the vehicle in the manufacturer's name.
  • Ask the Nevada DMV to inscribe the certificate of ownership with "Lemon Law Buyback."
  • Put a decal on the left front doorframe saying the title carries that notation. Knowingly removing or altering the decal is prohibited.

The next buyer must get a "Warranty Buyback Notice" on a form prepared by the manufacturer. It lists the year, make, model and VIN, each problem the original owner reported, and the repairs attempted, and the buyer signs to acknowledge it. Any later seller of a vehicle with a Lemon Law Buyback title must also get the buyer's signature on a written disclosure stating that the manufacturer repurchased the vehicle because of a defect. The form tells the buyer that Nevada law requires the manufacturer to warrant for one year that the vehicle is free of the listed problems.

Under NRS 597.686, a manufacturer, dealer or lienholder can't make a Nevada buyer agree to keep quiet about the vehicle's problems or the nonfinancial terms of a buyback. Any gag clause covering those subjects is void, although the financial terms can be kept confidential. Under NRS 597.688, a person harmed by a violation of the buyback rules can sue for actual damages, costs, reasonable attorney's fees and any punitive damages the facts warrant.
