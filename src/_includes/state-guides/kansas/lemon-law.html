Kansas's lemon law in one statute

All of Kansas's lemon law is in a single section, K.S.A. 50-645, which the Legislature passed in 1985 and last amended in 1996. It sets out who is covered, how long protection lasts, when a manufacturer is presumed to have had enough chances, and what the buyer gets. The Attorney General enforces it. A companion section, K.S.A. 50-646, says the act doesn't limit any other rights you have, including under the Uniform Commercial Code and the Uniform Consumer Credit Code.

The Attorney General's consumer page sums it up. If a certain number of repairs fail during a set period, or the vehicle is out of service for a certain number of days during a set period, the vehicle may be presumed a lemon. The manufacturer must then replace or repurchase it, less a reasonable value for use. The office stresses that the law "contains very specific definitions and time periods." It recommends that anyone who suspects a lemon either file a complaint with the Attorney General or contact a private attorney as early as possible.

Who and what is covered

A "motor vehicle" under the act is a new vehicle sold or leased in Kansas and registered for a gross weight of 12,000 pounds or less. Customized parts added or modified by second-stage manufacturers or converters, as defined in K.S.A. 8-2401, are excluded. In practice, the van or truck chassis may be covered while the conversion work isn't. Used cars aren't covered at all.

A "consumer" is the original buyer or lessee, other than someone buying or leasing for resale. Although the definition mentions lessees, the Kansas Court of Appeals held in Ford Motor Credit Co. v. Sims (1987) that liability falls only on the manufacturer and that the law didn't apply to the true lease in that case. That decision came before the 1989 and 1996 amendments, so if you lease, ask an attorney how it applies to your contract.

The reporting window

Under subsection (b), you must report the nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer before the warranty ends or within one year of the original delivery, whichever comes first. Kansas has no mileage cutoff. Once you've reported a problem in time, the manufacturer must make the repairs needed to conform the vehicle to the warranty, even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period expires.

The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30-day out-of-service count are all extended for any time repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

When a vehicle is presumed a lemon

Subsection (d) presumes a reasonable number of repair attempts in any of these situations, but only if the manufacturer has received actual notice of the nonconformity:

  • The same nonconformity, one that substantially impairs the vehicle's use and value to you, has been repaired four or more times within the warranty term or the first year, whichever ends first, and still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a total of 30 or more calendar days during that period
  • There have been 10 or more attempts to repair any nonconformities that substantially impair use and value, all made by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers

Under the third test, the 10 attempts don't have to involve the same problem. A vehicle with a series of different serious defects can qualify even if none of them alone reached four repairs.

Because the presumption depends on actual notice to the manufacturer, not just the dealer, it makes sense to write to the manufacturer directly. Use the address in your warranty booklet or owner's manual, describe the problem and list every repair visit, and keep proof that the letter was delivered. Keep every repair order, too. Each one shows a date, what was reported and what was done, and together they're your evidence for the counts above.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer and its dealers can't conform the vehicle to the warranty after a reasonable number of attempts, subsection (c) gives the manufacturer two options: replace it with a comparable vehicle under warranty, or take it back and refund the full purchase or lease price, including all collateral charges. Refunds go to you and any lienholder as your interests appear.

The manufacturer can deduct a reasonable allowance for use. The statute ties that allowance to the most recent edition of Your Driving Costs from the American Automobile Association. The deduction covers only use directly attributable to you, and to any earlier consumer, before the problem was first reported, plus any later period when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. Miles driven while you were dealing with the defect after reporting it, and days it spent at the dealer, aren't supposed to count against you.

Manufacturer defenses

A manufacturer has two affirmative defenses under the statute:

  • The alleged nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use and value
  • The nonconformity resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations by a consumer

Keeping up with scheduled maintenance and keeping the receipts can help answer a neglect defense.

Informal dispute programs

Kansas doesn't run a state arbitration board. Under subsection (e), if a manufacturer has set up an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies in all respects with the Federal Trade Commission's rules at 16 CFR part 703, you must use that procedure before you can demand a refund or replacement under the statute. Your warranty booklet should say whether such a program exists and how to start a claim. In Hemphill v. Ford Motor Co. (2009), the Court of Appeals mentioned the lemon law in a case where arbitration was required because the claims were tied to a dispute subject to an arbitration agreement. Read any arbitration clause in your purchase documents carefully.

Other Kansas protections that may apply

Because K.S.A. 50-646 keeps other remedies available, a lemon-law claim isn't your only option. Under the Kansas Consumer Protection Act, K.S.A. 50-639 generally bars a supplier in a consumer transaction from disclaiming the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose, or from limiting the remedies for breaching them. A supplier can limit those warranties for a particular defect only by showing that you knew about the defect and that it became part of the basis of the bargain. Even then, the limit doesn't cover personal injury or property damage. An improper disclaimer is void, and a court may award attorney fees and a civil penalty if you win a warranty case against a supplier who used one. Section 50-639 also keeps a warranty claim from failing just because you didn't buy directly from the party you're suing.

The Attorney General's office points out that, as a result, "as is" sales of vehicles to consumers are generally prohibited in Kansas unless that limited exception applies. Your federal rights under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act also continue to apply alongside state law.

Getting help
