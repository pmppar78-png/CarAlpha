No state purchase incentive

Florida doesn't offer a state rebate, tax credit or registration discount for buying an electric vehicle. The Alternative Fuels Data Center's Florida listings show no consumer purchase incentive. Instead, Florida's EV laws deal with charging stations, condominiums and insurance pricing. The FLHSMV fee schedule also has no separate EV charge, so an electric car pays the same weight-based registration as any other car.

Protection from EV insurance surcharges

Under § 627.06535, an insurer can't add a surcharge to an electric vehicle's premium because of new technology, passenger payload, weight-to-horsepower ratio, or materials such as aluminum or composites. The only exception is if the Office of Insurance Regulation reviews actuarial data and finds the surcharge justified. If a quote cites one of those four reasons, ask the insurer whether it has the required approval. If you get an unclear answer, you can call the Department of Financial Services consumer helpline.

Charging in a condominium

Under § 718.113, a condo association can't prohibit or restrict you from installing or using a charger in your own designated parking space. It can set reasonable rules, as long as they don't significantly increase the cost or block the installation. The association can require you to:

  • follow safety codes and architectural standards,
  • use a licensed installation contractor,
  • provide a certificate of insurance, and
  • reimburse any increase in the association's insurance premium caused by the charger.

You pay for the installation, electricity, maintenance, repair, and eventual removal or replacement, plus any damage the equipment causes. The law applies to condominiums. If you live in a rental or a homeowners' association, check your lease or HOA documents.

Rules for public chargers

Under § 366.94, the state regulates charging stations, and cities and counties can't adopt their own rules for them. The Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services writes the standards for placement, design, pricing displays and maintenance. Every public charger must be registered with the department before it opens, and the department can inspect stations, fine operators, or shut down equipment it considers unsafe. Local permits must follow the department's standards only. A business that isn't a utility can sell charging without being regulated as an electricity seller.

It's also illegal to stop, stand or park a vehicle that can't charge in a space marked for EV charging. Doing so is a noncriminal traffic infraction. Note that the law covers vehicles that can't plug in. It doesn't address a fully charged EV that stays parked in the space.

Utility home-charging programs

Since 2024, the Public Service Commission can approve voluntary utility charging programs, as long as other ratepayers won't end up subsidizing them. One example is FPL EVolution Home. It installs and maintains a Level 2 charger with no upfront payment for $36 a month, or $27 a month if you already have a permitted 240-volt circuit. Electricity is billed separately at a lower off-peak rate. The program requires a 10-year agreement, a single-family home or townhouse with an attached garage, and a strong Wi-Fi signal. You can't be on net metering or a time-of-use rate. Leaving early triggers a termination fee plus a $50 administrative fee, and you own the charger once the term ends. If FPL isn't your utility, ask your own utility what it offers.

Local financing

Florida law (§ 163.08) lets local governments help property owners finance charger installations. Ask your city or county whether it offers this.
