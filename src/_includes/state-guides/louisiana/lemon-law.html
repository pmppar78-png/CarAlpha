Louisiana's motor vehicle warranty chapter

Louisiana's lemon law is Chapter 27 of Title 51 of the Revised Statutes, R.S. 51:1941 through 51:1948, titled "Motor Vehicle Warranties." The Legislature enacted it in 1984 and has amended it several times, most recently in 2021. The chapter sets out a duty to repair, a presumption that the manufacturer has had enough attempts, and a refund or replacement. It also requires manufacturers to reimburse rental costs during long or repeated warranty repairs, and it requires a disclosure when a returned vehicle is resold.

Vehicles and people covered

Under R.S. 51:1941, a "motor vehicle" is a passenger vehicle, or a passenger and commercial vehicle as defined in R.S. 32:1252, sold in Louisiana on or after September 1, 1984. Personal watercraft and all-terrain vehicles used only for personal purposes are covered if they were sold, or still under warranty, on or after August 15, 1999. The chapter excludes:

  • Vehicles of 10,000 GVW or more
  • Vehicles used exclusively for commercial purposes

A "consumer" includes the original buyer of a new vehicle normally used for personal, family or household purposes, as long as the purchase wasn't for resale. It also includes anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the express warranty is still in effect, a person who leases the vehicle, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty. A later owner who buys a car still under the factory warranty may therefore have rights under the chapter.

A "nonconformity" is any specific or generic defect or malfunction, or any defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or both.

The duty to repair

R.S. 51:1942 requires you to report the nonconformity to the manufacturer or one of its authorized dealers and make the vehicle available for repair before the warranty expires or within one year of original delivery, whichever comes first. The manufacturer, its agent or dealer must then make the necessary repairs, even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period runs out. The chapter doesn't set a mileage cutoff.

The 45-day and four-repair presumption

R.S. 51:1943 presumes that a reasonable number of attempts have been made if, within the warranty term or one year from original delivery, whichever ends first:

  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair for a total of 45 or more calendar days
  • The same nonconformity has been repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer

A manufacturer that fails to respond to you, or fails to perform repairs within these time periods, waives its right to a final attempt to cure the problem. The warranty term is extended, and the presumption periods are suspended, for any time repairs can't be done because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or natural disaster.

Replacement, refund or lease buyback

Under R.S. 51:1944, the manufacturer must act if the problem hasn't been fixed after four or more attempts within the warranty term or first year, or if the vehicle has been out of service for repair for a total of 45 or more days during the warranty period. The manufacturer decides which remedy to provide:

  • Replace the vehicle with a comparable new one
  • Take it back and refund the full purchase price, plus any amounts you paid at the point of sale and all collateral costs, which the statute defines as sales tax, license and registration fees and similar government charges

The refund goes to you and any holder of a perfected security interest, as their interests appear. For a lease, the manufacturer can provide a replacement. If the lessor is willing, it can instead take the vehicle back, reimburse you for reasonable lease expenditures and satisfy the lease's early-termination conditions.

A reasonable allowance for use is deducted. It covers only use before you first notified the manufacturer, agent or dealer of the problem, and any later time the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. When you get the replacement or refund, you surrender the vehicle and an endorsed title. Under R.S. 51:1945, the manufacturer must deliver the replacement or refund within 30 days after you offer to transfer title, or within 30 days after its dispute program awards it.

Building your record

Every deadline in the chapter is measured from a date you'll need to prove. Keep the purchase or lease contract showing the delivery date, the warranty booklet showing when coverage ends, and each repair order with its drop-off and pickup dates. Those documents show the four-attempt count, the 45-day total and whether a repair ran past 10 work days for rental reimbursement. If you contact the manufacturer directly, keep a copy of the message and any reply, since a failure to respond can cost the manufacturer its final repair attempt.

Informal dispute programs and deadlines

Louisiana doesn't run a state lemon-law arbitration program. If a manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that substantially complies with the FTC's rules at 16 CFR part 703, R.S. 51:1944(D) says the refund and replacement provisions don't apply until you've first used that procedure. Your warranty booklet should say whether one exists.

You have three years from the purchase date, or one year after the warranty ends, whichever is longer, to sue the manufacturer to enforce the refund or replacement section. Under R.S. 51:1947, if you've complied with the chapter and win a judgment in whole or in part, you're entitled to the reasonable attorney fees you actually incurred. R.S. 51:1946 preserves any other remedies you have under other laws.

Rental reimbursement during repairs

R.S. 51:1948 requires the manufacturer to pay you directly up to $20 a day for a rental vehicle for the length of the repair when you take a car under warranty to the dealer that sold it, and either:

  • The repair takes more than 10 work days, counting the day you dropped it off
  • The same defect has already been taken to the dealer for repair twice before

This duty lasts for the length of the express warranty or two years, whichever ends first. It applies to cars sold after January 1, 1987, but not to personal watercraft or ATVs. If the manufacturer doesn't comply, you can recover your damages, at least $200, plus reasonable attorney fees. The duty doesn't apply when war, work stoppages or natural disasters beyond the manufacturer's control prevent timely repairs or parts delivery.

When a returned vehicle is resold

Under R.S. 51:1945.1, when a manufacturer, agent or dealer sells a used vehicle that was returned under this chapter, the manufacturer must give the buyer a written notice in capital letters. It must say the vehicle was returned because it didn't conform to its warranty and the defect wasn't fixed within the time Louisiana law allows. The notice must also be printed conspicuously on the title. A dealer that fails to deliver the notice can be fined $500 to $1,000 per violation. Separately, R.S. 51:1949 makes it an unfair trade practice to sell a 1980 or newer vehicle with disabled emission controls without telling the buyer.
