Pennsylvania's Automobile Lemon Law

Pennsylvania's Automobile Lemon Law is the Act of March 28, 1984, P.L. 150, No. 28. It sets out the rights of people who buy or lease a defective new motor vehicle. The law has been amended several times, including by Act 151 of 2024, which added specific rules for motorcycles. The full text is posted on the General Assembly's unconsolidated statutes site.

Which vehicles and buyers are covered

The law defines a "new motor vehicle" as a new and unused self-propelled conveyance driven on public roads that is designed to carry no more than 15 people. It must be bought or leased and registered in Pennsylvania, or bought or leased elsewhere and registered for the first time in Pennsylvania. It must also be used, leased or bought mainly for personal, family or household purposes. Demonstrators and dealer cars used before sale are included.

Since the 2024 amendment, the definition expressly includes motorcycles. It excludes motor homes, off-road vehicles and dual sport motorcycles driven off road. A dual sport motorcycle is one designed to be ridden both on and off road.

A "purchaser" includes a person who has gained possession or ownership of a new motor vehicle by lease, transfer or purchase, and that person's successors or assigns. Someone who has signed an agreement or contract to buy or lease one also counts, as long as the vehicle is for personal, family or household use.

A "nonconformity" is a defect or condition that substantially impairs the use, value or safety of the vehicle and doesn't conform to the manufacturer's express warranty.

Disclosure at the time of purchase

Section 3 has the Attorney General publish a statement in the Pennsylvania Bulletin explaining a purchaser's rights under the law. At the original purchase of a new vehicle, the manufacturer must give the buyer a written statement with a copy of the Attorney General's statement and a list of zone offices, with addresses and phone numbers, the buyer can contact to seek a remedy.

How long coverage lasts

Under section 4, the manufacturer must repair, at no cost to the purchaser, a nonconformity that occurs within these periods:

VehicleCoverage period (whichever comes first)
MotorcycleOne year after delivery, or the warranty term
Other new motor vehicleOne year after delivery, the first 12,000 miles, or the warranty term

The purchaser must deliver the vehicle to the manufacturer's authorized service and repair facility in Pennsylvania. If size, weight, method of attachment or installation, or the nature of the defect makes that unreasonable, the purchaser notifies the manufacturer or its facility instead. Written notice then counts as returning the vehicle. The manufacturer chooses whether to repair the vehicle where it is, pick it up, or arrange transport, and it pays the transport costs.

Presumption of a reasonable number of attempts

Section 6 presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of repair attempts if:

  • the same nonconformity has been repaired three times by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers and still exists, or
  • the vehicle has been out of service because of any nonconformity for a cumulative total of 30 or more calendar days.

For a motorcycle, the presumption also requires that manufacturer-sourced parts were used in the repair attempts. It applies to a motorcycle when all the attempts were made by the same authorized facility, or if the purchaser gives a complete set of repair records to an authorized facility that hasn't worked on the problem before. The facility that did the repairs provides an affidavit that the defect has been repaired three times.

The 30-day period can be extended by up to 30 more days if war, terrorism, civil unrest, fire, flood or natural disaster prevents the repair. It can be extended by up to 90 more days if the manufacturer files a sworn affidavit with the Office of Attorney General giving one of those reasons. Either extension requires that the owner is lent a vehicle at no charge for that period. Days a motorcycle spends in storage at an authorized facility don't count toward the 30 days if the purchaser signs a waiver or a storage contract.

Refund or replacement

If the manufacturer can't fix the nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts, section 5 lets the purchaser choose between:

  • a comparable replacement vehicle of equal value, or
  • a refund of the full purchase or lease price, including all collateral charges.

The manufacturer may deduct a reasonable allowance for use, capped at 10 cents per mile or 10% of the purchase or lease price, whichever is less. The allowance covers miles driven before the purchaser first reported the defect to the manufacturer. Refunds go to the purchaser and any lienholder as their interests appear, and a refund must be paid within 30 days after the consumer chooses it.

There's no refund or replacement if the defect doesn't substantially impair use, value or safety, or if it results from the purchaser's abuse, neglect, modification or alteration. For a motorcycle, that includes changes made after delivery.

Repair statements and dealer notice

Section 7 requires the manufacturer or dealer to give the purchaser a fully itemized statement of all work done, including parts and labor, every time the vehicle comes back from service or repair. When a purchaser brings the vehicle to the same dealer a second time for the same defect, the dealer must notify the manufacturer within seven days by certified mail, return receipt requested. Keep every repair statement, since they record each repair attempt and the days the vehicle was out of service.

Informal dispute settlement and lawsuits

If the manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies with 16 CFR Part 703, section 9 requires the purchaser to use it before suing over defects affecting substantial use, value or safety. The decision isn't binding on the purchaser, who may go on to court under section 8.

A purchaser who suffers a loss because the manufacturer didn't comply with the act may sue in a court of common pleas. In addition to other relief, the purchaser is entitled to reasonable attorneys' fees and all court costs. A violation of the Lemon Law is also a violation of the Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law.

Resale of returned vehicles

A vehicle repurchased under Pennsylvania's law or a similar law in another state can't be resold, transferred or leased in Pennsylvania unless:

  • the manufacturer gives the same express warranty as the original buyer had, lasting at least 12,000 miles or 12 months after resale, whichever comes first
  • the manufacturer gives the next buyer a separate written statement in 10-point capital type saying the vehicle was repurchased because it didn't conform to the warranty
  • the dealer, lessor or transferor discloses that notice before the sale and gets a signed receipt, kept for four years, and
  • a branded title is issued by PennDOT showing the vehicle was repurchased under the act.

Failing to notify the next buyer carries a $2,000 civil penalty per violation, and that buyer can choose a replacement or refund on the same terms as the original purchaser. A vehicle returned because of a complete failure of the braking or steering system likely to cause death or serious injury can't be resold in Pennsylvania at all.

Your rights can't be waived

Any agreement that waives, limits or disclaims rights under the act is void as against public policy. Where they apply, the rights extend to a later purchaser, lessee or transferee. The act doesn't limit other rights or remedies under any law, contract or warranty.
