Tennessee's lemon law

Tennessee's motor vehicle warranty law is in Tenn. Code Ann. Title 55, Chapter 24, sections 55-24-101 through 55-24-112. It applies when a new vehicle has a defect covered by the manufacturer's express warranty that the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer can't fix after a reasonable number of attempts. If the defect substantially impairs the vehicle, the manufacturer must either replace it with a comparable vehicle or take it back and refund the full purchase price. The law also requires a written notice to the manufacturer by certified mail before you can claim those remedies.

Who and what is covered

Section 55-24-101 defines a consumer as the purchaser of a motor vehicle, other than for resale, or the lessee. It also includes anyone the vehicle is transferred to while an express warranty still applies, and anyone else entitled under the warranty's terms to enforce it. Government entities are excluded, as are businesses or commercial entities that register three or more vehicles.

A lessee is covered when the vehicle is leased under a written lease agreement for which a manufacturer's warranty was issued as a condition of sale, or which makes the lessee responsible for repairs. For a vehicle built outside the United States, the importer counts as the manufacturer.

The law covers passenger vehicles that are sold and subject to registration and title in Tennessee, or under similar rules in another state, and classified as Class B under § 55-4-111. Motorcycles classified as Class A are also included. The definition leaves out:

  • motorized bicycles
  • motor homes
  • lawnmowers and garden tractors
  • recreational vehicles and off-road vehicles
  • vehicles over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight

The term of protection

Tennessee calls the coverage period the "term of protection." It runs for the term of the applicable express warranties or for one year after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, whichever comes first. The statute doesn't set a mileage cap. When a manufacturer hands over a replacement vehicle under the lemon law, the replacement gets its own term of protection, lasting one year from the date it's delivered.

If you report a defect to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer during the term of protection, § 55-24-102 requires them to fix it at no charge to you, even if the repairs happen after the term has ended. Dealers bill that work to the manufacturer the same way they bill other warranty repairs.

Under § 55-24-109, every time your vehicle comes back from service or repair, the manufacturer, agent or dealer must give you a copy of the repair order. It has to show all the work done, including parts and labor provided free or at a reduced cost under warranty, the date the vehicle was dropped off, the date it was returned and the odometer reading. These repair orders are the record of your repair attempts and days out of service, so keep every one.

When repair attempts are presumed reasonable

Section 55-24-105 presumes that a reasonable number of repair attempts has been made if either of these happens during the term of protection:

  • the same nonconformity has been subject to repair three or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers, and the problem continues, or
  • the vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative total of 30 or more calendar days

Both the term of protection and the 30-day period are extended by any time when repair services weren't available to you because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The law treats a defect as substantially impairing the vehicle if it makes the vehicle unreliable or unsafe for normal operation, or reduces its resale value below the average for comparable vehicles.

Written notice to the manufacturer

Before asking for a refund or replacement under § 55-24-103, you or your representative must notify the manufacturer in writing, by certified mail, that the nonconformity needs to be corrected or repaired. If the manufacturer's address isn't readily available in the owner's manual or warranty you received at purchase, mail the notice to an authorized dealer, which must forward it to the manufacturer.

If one of the presumption conditions already exists when you send the notice, the manufacturer gets one more opportunity to correct or repair the problem after receiving it. That final opportunity can't exceed 10 days.

Replacement or refund

If the defect substantially impairs the vehicle and can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, § 55-24-103 gives the manufacturer two choices. It can replace the vehicle with a comparable motor vehicle, meaning a new vehicle of comparable worth to the same make and model with all options and accessories, adjusted for any difference in model year. Or it can accept the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price.

The full purchase price is the actual cost you paid, including all collateral charges, minus a reasonable allowance for use. Collateral charges include:

  • items or service charges installed by the manufacturer or its agent
  • credit life and disability insurance charges
  • sales taxes
  • title charges, license fees and registration fees, and similar government charges
  • other reasonable expenses incurred to buy the vehicle

The allowance for use covers mileage before your first report of the problem to the manufacturer, agent or dealer, and any later period when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. It also covers a reasonable amount for damage beyond normal wear. The mileage part can't exceed half of the IRS standard mileage rate for business use of a personal vehicle, plus any loss in market value from damage beyond normal wear and tear that didn't result from the warranty defect.

The refund goes to you and any lienholder as your interests appear. Unless the lienholder agrees to move its lien to the replacement vehicle, it must be paid in full, with interest and other charges, before an exchange or refund. If the manufacturer or its finance subsidiary financed the vehicle and replaces it, it can't make you sign a refinancing agreement that adds obligations beyond your original financing. Funds a manufacturer needs to meet these obligations are held in trust for the consumers entitled to them.

Leased vehicles

Section 55-24-104 splits a refund on a leased vehicle between the two parties. You receive the lessee cost, which is the deposit and rental payments you've paid to the lessor, minus service fees. Service fees cover interest on those payments at two points above the prime rate on the date the lease was signed, plus insurance or other costs the lessor paid for your benefit.

The lessor receives the lease price minus the deposit and rental payments it already collected. The lease price adds up the lessor's actual purchase cost, freight and accessories if applicable, any fee paid to someone else to obtain the lease, and an amount equal to 5% of that total.

Informal dispute settlement

Under § 55-24-106, if a manufacturer has set up or takes part in an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies with 16 CFR Part 703 and with Chapter 24, and has you notified of it, you must use that procedure before you can claim a refund or replacement. On application, the Attorney General and Reporter decides whether a manufacturer's mechanism qualifies.

The panel works through a set order of questions. It decides whether the vehicle conforms to its express warranties and, if it doesn't, whether the defect substantially impairs it. Next it considers whether a reasonable number of repair attempts has been made and whether the manufacturer has had its chance to repair under § 55-24-102. If every answer goes your way, the panel must find that you're entitled to a refund or replacement, and it also sets the amount of collateral charges where appropriate.

Using the procedure doesn't take away your right to go to court. Under § 55-24-110, if you pursue another remedy in state or federal court, you can't recover more than § 55-24-103 allows unless you prove fault that caused greater damages.

Defenses, fees and deadlines

A manufacturer has two affirmative defenses: that the alleged defect doesn't substantially impair the vehicle, or that it results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations by the consumer.

A lawsuit under the chapter must be filed within six months after the express warranty expires or six months after the first anniversary of original delivery, whichever is later. The deadline is paused from the day you submit the dispute to an informal procedure until the date of its decision, or the date by which the manufacturer must carry it out, whichever comes later.

If you finally prevail, the court may award your costs and expenses, including attorneys' fees based on actual time spent, that it finds reasonable. You generally can't sue the seller or lessor under this chapter unless it is also the manufacturer, the manufacturer can't be served in Tennessee or reached through the state's long-arm statutes, or the manufacturer has been judicially declared insolvent.

The law doesn't limit rights you have under any other law. Any agreement in a purchase or lease that waives, limits or disclaims these rights is void, and the rights pass to a later owner of the vehicle.

Former fleet vehicles

Section 55-24-112 requires a business that buys a fleet of new vehicles, titles them in its own name and then sells them to individuals to disclose in writing any remaining manufacturer's warranty to the buyer.
