Required liability coverage

Every vehicle with a valid North Carolina registration needs continuous liability insurance under N.C.G.S. § 20-309, and the North Carolina Division of Motor Vehicles (NCDMV) says the policy has to come from a company licensed to do business in the state. Out-of-state policies aren't accepted. The minimums went up on July 1, 2025, under Session Law 2023-133 as amended by Session Law 2024-29:

CoveragePolicies issued or renewed before July 1, 2025Policies issued or renewed from July 1, 2025
Bodily injury, one person$30,000$50,000
Bodily injury, two or more people$60,000$100,000
Property damage$25,000$50,000

The new figures, usually written as 50/100/50, are the amounts in the definition of proof of financial responsibility in § 20-279.1(11). The Department of Insurance (NCDOI) told consumers to expect higher premiums at renewal on policies that had been written at the old minimums.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage

Section 20-279.21 requires uninsured motorist coverage in every bodily injury liability policy for a vehicle registered or principally garaged in the state. It pays you when the at-fault driver has no insurance. The uninsured limits match the greatest bodily injury and property damage liability limits on any one insured vehicle, capped at $1 million, though you can choose lower limits down to the state minimums. Uninsured motorist property damage coverage carries a $100 deductible for each insured.

NCDOI says underinsured motorist coverage is now included in every new or renewed policy. Before July 1, 2025, a policy written at the minimums didn't have to include it. The 2024 changes also measure whether a driver is underinsured against your total damages instead of the other driver's limits, limit setoffs against the coverage, other than workers' compensation, and let you combine the top limit from each policy that covers you.

Other rating changes from July 2025

The same legislation changed how premiums are set:

  • Inexperienced operators: drivers first licensed on or after July 1, 2025, pay an inexperienced operator surcharge for eight years. It used to last three. The surcharges for years four through eight are lower and drop each year.
  • Serious convictions: for convictions on or after that date carrying four or more Safe Driver Incentive Plan (SDIP) points, the surcharge lasts five years, up from three. Some speeding offenses are handled separately.
  • Minor speeding and PJCs: a conviction for speeding 10 mph or less over the limit, or a prayer for judgment continued, is normally waived for insurance purposes if there's no other qualifying violation in the lookback period. That lookback grows from three years to five.

Starting July 1, 2026, § 20-309(a3) bars a driver who is subject to the inexperienced operator surcharge from driving unless the policy covering them includes that surcharge.

Proof of insurance

You must show proof of financial responsibility to register a vehicle, and keep it for the whole registration period. Under § 20-309(b), it can be a liability policy, a financial security bond, a financial security deposit or self-insurance. Section 20-309(c1) allows you to show records of insurance on paper or as an electronic image on a phone from your insurer's app or website. If the NCDMV asks for records and you can't produce them, that's prima facie evidence you don't have coverage.

When coverage lapses

Insurers have to tell NCDMV when a policy is canceled or lapses. NCDMV then sends the owner a notice. Under § 20-311 the owner had 10 days to respond, and NCDMV says that window became 30 days on October 1, 2026. If your coverage never actually lapsed, have your insurer send a Certificate of Insurance (FS-1) electronically, and NCDMV will clear the record.

For an actual lapse, the civil penalty depends on the number of penalties you've had in the three years before it began:

Earlier lapse penalties in three yearsPenalty
None$50
One$100
Two or more$150

If you now have coverage, the car wasn't in a crash during the gap and nobody knowingly drove it uninsured, you pay the penalty and nothing more. A crash or knowing use during the lapse adds a 30-day registration revocation. A revocation for still lacking coverage, or for not answering the notice, lasts until you get insurance or transfer the vehicle. To re-register after a revocation you pay the penalty, a $50 restoration fee and the plate fee. During the revocation, the vehicle can't be registered to you, your spouse or your children or stepchildren, unless the spouse lives separately. Unpaid penalties block registration renewals for every vehicle in your name.

Driving, or letting someone drive, an uninsured vehicle that's registered or required to be registered here is a Class 3 misdemeanor under § 20-313.

Canceling a policy or moving away

NCDMV says to turn in your plate before you cancel your insurance, including when you store a vehicle for a long time. Plates can go to any license plate agency or by mail to the Vehicle Registration Section. A lost or stolen plate is canceled with form MVR-18A. If you move, § 20-311(g1) waives the penalty and restoration fee if you register the car in your new state within 30 days after the North Carolina policy ends, send NCDMV a copy of the new registration card and return the plate or file an affidavit that it was lost, stolen or destroyed. Service members deployed outside the continental United States for 45 days or more get a separate waiver under § 20-311(g).

Costs and uninsured drivers

The NAIC's 2024 combined average premium for liability, collision and comprehensive coverage in North Carolina was $1,233.32. The Insurance Research Council estimated that 11.8% of North Carolina drivers were uninsured, which ranked 29th among the states.
