New Jersey's New Car Lemon Law

New Jersey's lemon law is N.J.S.A. 56:12-29 and the sections after it, enacted as P.L.1988, c.123. In its findings section, the Legislature calls a new vehicle "a major, high cost consumer transaction" and says the act is meant to make manufacturers correct warranty defects reported within a set period, and to resolve disputes quickly when they don't. The Division of Consumer Affairs runs a Lemon Law Unit that sends eligible cases to a hearing before an administrative law judge, and you can file in Superior Court instead.

Vehicles and owners covered

Under N.J.S.A. 56:12-30, a "motor vehicle" is a passenger automobile, farm tractor, authorized emergency vehicle or motorcycle that was bought or leased in New Jersey or is registered with the Motor Vehicle Commission. The definition leaves out the living facilities of a motor home. A "consumer" is a buyer or lessee who didn't acquire the vehicle for resale or sublease, anyone the vehicle passes to while a warranty still applies, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it. A lease counts if it runs longer than 60 days.

The defect has to be a "nonconformity," which the statute defines as a defect or condition that substantially impairs the use, value or safety of the vehicle. A manufacturer is covered if it builds, assembles or distributes at least 10 new vehicles a year in the ordinary course of business.

The term of protection

The presumption in N.J.S.A. 56:12-33 covers the first 24,000 miles of operation or the two years after original delivery, whichever comes first. A farm tractor uses the two-year period alone. The two years and the day counts are extended for any time repair service isn't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

When the law presumes the car can't be fixed

The manufacturer or its dealer is presumed unable to repair the vehicle within a reasonable time if, during the term:

  • Substantially the same nonconformity has been repaired three or more times and still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a cumulative 20 or more calendar days, or 45 for a motor home, and a nonconformity still exists.
  • A nonconformity likely to cause death or serious bodily injury has been examined or repaired at least once and still exists.

For a motor home built by more than one manufacturer, a visit to a shop that isn't authorized to work on the defective part doesn't count. Repeat visits to different authorized shops for the same problem, made because the owner kept traveling, count as one attempt.

The certified-mail notice and the final repair

The presumption applies against the manufacturer only after it has received written notice of a potential claim, sent by certified mail with return receipt requested, and has had one chance to fix the problem within 10 calendar days of receiving it. You can send that letter once substantially the same problem has been repaired twice, once the car has been out of service 20 calendar days (45 for a motor home), or once a serious safety defect has been examined or repaired one time. The Division's rule, N.J.A.C. 13:45A-26.5, adds that the notice must go out within the term of protection.

Every time the car comes back from an examination or repair during the term, N.J.S.A. 56:12-34 requires an itemized statement of repair. It must describe the problem you reported, the diagnosis and the work done, any parts and labor you paid for, and the date and odometer reading when the car went in and when it was returned. These statements are the evidence the Lemon Law Unit asks for, so keep every one. At the sale or lease, the dealer or lessor must also hand you a written summary of your rights in English and Spanish on a separate sheet.

Refund, or a replacement if you accept one

When the manufacturer can't correct the defect in a reasonable time, N.J.S.A. 56:12-32 requires it to take the vehicle back and give a full refund. The refund includes:

  • The purchase price, including any stated trade-in credit.
  • Options or modifications the manufacturer or dealer installed or arranged within 30 days after delivery.
  • Sales tax, license and registration fees, and finance charges.
  • Towing, and the actual cost of renting an equivalent vehicle while yours was out of service for the defect.

The manufacturer may offer a replacement, but you can turn it down and demand the refund. A lessee gets back what was actually paid under the lease, the lease ends without an early-termination penalty, and the lessor is repaid separately. The refund goes to you and any lienholder as their interests appear on the MVC's ownership records.

The statute fixes the use deduction. The "reasonable allowance for vehicle use" is the odometer reading when you first brought the car in for the defect, multiplied by the purchase or lease price and divided by 100,000. A $40,000 car first presented at 8,000 miles would carry a deduction of $3,200. Under N.J.A.C. 13:45A-26.11, a refund in a state hearing also covers the filing fee, reasonable attorney's fees, expert witness fees and costs. A manufacturer that thinks your trade-in allowance was inflated can challenge it at the hearing, but it carries the burden of proof.

Applying to the Lemon Law Unit

If the defect is still there after the final repair chance and the manufacturer won't refund or replace the vehicle, you can apply to the Lemon Law Unit for dispute resolution. The application asks for the dates and mileage involved, a written account of the repair history, the certified-mail letter and its signed return receipt, the repair statements, and the sale or lease paperwork with receipts for taxes, fees, towing and rentals. Mail it to the Division of Consumer Affairs, Lemon Law Unit, P.O. Box 45026, Newark, NJ 07101. The Unit's phone number is 973-504-6226.

The Unit checks only whether the application is complete and meets the eligibility rules. It doesn't decide whether the defect is real. If the application is accepted, the Unit asks for a $50 filing fee by certified check or money order. The fee is nonrefundable, but you recover it if you win. The case then goes to the Office of Administrative Law, and the hearing is set for no later than 20 days after the notice of acceptance unless you agree to a later date. You may apply only once for a given vehicle.

The administrative law judge's proposed decision goes to the Director of the Division, who has 15 days to adopt, reject or change it. The application instructions say the Director's decision binds both sides, subject to an appeal to the Superior Court, and they suggest talking to a lawyer because the manufacturer will have one. An appeal must be filed within 45 days of the final decision. A manufacturer that appeals has to post a bond covering the award plus $2,500 for fees and costs. If it unreasonably fails to comply with a final decision, the rule makes it liable for $5,000 a day.

Branded titles on returned cars

A car returned to the manufacturer under the lemon law, a similar law in another state, a lawsuit or an informal dispute program can't be resold or re-leased in New Jersey until its title is stamped "R—RETURNED TO MANUFACTURER UNDER LEMON LAW OR OTHER PROCEEDING." The stamped document goes to the MVC's Special Title Section within 10 days so the title is permanently branded. The next buyer must get a "Notice of Nonconformity" on a separate sheet and sign a receipt for it.

Used cars

New Jersey's used-car lemon law is N.J.S.A. 56:8-67 and the sections after it. The Lemon Law Unit reviews used-car applications too, and a qualifying dispute goes to a hearing with the Office of Administrative Law.
