Rhode Island's lemon law

Rhode Island's lemon law is chapter 31-5.2 of the General Laws, titled "Lemon Law." It requires manufacturers to repair new vehicles that don't conform to their warranties and to refund or replace vehicles they can't fix. It also sets up a state arbitration board run by the Department of Attorney General.

Who and what the law covers

Under § 31-5.2-1, a "motor vehicle" is an automobile, truck, motorcycle or van with a registered gross vehicle weight under 10,000 pounds that is sold, leased or replaced by a dealer or manufacturer. Motorized campers are excluded. Fire apparatus owned or leased by a municipality, municipal agency or fire district is also covered, if it hasn't been altered in a way that breaches the warranty.

A "consumer" is a buyer who isn't purchasing for resale, anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while a warranty is in effect, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty. A "lessee" is someone who leases a vehicle for one year or more under a written lease that makes the lessee responsible for repairs, or under a lease-purchase agreement.

A "nonconformity" is any specific or generic defect or malfunction, or combination of them, that substantially impairs the use, market value or safety of the vehicle.

Term of protection

The term of protection is one year or 15,000 miles from the date the new vehicle was first delivered to the consumer, whichever comes first. A replacement vehicle provided under the law gets its own term of one year or 15,000 miles from its delivery.

Under § 31-5.2-2, if you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent, or its authorized dealer or lessor during the term, the manufacturer must make the repairs needed to conform the vehicle to the warranty, even if the repairs happen after the term ends. The law covers express warranties and the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose.

Reasonable number of repair attempts

Section 31-5.2-5 presumes a reasonable number of attempts has been made if, within the term of protection:

  • the same nonconformity has been repaired four or more times and still exists or has come back, or
  • the vehicle has been out of service for repair of any nonconformity for a cumulative total of 30 or more calendar days.

The manufacturer then gets one more chance to fix the problem, lasting no more than seven calendar days, even if it starts after the term ends. That extra period begins on the day the manufacturer first knows, or should have known, that the four-repair or 30-day limit was reached.

The term, the 30 days and the extra cure period are extended for any time repair services aren't available because of war, invasion, fire, flood or other natural disaster, or because of a strike. During a strike, the manufacturer or dealer must provide free use of a comparable vehicle to a consumer whose car is out of service. The manufacturer has the burden of showing that the event caused the delay, and extensions for overlapping events don't add together.

Refund or replacement

If the manufacturer can't fix the nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts, § 31-5.2-3 requires it to take the vehicle back and, at your option, either:

  • refund the full contract or lease price, including all credits and allowances for a trade-in, less a reasonable allowance for use, or
  • replace it with a comparable new vehicle in good working order.

A manufacturer that chooses replacement has 30 calendar days from the return to deliver a comparable vehicle. If it doesn't, it must refund the price less the use allowance.

The use allowance is the total contract price or lessee cost multiplied by a fraction: the miles driven before the first report of the problem, plus miles driven during later periods when the vehicle wasn't in for repair, divided by 100,000. You may keep using the vehicle until you're offered a refund or a replacement you accept, and that use is counted in the allowance.

The manufacturer must also reimburse:

  • on a replacement, registration transfer fees and sales tax caused by the replacement
  • on a refund, incidental costs including sales tax, registration fees, finance charges and nonremovable options added by an authorized dealer or lessor, and
  • in either case, towing and reasonable rental costs caused by the defect, if free towing and a comparable rental weren't provided.

If the manufacturer or its finance arm financed the vehicle, it can't require a refinancing agreement on worse terms for the replacement. For a lease, the lessee receives the lessee cost and the lessor receives the lease price less those payments, and the lease ends with no early termination penalty.

Manufacturer defenses

Under § 31-5.2-4, the manufacturer has an affirmative defense if the nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety, or if it results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized substantial modification or alteration by the consumer or lessee.

Arbitration

If a manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies with 16 CFR Part 703, or that the FTC or the Rhode Island Attorney General has approved, § 31-5.2-7 requires you to use it, or the state board, before you can claim a refund or replacement. The requirement applies when you got clear written notice of the procedure when the vehicle was delivered. A decision binds the manufacturer if you accept it, and the manufacturer has 30 days to perform.

Section 31-5.2-7.1 has the Department of Attorney General run the five-member Motor Vehicle Arbitration Board. You file a complaint on the Attorney General's form with a nonrefundable $20 fee, and the manufacturer pays a $50 fee. The board can subpoena witnesses and documents, and both sides can present testimony and evidence and be represented by counsel. An independent technical expert may advise the board.

The board must decide within 90 days after the dispute is found eligible, and you have five days to accept or reject the decision. If the decision favors you and you accept it, the manufacturer has 30 days to comply or to appeal to superior court. An appeal requires a bond equal to the award plus $2,500 for attorneys' fees. If the court upholds a refund or replacement, you may also recover $25 a day for each day the vehicle was out of use after its return, unless a free comparable vehicle was provided. If the court finds the appeal frivolous or without a reasonable basis, it doubles the total award.

Going to court

You can sue in superior court under § 31-5.2-10, and a prevailing consumer is awarded reasonable attorney's fees. Using an informal procedure doesn't take away your rights under the chapter, and time limits are paused from the start of the procedure until 30 days after the final decision. Under § 31-5.2-12, a suit must be filed within three years of original delivery or within two years of the date the vehicle reached 15,000 miles, whichever is earlier. A manufacturer's failure to comply is also a deceptive trade practice under chapter 6-13.1.

Resale and waivers

A vehicle returned under the law can't be resold or re-leased in Rhode Island without clear written disclosure, before the sale, that it was returned because of a nonconformity. The Attorney General prescribes the form. Any agreement that waives, limits or disclaims these rights is void, and the rights pass to a later owner of the vehicle.
