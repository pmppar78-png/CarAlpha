The New Motor Vehicle Quality Assurance Act

Arkansas's lemon law is the New Motor Vehicle Quality Assurance Act, Act 297 of 1993, codified at Ark. Code Ann. §§ 4-90-401 through 4-90-417. Three agencies have a role in it. The Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division certifies manufacturers' dispute programs. The Department of Finance and Administration handles the sales-tax side of a buyback. And because a violation of the act is a deceptive trade practice under the Arkansas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (§ 4-90-417), the AG can enforce it too.

Who is covered

The act protects the buyer or lessee of a new or previously untitled vehicle, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty during the coverage period, as long as the vehicle was titled and registered as the law requires (§ 4-90-403). A lessee has to have a written lease of a year or more that makes the lessee responsible for repairs. Mopeds, motorcycles, and the living quarters of a motor home are excluded. So are vehicles over 14,000 pounds GVWR, except motor homes, and vehicles over 10,000 pounds that were substantially altered after the dealer sale. The act counts more than outright failures. A "nonconformity" includes any defect or combination of defects that substantially impairs use, market value, or safety, and also any breach of the express warranty or the implied warranty of merchantability.

A longer coverage period than most states

Arkansas measures its lemon-law period differently from its neighbors. Coverage lasts 24 months from original delivery or the first 24,000 miles, whichever is later, according to the DFA's new car lemon law page. Most states end coverage at whichever comes first. A high-mileage driver who passes 24,000 miles in the first year is still covered through month 24. A low-mileage driver stays covered until 24 months have passed, even with only a few thousand miles on the car.

When the law presumes a lemon

Under § 4-90-410, Arkansas presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts to repair a defect in three situations:

  1. Three repairs of the same defect that substantially impairs the vehicle, or a single repair attempt on a defect likely to cause death or serious bodily injury, followed by the manufacturer's final attempt, with the defect still there;
  2. a cumulative 30 calendar days out of service for repairs; or
  3. five or more repair attempts, on separate occasions, for any defects that together substantially impair the use and value of the vehicle.

The third route is unusual. A car with a series of different problems, none repaired three times, can still qualify if together they make the car substantially less usable or less valuable. The 30-day count is extended for delays caused by war, invasion, strikes, fire, floods, or natural disasters. If those delays push the car past 30 days in the shop, the manufacturer or dealer has to give you a free loaner. The manufacturer bears the burden of proving the delay was the direct cause of its failure to fix the car.

The final-repair letter

Notice is the step owners most often get wrong. After the third failed repair, or after the first one for a dangerous defect, you have to notify the manufacturer in writing by certified or registered mail that the car still needs repair (§ 4-90-406(a)). A complaint to the dealer doesn't count. The manufacturer then has two 10-day windows:

  • 10 days after receiving your letter to direct you to a reasonably accessible repair facility; and
  • 10 days after you deliver the car there to fix it.

If the manufacturer misses either deadline, it loses its right to a final repair attempt, and the law then conclusively presumes it had a reasonable number of attempts. That presumption can't be rebutted. Keep the certified-mail receipt and mark the dates on a calendar.

Refund or replacement within 40 days

Once the manufacturer has failed after a reasonable number of attempts, it has 40 days to act (§ 4-90-406(b)). You have an unconditional right to choose a refund over a replacement. The two options:

  • Replacement: a vehicle acceptable to you, in exchange for a reasonable offset for your use.
  • Refund: the full purchase or lease price, minus a use offset and minus a reasonable offset for any physical damage the car took while you owned it.

Either way, the manufacturer also pays all collateral charges and reasonably incurred incidental charges. The DFA lists sales tax, title tax, and extended warranties as collateral charges. The statute fixes the use offset: the price you paid, including transportation and installed options, times the miles driven before you first took the car in for the problem, divided by 120,000. On a $36,000 car first taken in at 4,000 miles, the offset is $36,000 × 4,000 ÷ 120,000 = $1,200. That's a gentler formula than the 100,000-mile divisor many states use.

Sales-tax refunds and the trade-in voucher

The DFA handles two tax pieces of a buyback. First, a manufacturer that refunds your sales tax can recover it from the DFA. It applies with your assignment of the refund and proof that it paid you. Second, if you traded in a car toward the lemon, you can ask for a trade-in voucher. It lets you claim the same trade-in allowance against the replacement car you buy, so you don't pay sales tax on the full price. Send a written request to the Revenue Division's Tax Credits/Special Refunds Section with the original bill of sale describing the trade, the sales-tax receipt showing the trade allowance, and the manufacturer's buyback agreement. The voucher is good for six months and has to be presented when you register the replacement.

The in-state dispute program comes first

Arkansas doesn't run a state arbitration board. Instead, § 4-90-414 requires every manufacturer that sells or warrants cars here to run or join an informal dispute program located in Arkansas. Before you can sue for a refund or replacement, you generally have to use that program first. Two exceptions apply: the manufacturer lets you skip it, or it failed to give you the disclosures the law requires. Arkansas also sets its own rules for these programs, on top of the federal 16 CFR Part 703 rules:

  • You can't be charged a fee to take part.
  • You can bring a lawyer, and you can appear and argue in person in Arkansas.
  • You have to be shown every document the program considers. If something new is handed over at the meeting, you can ask for a postponement to review it.
  • If the manufacturer claims a problem is "within specifications," you can hire your own technical expert to review that claim before the hearing.
  • One arbitrator hears the case, chosen by rotation, unless you ask for a three-member panel. The programs submit their arbitrator pools to the AG's Consumer Protection Division every year.
  • Once you accept a decision, the manufacturer has no more than 30 days to carry it out.

Going to court

You can file a civil action to enforce the act (§ 4-90-415). If you win, the court adds your reasonable costs and attorney's fees, based on the lawyer's actual time, to the judgment. Incidental charges you can recover include towing and alternative transportation caused by the defect. Lost income, loss of use, and personal-injury claims aren't covered (§ 4-90-403(5)). The act doesn't limit your other remedies, including federal Magnuson-Moss warranty claims.

Resale of buybacks

A returned vehicle can be resold in Arkansas only if the manufacturer gives the next buyer the same express warranty for at least 12 months or 12,000 miles, plus a signed written disclosure that it was returned for an uncured defect (§ 4-90-412).
