How Alabama's lemon law works

Alabama's new-car lemon law is Chapter 20A of Title 8 of the Code of Alabama, "Motor Vehicle Lemon Law Rights," sections 8-20A-1 through 8-20A-6. It dates from Act 90-479 of 1990 and it is short: six sections, no state arbitration board, and no filing fee to a state agency. What it does give you is a clear rights period, a presumption that kicks in after a set number of failed repairs, and a refund formula written into the statute.

The law protects the buyer of a new or previously untitled vehicle bought mainly for personal, family, or household use, along with anyone else the warranty lets enforce it. A "motor vehicle" here is a self-propelled vehicle meant for public highways. Two groups are carved out: motor homes, and any vehicle with a manufacturer's gross vehicle weight rating of 10,000 pounds or more. A used car bought from a dealer is outside Chapter 20A unless it was never titled.

The rights period and the 24-month repair window

Alabama uses two clocks, and mixing them up is the most common mistake owners make.

  • The lemon law rights period ends one year after original delivery or at 12,000 miles, whichever comes first (§ 8-20A-1(8)). The defect has to first show up, and be reported, inside this window.
  • The repair window runs 24 months or 24,000 miles from delivery, whichever comes first (§ 8-20A-2(a) and (c)). Repair attempts counted toward the presumption must fall inside it, and at least one of them has to fall inside the one-year rights period.

So a transmission that starts slipping at 14 months is not a lemon-law claim in Alabama, even if the warranty still covers the repair. A transmission that started slipping at month ten, was reported then, and kept failing through month twenty can be.

What counts as a lemon

The statute calls the defect a "nonconforming condition." It has to break the manufacturer's written warranty, significantly impair the use, value, or safety of the car, and arise from ordinary use. Problems caused by abuse, neglect, unauthorized modification, or an accident after delivery do not count (§ 8-20A-1(6)).

Alabama presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of chances when, within the 24-month or 24,000-mile window, either of these happened (§ 8-20A-2(c)):

  1. The same condition was repaired three or more times by the manufacturer or its dealers, at least once during the rights period, plus a final attempt by the manufacturer, and it is still there; or
  2. The car was out of service in the shop's custody for a cumulative 30 calendar days for repairs, counting the final attempt, with at least one visit during the rights period. Days lost to war, strikes, fire, flood, or other natural disasters are excluded.

Alabama has no separate shortcut for safety defects. A brake failure and a rattling dashboard both need three tries plus the final attempt, or 30 days down.

Notice: the certified letter is not optional

Before you can sue, § 8-20A-3(a) requires written notice to the manufacturer by certified U.S. mail demanding repair. The statute spells out what the letter has to contain (§ 8-20A-1(7)): a description of the vehicle, the defect, and every earlier repair attempt, with who did it and when. Repair orders are the evidence here, so ask for a copy every time and check that each one records your complaint in your own words.

If the presumption already exists when the manufacturer gets your letter, the final-repair rules start running:

  • Within 7 calendar days of receiving the letter, the manufacturer must tell you which reasonably accessible repair facility to use.
  • Once you deliver the car there, the manufacturer has no more than 14 calendar days to fix it.

Send the letter to the address in your warranty booklet or owner's manual, keep the green-card receipt, and keep a log of the dates the car was in and out of the shop.

Arbitration and the manufacturer's dispute program

Alabama does not run a state lemon-law arbitration program or hearing board. The dispute route that matters is the manufacturer's. Under § 8-20A-3(a), if the manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies with federal rules (the FTC's 16 CFR Part 703), you must use it before you file suit. Check the warranty booklet: if it names a program, file there first and keep the decision letter. You are not bound to accept the program's decision before going to court.

What you can recover

If the car qualifies, the manufacturer must, at your option, replace it with a comparable new vehicle or take it back and refund (§ 8-20A-2(b)):

  • the full contract price, including undercoating, dealer prep, transportation, installed options, and the nonrefundable part of any extended warranty or service contract;
  • collateral charges such as sales tax, license and registration fees;
  • finance charges you paid after you first reported the problem; and
  • incidental damages, including the reasonable cost of alternate transportation while the car was out of service.

The manufacturer can deduct a use allowance for the miles you drove before your first report of the defect. The statute fixes the formula: purchase price × miles before first report ÷ 100,000. On a $40,000 car first reported at 6,000 miles, that offset is $40,000 × 6,000 ÷ 100,000, or $2,400. Refunds go to you and to any lienholder as your interests appear.

If the manufacturer is found to have broken its obligations, § 8-20A-3(c) adds reasonable attorney's fees on top of the refund or replacement. The manufacturer can defend by showing the defect does not significantly impair use, market value, or safety, or that it came from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes (§ 8-20A-3(b)). Under § 8-20A-5, the selling dealer is not liable under this chapter; the claim is against the manufacturer.

Deadline to sue

File within three years of the original delivery date (§ 8-20A-6). The clock does not wait for the manufacturer's program to finish, so if the program is slow, track the date yourself.

Lemon buybacks resold in Alabama

A car returned under Alabama's law, or a similar law in another state, can be resold here only if the manufacturer tells the next buyer in writing that it was returned and why. The manufacturer must also retitle it through the Alabama Department of Revenue. ADOR brands that title and every later one with: "THIS VEHICLE WAS RETURNED TO THE MANUFACTURER BECAUSE IT DID NOT CONFORM TO ITS WARRANTY" (§ 8-20A-4). If you are shopping used, that brand on an Alabama title tells you the car's history.

Where to get help in Alabama

The Attorney General's Consumer Interest Division takes consumer complaints and can mediate with a business, but it does not act as your lawyer or give legal advice. Its hotline is 1-800-392-5658 (334-242-7335), and the complaint form is online. A claim that misses the one-year rights period may still be a federal warranty claim under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 2301–2312, which our federal lemon law guide covers.
