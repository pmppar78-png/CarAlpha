Georgia's lemon law at a glance

The Georgia Lemon Law (O.C.G.A. §§ 10-1-780 to 10-1-798) is run by the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division (CPD). The division doesn't represent either side. It explains the process, screens applications, and provides state-run arbitration free to both parties. Georgia also requires you to use that arbitration before you can sue under the lemon law (§ 10-1-788), so the steps below matter.

Who and what is covered

The law covers a new motor vehicle leased, bought or registered in Georgia whose title went straight from the dealer to its first owner. A vehicle whose paperwork shows it was sold used isn't covered, and neither are motorcycles, golf carts, or trucks with a gross vehicle weight rating over 12,000 pounds. For motor homes, coverage is limited to the chassis and drivetrain. The living quarters fall outside the law.

You count as a consumer if you bought or leased the vehicle for personal, family or household use. Businesses that buy or lease ten or fewer new vehicles a year also qualify, except limousine services. A lease must run at least a year and make you responsible for repairs.

The problem has to be a nonconformity: a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety, or that breaks the warranty. Damage from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications doesn't count.

The rights period

Under § 10-1-782(10), your lemon law rights period ends two years after original delivery or after 24,000 miles, whichever comes first. It's extended one day for each day repairs aren't available because of a strike, war, terrorist act, blackout, fire, flood, other disaster or declared state of emergency. If the car is in the shop on the day the period would end, the period stays open until that repair attempt is finished. Georgia counts all deadlines in calendar days.

A reasonable number of attempts

Section 10-1-784(a) says the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts if, during the rights period:

  • A serious safety defect has been repaired once and is still there. That means a life-threatening defect, a malfunction that interferes with your control of the vehicle, or a risk of fire or explosion.
  • The same nonconformity has been repaired three times and is still there.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a total of 30 days, for one or more problems.

A manufacturer, its agent or the dealer can't refuse to diagnose or repair a reported problem to avoid lemon-law liability.

The final repair attempt

Once you hit one of those thresholds, you must notify the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested, or statutory overnight delivery, at the address listed in your owner's manual. The deadlines then run like this:

  1. The manufacturer has 7 days from receiving your notice to tell you which reasonably accessible repair facility to use.
  2. You have until the 14th day to deliver the car there.
  3. The manufacturer has 28 days from receiving your notice to make its final repair attempt. If you deliver the car after day 14, it gets 14 days from delivery instead.

If the manufacturer misses the 7-day reply or the 28-day repair deadline, it loses its right to a final attempt.

Choosing a refund or replacement

If the final attempt fails, or the car has been out of service for 30 days during the rights period, the manufacturer must buy the car back or replace it, whichever you choose. Send your choice in writing by the same certified or overnight method. The manufacturer then has 20 days to act.

  • Repurchase: You get the purchase price plus collateral charges (sales tax, title fees, dealer options, earned finance charges) and incidental costs (repair bills, towing, alternative transportation). A reasonable offset for use is subtracted. Payment goes to you and any lienholder.
  • Replacement: You get an identical or equivalent new vehicle, plus your incidental costs and the charges the replacement deal creates.
  • Leases: On a repurchase, the lessee gets back what they've paid, less the offset, and the lessor gets 110% of the adjusted capitalized cost. On a replacement, the lease continues with the new VIN.

The offset uses the miles on the car when you first brought it in for the defect. It's the purchase price times those miles, divided by 120,000 (90,000 for a motor home). For example, a $45,000 SUV first taken in at 4,000 miles has an offset of $1,500.

Repair orders and records

Every time the car comes back, the dealer or manufacturer must give you an itemized, legible repair order (§ 10-1-783). It must show the problem you reported, the dates and odometer readings when the car went in and came out, the results of any test drive or diagnostic, the diagnosis, and the parts and labor. You can also ask for a copy of any report or computer readout the manufacturer's representative prepared about your car. Your owner's manual must list the manufacturer's customer-service addresses, email, fax and toll-free numbers. When you buy the car, the dealer must give you the Attorney General's written statement of your rights.

State-run arbitration

Manufacturers can have their informal dispute programs certified by the Attorney General. If yours has one, you must use it first, and file within a year after the rights period ends. According to the CPD's process page, no manufacturer currently has a certified program in Georgia. You also don't have to go through non-certified programs such as BBB AUTO LINE before applying to the state.

Your application for state arbitration is due within one year after the rights period ends, or within 60 days after a certified program finishes, whichever is later. Call the Lemon Law Team at 404-458-3827 to request one. The CPD only accepts the original form it mails you. Attach copies of your documents and keep the originals for the hearing. If the CPD rules you ineligible, you can appeal that decision to an arbitrator.

The CPD says hearings are scheduled within 40 days after a case is accepted and held within 120 miles of your home if you live in Georgia. Usually one arbitrator hears a case, and three hear motor-home cases. Arbitrators must be Georgia attorneys or experienced dispute-resolution professionals, with no ties to the auto industry. If you lease, you must notify your leasing company within 10 days of being accepted. A lawyer isn't required. You can bring witnesses, and you should bring the car if the arbitrator might need to see the defect.

An arbitrator who finds in your favor must award a repurchase or replacement, and may also award attorney's fees and expert-witness fees.

Appeals and penalties

Either side can appeal to superior court within 30 days, and the case is retried from scratch (§ 10-1-787). If the manufacturer appeals and loses, it also pays your costs from the appeal, including continuing collateral and incidental costs, attorney's fees, expert fees, and court costs. A manufacturer that doesn't appeal has 40 days to comply. After that, the Attorney General can impose a civil penalty of up to $1,000 a day.

Reacquired vehicles

A car bought back under Georgia's law, or a similar law in another state, can only be resold, leased or transferred if the buyer is told in writing that it was reacquired and what the defect was. The manufacturer must also warrant the repair for one year or 12,000 miles (§ 10-1-790). Hiding a buyback is a deceptive practice that lets the buyer sue. Manufacturers must report each reacquisition and resale to the Attorney General within 30 days.
