Required coverage in Maine

Under 29-A M.R.S. § 1601, the owner or operator of any vehicle registered in Maine, or required to be registered here, must carry the financial responsibility amounts set out in § 1605. A policy must provide at least:

  • $50,000 for injury to or death of any one person
  • $100,000 for injury to or death of more than one person in one accident
  • $25,000 for damage to property
  • $2,000 in medical payments coverage
  • $500 for towing and storage

The coverage must be in place for every registered vehicle and must apply to accidents anywhere in the United States or Canada. You can show proof at registration electronically, including as an image on your phone. A trailer, camp trailer or mobile home doesn't need separate proof if your vehicle policy already covers it. Instead of a policy, you can deposit money or securities with the Treasurer of State. That deposit has to equal the policy amounts, and the Secretary of State then issues a certificate of compliance good for up to one year at a time.

Medical payments and towing

Section 1605-A requires at least $2,000 per person for medical costs that the driver and passengers of the insured vehicle incur after an accident. It covers costs incurred within one year of the injury. A 2023 law added § 1605-B, which requires up to $500 per accident for reasonable towing and storage when police ask for the insured vehicle to be towed. Neither requirement applies to policies insuring more than four vehicles, or to policies covering a garage, dealership, repair shop, service station or public parking place.

Uninsured vehicle coverage

24-A M.R.S. § 2902 requires every auto liability policy for a vehicle registered or principally garaged in Maine to include uninsured vehicle coverage. It protects you against uninsured, underinsured and hit-and-run drivers. The statute treats a vehicle as underinsured if its coverage is below Maine's bodily injury minimums or below the limits of your own uninsured vehicle coverage.

For private passenger policies under the Maine Automobile Insurance Cancellation Control Act or the assigned risk plan, your uninsured coverage must match your bodily injury liability limits unless you reject that amount in writing. Even then, it can't fall below the state minimums. The rejection must be on the insurer's form, signed and dated by a named insured, and include the statement quoted in the law. If the insurer doesn't receive a signed form before the policy takes effect, it must provide the equal coverage. If you keep the same uninsured limits with the same insurer for two years in a row, you're presumed to have accepted that amount on future policies until you ask in writing to change it. Renewing within 30 days of a policy's expiration counts as continuous coverage, so you don't need to sign a new rejection.

Showing proof of insurance

When an officer stops you for a moving violation, or you're in a crash that must be reported, the officer will ask for proof of insurance. You can show it on a phone, and doing so doesn't give the officer consent to look at anything else on the device. Not producing proof is prima facie evidence that you're uninsured, and showing a policy that isn't in effect is also a violation. If you were insured at the time, you can get the summons dismissed. Send the District Court Violations Bureau a copy of the summons with proof of the coverage before your answer is due, or present the proof at trial. Under § 1601(8), an insurance agent isn't liable for an inaccurate ID card that was based on its records or on false statements by the insured.

Penalties for driving uninsured

Under § 1601(5), failing to maintain insurance is a traffic infraction with a forfeiture of $100 to $500. Thirty days after the Secretary of State receives the record of the violation, it suspends your license, the registration of your vehicle, or your right to apply for either. The suspension lasts until you file proof of financial responsibility under § 1605, and anyone who violates § 1601 becomes subject to those proof-filing requirements. Under § 1605(6), if you're required to maintain that proof and you drive without it, or knowingly let someone else drive your vehicle without it, that's a Class D crime. The Secretary of State may issue a restricted license while you keep the required proof on file.

Higher limits for some vehicles

Section 1611 sets higher limits for certain commercial and rental vehicles. Rental cars must meet the same limits as § 1605. Rental trucks of 26,000 pounds or less that are rented for less than 30 days need a $125,000 combined single limit, or 50/100/25 split limits. Intrastate commercial motor vehicles need a $750,000 combined single limit. Insurers must give the Secretary of State at least 30 days' notice before canceling a policy covered by that section. Under § 1612, dealers and transporters need 100/300/100 coverage before they can get plates.

What Mainers pay

In NAIC's 2024 Average Premium Supplement, Maine's combined average premium is $1,000.13, up from $789.47 in 2020, and its average expenditure is $934.54. The Insurance Research Council estimates that 5.7% of Maine drivers are uninsured, ranking the state 51st.
