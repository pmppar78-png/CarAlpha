The Illinois EPA electric vehicle rebate

Illinois pays a cash rebate to residents who buy an electric car. The Illinois Environmental Protection Agency runs it under the Electric Vehicle Rebate Act (415 ILCS 120/27), and it opens in application cycles. The current cycle runs from August 1 through December 31, 2026. For the state fiscal year ending June 30, 2027, the program's appropriation is $14 million, and rebates are paid only while money remains in the Electric Vehicle Rebate Fund.

  • $2,000 for an eligible new or used all-electric vehicle
  • $4,000 for a low-income applicant (the standard $2,000 plus an extra $2,000)
  • $1,500 for an eligible electric motorcycle, for any applicant

Low-income applicants are paid first. After that, rebates go out in order of postmark date. In the previous cycle, which ran from October 28, 2025 through May 31, 2026, IEPA received 3,499 applications and issued 3,001 rebates, 1,658 of them to low-income applicants.

Who qualifies

IEPA's FAQ lists the basic requirements:

  • The vehicle must run only on electricity, plug in to charge, and be legal on all public roads, including interstates. Plug-in hybrids, extended-range EVs, golf carts, off-road vehicles and anything with a top speed below 45 mph don't qualify.
  • The selling price can't exceed $80,000, not counting taxes, registration fees, documentary fees and separately itemized add-ons such as extended warranties.
  • You must buy the vehicle from a dealer licensed by the Illinois Secretary of State. Leases, rentals, out-of-state dealer purchases and vehicles delivered outside Illinois are excluded.
  • You must live in Illinois at purchase and when the rebate is issued, and your household income can't exceed 500% of the federal poverty line.
  • You can't have received a rebate under this program in the previous 10 years, and the vehicle itself can't have received one before.
  • You must keep the vehicle and keep it registered in Illinois for at least 12 consecutive months after purchase.

"Low-income" means household income at or below 80% of the area median income set by HUD for your county or metropolitan area. You can finance the purchase, but leased vehicles do not qualify. If you don't meet the requirements, you have to repay the full rebate and notify IEPA within 60 days.

Applying on time

Applications are accepted only by physical mail, postmarked within 180 days after the purchase date and during an open cycle. The purchase date is the day both you and the dealer sign the purchase agreement. Anything postmarked before August 1 or after December 31, 2026 is rejected. IEPA suggests taking the envelope to a post office counter and asking for a hand postmark. Funds can't be reserved before you buy. Questions go to EPA.EV@Illinois.gov.

Registration costs for an EV

Since January 1, 2020, Illinois has charged electric vehicles the standard first-division registration fee plus $100 a year in place of motor fuel tax. The Secretary of State's electric vehicle plate page lists the totals:

  • Newly bought vehicle: $416 ($165 title plus $251 registration)
  • Already titled vehicle getting EV plates for the first time: $251
  • Annual renewal: $251

One dollar of each $100 surcharge goes to the Secretary of State Special Services Fund, and the rest goes to the Road Fund. To get EV plates, mail an Affirmation for Electric Vehicle, a VSD-190, the tax forms and the fees to the Special Plates Division in Springfield. Converted vehicles also need photos of the vehicle, motor and battery.

No emissions test for battery EVs

Battery electric vehicles are exempt from the state emissions inspection under 625 ILCS 5/13C. Even in the Chicago and Metro East test areas, a fully electric car doesn't need an emissions test.

Charging access at home and in public

Provisions in 765 ILCS 1085 protect drivers who don't own a detached house:

  • Condominiums: an association can't ban or restrict installing a charger in a unit owner's designated parking space. It can set reasonable rules, but they can't raise the cost or reduce the charger's performance. The owner pays for the electricity, installation and upkeep, hires a licensed contractor, and provides a certificate of insurance within 14 days of installation and every year after that.
  • Renters: a tenant may install a Level 1 or Level 2 charger at their assigned space. The landlord can charge for electricity, any wiring upgrades and a restoration deposit.
  • New homes: new single-family and small multifamily housing needs an EV-capable space for each unit with designated parking. Large new or renovated multifamily buildings must make every space EV-capable.

Parking a non-electric car in a designated EV charging space can bring a fine of up to $100 plus towing costs. Charger installers must be certified by the Illinois Commerce Commission, although you can install a charger yourself for personal use at your own home. IEPA also offers grants covering up to 80% of eligible costs for publicly available Level 2 and DC fast chargers.
