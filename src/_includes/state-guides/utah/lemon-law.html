Utah's New Motor Vehicles Warranties Act

Utah's lemon law is the New Motor Vehicles Warranties Act, Title 13, Chapter 20 of the Utah Code. It makes the manufacturer responsible for fixing a new vehicle that doesn't conform to its express warranties. When repeated repairs fail, the manufacturer must replace the vehicle or buy it back. The Division of Consumer Protection enforces the act, and a consumer can bring a lawsuit too, but only after the division has investigated and evaluated the claim.

Who and what the law covers

Section 13-20-2 defines a "consumer" as an individual who buys, leases, subleases or otherwise acquires a new motor vehicle for any purpose except resale, during the period the act covers. A "manufacturer" includes an importer, a distributor or anyone named as the warrantor on the vehicle's express written warranty.

The act covers these vehicles when sold in Utah:

  • Motor vehicles as defined in the state motor vehicle code, § 41-1a-102
  • Motorcycles designed primarily for paved highways
  • Motor homes, limited to the self-propelled vehicle and chassis
  • Farm tractors, as an exception to the weight limit below

It leaves out:

  • The living, office or commercial space in a motor home
  • Road tractors and truck tractors
  • Mobile homes
  • Vehicles with a gross laden weight over 14,000 pounds, other than motor homes and farm tractors
  • Motorcycles designed primarily for unimproved terrain
  • Electric-assisted bicycles, mopeds and motor-assisted scooters

The definitions section was amended in the 2026 General Session (Chapter 334), so check the current text on the Utah Legislature's site if you're near one of these lines.

Reporting the problem and the repair duty

Under § 13-20-3, the clock that matters is the term of the express warranty or the one year after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, whichever ends earlier. If you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer within that period, they have to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty. That repair duty continues even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period has run out.

Make the report in a way you can prove later. A dated repair order that describes your complaint, or a letter or email to the manufacturer, gives you a record of when the problem first came up.

When a vehicle is presumed to be a lemon

Section 13-20-5 presumes that the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of repair attempts when, within the warranty term or the first year after original delivery, whichever is earlier, either of these happens:

  • The same nonconformity has been repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative total of 30 or more business days.

The section counts business days for the out-of-service test and doesn't add a mileage cap. The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30-day period are all extended for any time repair services weren't available to you because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

A presumption is a starting point. Meeting one of the tests shifts the argument in your favor, and the manufacturer can still raise the defenses described below.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer can't fix a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety after a reasonable number of attempts, § 13-20-4 gives the manufacturer two options:

  • Replace the vehicle with a comparable new motor vehicle.
  • Take the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price, including all collateral charges, minus a reasonable allowance for your use.

The refund goes to you and to any lienholder or lessor according to their interests. Once you receive the refund or replacement, you, your lienholder or your lessor must give the manufacturer clear title to and possession of the vehicle.

The use allowance is limited. It covers the use directly attributable to you before you first reported the nonconformity, plus use during later periods when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. Time the car spent in the shop doesn't count against you.

The manufacturer's defenses

Section 13-20-4(4) gives the manufacturer two affirmative defenses to a claim under the act:

  • The nonconformity doesn't substantially impair your use of the vehicle, its market value or its safety.
  • The nonconformity results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations by the consumer.

Keeping the vehicle maintained on schedule, and keeping receipts for that maintenance, helps you answer a claim of neglect.

Informal dispute settlement first

Under § 13-20-7, if the manufacturer has set up an informal dispute settlement procedure that complies with the Federal Trade Commission's rule at 16 C.F.R. Part 703, the refund and replacement remedies of § 13-20-4 don't apply to you until you've used that procedure first. Your warranty booklet or owner's manual tells you whether the manufacturer has one.

The federal rule sets the ground rules for these programs. Under 16 C.F.R. § 703.5:

  • The program has to tell both sides as soon as it receives a dispute.
  • It has to investigate and share contradictory evidence with both parties so each can respond.
  • It must reach a decision within 40 days of being notified of the dispute, with limited exceptions for delays.
  • Its decision isn't legally binding on anyone, and it can be used as evidence in a later lawsuit.

When the program finishes, it has to tell you that you can still pursue legal remedies, including small claims court, if you're unhappy with the outcome.

Filing with the Division of Consumer Protection

Section 13-20-6 sets the order of steps. The Division of Consumer Protection, part of the Utah Department of Commerce, enforces the act, and a consumer may enforce it too. A consumer can file a lawsuit only after the division has investigated and evaluated the claim.

The division's online complaint form has a path for the New Motor Vehicles Warranties Act. It asks whether the vehicle was bought or leased new, whether the dealer was in Utah, the number of times the same issue was repaired in the first 12 months, whether the vehicle was out of service for 30 or more business days in that time, whether the problem continues, and whether you've used the manufacturer's informal dispute settlement procedure. If the dealer was in another state, the form points you to that state's consumer protection agency. Safety defects can also be reported to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.

The division can be reached at (801) 530-6601 during business hours with questions.

Going to court

In a lawsuit a consumer brings under the act, § 13-20-6(4) lets the court award attorney's fees to the prevailing party. The act generally doesn't make the selling dealer liable or create a claim against the dealer, except for written express warranties the dealer made apart from the manufacturer's own. The act also doesn't limit rights or remedies you have under any other law.

Recreational vehicle trailers

Travel trailers, camping trailers and fifth-wheel trailers have a separate mediation process under § 13-20-8. The owner of a new recreational vehicle trailer and its manufacturer must mediate when:

  • The owner notified the manufacturer of the nonconformity in writing.
  • The problem affects the structural or functional integrity of the roof, subfloor or wall.
  • The manufacturer made at least four attempts to fix it and the problem persists.
  • The owner then asked the manufacturer for mediation in writing.
  • The problem substantially impairs the trailer's use, value or safety.
  • The problem didn't come from business or commercial use, or from abuse, neglect, modification or alteration by anyone other than the manufacturer or its authorized service agent.

The mediation takes place in the county where the owner bought the trailer, is conducted by the Consumer Arbitration Program for Recreation Vehicles, and is paid for by the manufacturer. If it doesn't resolve the problem, the owner keeps every other right and remedy available under the law.

Putting a claim together

Gather your purchase or lease contract, the warranty, and every repair order, including the dates the vehicle went in and came out of the shop. Count repair visits for the same problem and business days out of service, both within the earlier of the warranty term or one year from delivery. Use the manufacturer's dispute program if it has one, then file with the Division of Consumer Protection before you consider a lawsuit.
