Wisconsin's lemon law

Wisconsin's lemon law is Wis. Stat. § 218.0171, titled repair, replacement and refund under new motor vehicle warranties. The Department of Transportation (WisDOT) prescribes the consumer forms, certifies manufacturers' informal dispute programs and publishes an annual report on them. Its rules are in ch. Trans 143 of the Wisconsin Administrative Code.

Who and what is covered

Under § 218.0171(1)(b), a "consumer" is any of the following:

  • The purchaser of a new motor vehicle bought from a dealer for purposes other than resale
  • Someone the vehicle is transferred to, other than for resale, before an express warranty expires
  • Anyone who may enforce the warranty
  • Someone who leases the vehicle from a lessor under a written lease

A "motor vehicle" is any motor-driven vehicle that must be registered under ch. 341, or is exempt under § 341.05(2), and that a consumer buys or accepts transfer of in Wisconsin. That includes untitled demonstrator and executive vehicles, or ones titled by a manufacturer or dealer. Mopeds, semitrailers, and trailers built to be pulled by a truck or truck tractor are excluded. A "heavy-duty vehicle" has a gross vehicle weight rating or actual gross weight over 10,000 pounds, and a few rules apply differently to it.

A "nonconformity" is a condition or defect covered by an express warranty that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety. It doesn't include problems caused by the consumer's abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration. "Collateral costs" are expenses connected with repairing a nonconformity, including alternative transportation.

The duty to repair

Under § 218.0171(2)(a), the nonconformity must be repaired if you report it to the manufacturer, the lessor or an authorized dealer and make the vehicle available for repair. You have to do this before the warranty expires or one year after first delivery to a consumer, whichever is sooner. Mileage isn't part of the test.

What counts as a reasonable attempt to repair

Under § 218.0171(1)(h), either of these, within the warranty term or one year after first delivery (whichever is sooner), is a reasonable attempt to repair:

  • The same nonconformity has been subject to repair at least four times by the manufacturer, lessor or an authorized dealer, and it continues.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for a total of at least 30 days because of warranty nonconformities.

Days when repair services weren't available because of a flood or other natural disaster, war, invasion, fire or strike don't count toward the 30 days. A vehicle is "out of service" while the manufacturer, lessor or a dealer has it for repair. It's also out of service while you have it, if a nonconformity substantially affects its use or safety and has been through at least two repair attempts.

Refund or replacement

If the nonconformity still isn't fixed after a reasonable attempt, give the manufacturer the WisDOT form electing either a refund or a comparable new vehicle. WisDOT must post the forms on its website. Each form asks for your contact information, the selling dealer, the delivery date and purchase price, any lienholder, the mileage when the first nonconformity occurred, your election, and an itemization of other damages. If the form is incomplete, the manufacturer can ask for more information within 30 days, and its deadlines don't start until you provide it.

A purchaser who chooses a refund gets the full purchase price plus sales tax, finance charges, the amount paid at the point of sale and collateral costs. The refund is paid to you and any lienholder, as your interests appear, less a reasonable allowance for use. That allowance can't exceed the purchase price times the miles driven before you first reported the nonconformity to the dealer, divided by 100,000, or 20,000 for a motorcycle.

To get the refund, offer to transfer title to the manufacturer. The manufacturer has 30 days after that offer to pay. Then you return the vehicle with the certificate of title and endorsements.

If you choose a comparable new vehicle, the manufacturer has 30 days after receiving the form to agree in writing to provide one or a full refund. It then has until the 45th day, or the 120th day for a heavy-duty vehicle, to deliver either one. It must use due diligence to find a comparable vehicle, and it can provide the refund instead if none exists or can't be delivered in time.

For leased vehicles, the manufacturer refunds the lessor the current value of the lease and refunds what you paid under the lease, plus sales tax and collateral costs, minus a use allowance based on 100,000 miles. The lease can't be enforced against you after the refund. When it pays a refund, the manufacturer has to give you a written statement of the trade-in amount previously applied and the refund date.

Sales tax refunds

Under § 218.0171(2)(e) and (f), the Department of Revenue refunds the manufacturer any sales tax it refunded to you, if it asks in writing with proof. If the manufacturer refunded the purchase price without the matching sales tax, you can request that sales tax refund directly from the Department of Revenue. The department can't make both refunds for the same vehicle.

Informal dispute settlement

If a manufacturer offers an informal dispute settlement procedure certified by WisDOT, § 218.0171(3) requires you to use it before suing. To be certified under § 218.0171(4), the procedure has to give consumers rights at least as favorable as 16 CFR Part 703, and it has to require the statutory remedy when a reasonable attempt to repair has failed. WisDOT investigates each procedure, may revoke a certification, and publishes its annual evaluation. Each program files its annual 16 CFR Part 703 audit with the department. WisDOT may also consider certification when deciding whether to license a manufacturer.

Going to court

Under § 218.0171(7), you can sue for damages caused by a violation within 36 months after first delivery. If you win, the court awards your pecuniary loss, costs, disbursements and reasonable attorney fees, and may grant equitable relief. If a party fails to reasonably cooperate with another party's efforts to comply, the court can extend deadlines, reduce damages, fees or costs, strike pleadings or enter a default judgment.

The official annotations note some court decisions. In Hughes v. Chrysler Motors Corp. (1996), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that pecuniary loss includes the entire purchase price. In Church v. Chrysler Corp. (1998), the Court of Appeals held that the 30-day refund deadline isn't paused by a disagreement over the amount. Other rulings hold that personal injury damages aren't recoverable under sub. (7), and that a buyer's awareness of defects before delivery doesn't make the law inapplicable.

Waivers, settlements and resale

Any waiver of your rights is void. The section doesn't limit remedies under other law, except for the court's cooperation powers in sub. (7)(b). For heavy-duty vehicles only, a negotiated written settlement releases the manufacturer from the section's other requirements for that vehicle. A vehicle returned under the law, or under a similar law in another state, can't be sold or leased again in Wisconsin unless the reasons for its return are fully disclosed to the buyer or lessee.
