Washington's lemon law

Washington's lemon law is the Motor Vehicle Warranties Act, chapter 19.118 RCW. In RCW 19.118.005, the Legislature says a new vehicle is a major consumer purchase and that a defective one is likely to cause hardship or even injury. It wants consumers to know their rights and not be refused information, documents or service that would get in the way of using them. The chapter combines private and public remedies, and disputes go to new motor vehicle arbitration boards that the Attorney General contracts with.

Who and what is covered

Under RCW 19.118.021, a "consumer" is anyone who has entered into an agreement to buy, lease or take transfer of a new motor vehicle, other than for resale or sublease, during the eligibility period. A subsequent transferee who acquires the vehicle within that period with a manufacturer's written warranty still in effect is also covered.

A "new motor vehicle" is a new self-propelled vehicle, including a new motorcycle, designed mainly to carry people or property on public highways and bought or leased at retail from a new vehicle dealer or leasing company in Washington. Demonstrators and lease-purchase vehicles count if a manufacturer's warranty was issued. The definition excludes:

  • Vehicles bought or leased by a business as part of a fleet of ten or more at one time or under one agreement
  • Trucks with a gross vehicle weight rating of 19,000 pounds or more
  • The parts of a motor home used mainly as a mobile dwelling, office or commercial space

A motorcycle has to have an engine displacement of at least 750 cc. Members of the armed forces stationed or living in Washington are covered even if they bought or leased the vehicle in another state, as long as it otherwise fits the definition.

The eligibility period and warranty coverage

The eligibility period ends two years after the original delivery or after the first 24,000 miles, whichever comes first. If you report a nonconformity during the eligibility period or the manufacturer's written warranty, whichever is less, RCW 19.118.031(3) requires the manufacturer, its agent or the selling dealer to make the necessary repairs, even after the eligibility period ends. For this purpose, the written warranty counts as at least one year or 12,000 miles.

The eligibility period and the out-of-service periods are extended by any time repairs weren't available because of a strike, war, invasion, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

What the dealer and manufacturer must give you

RCW 19.118.031 sets out several duties:

  • At the time of purchase, the dealer gives you a written statement of your rights, prepared by the Attorney General, with a toll-free number for information.
  • Each time the vehicle comes back from warranty diagnosis or repair, the dealer gives you a fully itemized, legible repair order. It shows the problem you reported, the diagnosis, parts and labor, the date and odometer reading when the vehicle came in, and the date it was made available to you.
  • On request, the manufacturer or dealer provides copies of field or zone representative reports on your vehicle and technical service bulletins for its year and model.
  • No one may refuse to diagnose or repair a covered nonconformity to avoid liability under the chapter.

Under RCW 19.118.170, the Department of Licensing makes the vehicle's full title history available to a registered owner who says they're pursuing rights under the chapter.

Nonconformities and serious safety defects

A nonconformity is a defect, serious safety defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety. "Substantially impair" means making the vehicle unreliable or unsafe for ordinary use, or lowering its resale value below the average for comparable vehicles. Problems caused by abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration don't count. A serious safety defect is a life-threatening malfunction or nonconformity that impedes your ability to control or operate the vehicle, or that creates a risk of fire or explosion.

When the presumption applies

For a vehicle other than a motor home, RCW 19.118.041(2) deems a reasonable number of attempts to have been made within the eligibility period if any one of these is true:

  • The same serious safety defect has been diagnosed or repaired two or more times, at least once during the written warranty, and it still exists.
  • The same nonconformity has been diagnosed or repaired four or more times, at least once during the written warranty, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for diagnosis or repair for a cumulative 30 calendar days, at least 15 of them during the written warranty.
  • Within a 12-month period, two or more different serious safety defects have each been diagnosed or repaired at least once, with at least one attempt for each during the written warranty and the eligibility period.

Motor homes follow RCW 19.118.041(3). After one repair attempt on a serious safety defect, three attempts on the same nonconformity, or 30 cumulative days out of service, you send written notice to each motor home manufacturer. They then have 15 days to tell you where the vehicle will be repaired and 30 days after you deliver it to complete a final attempt. They pay to transport it if it's unsafe to drive or the facility is more than 100 miles away. The out-of-service threshold for a motor home is 60 days.

Replacement or repurchase

If the vehicle can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must replace or repurchase it, at your option, within 40 calendar days of your written request to its corporate, dispute resolution, zone or regional office.

A replacement must be identical or reasonably equivalent to your vehicle as originally bought or leased, including service contracts, undercoating, rustproofing and installed options. The manufacturer pays the sales tax, license and registration fees and refunds incidental costs.

A repurchase refunds the purchase price, all collateral charges and incidental costs. Collateral charges include sales and use tax, arbitration fees, unused license, registration and title fees, finance charges, and charges for service contracts and options. Incidental costs include towing and alternative transportation. In a lease, you get back your lease payments, trade-in or inception payment, security deposit, and collateral and incidental costs, and the manufacturer pays the lessor or lienholder to clear title.

Either way, there's an offset for use. It's the miles you drove before the first attempt to fix the nonconformity, times the purchase price, divided by 120,000. The divisor is 90,000 for a motor home and 25,000 for a motorcycle.

Under RCW 19.118.041(4), dealers aren't liable to the manufacturer for refunds or replacements, and you can't bring a claim against a dealer under this chapter, though other laws still apply.

Arbitration through the Attorney General

You request arbitration by submitting a request to the Attorney General under RCW 19.118.090. Within ten days, the office reviews it and accepts it unless it's clearly ineligible, for example incomplete, untimely, frivolous or filed in bad faith. Manufacturers must arbitrate if you request it within 30 months of the original retail delivery and the Attorney General accepts the dispute. If the manufacturer has an informal dispute program that substantially complies with 16 CFR Part 703, RCW 19.118.150 lets you choose to use it first. Under RCW 19.118.080, you have to go through the board or that informal procedure before filing in superior court.

Once your case is assigned, the board has 45 days to hold a hearing and 60 days to submit a decision. The manufacturer must send its written response, with all issues and affirmative defenses, within ten days of notice. At the hearing, both sides can testify, present evidence and witnesses, cross-examine and have a lawyer.

If the board finds a reasonable number of attempts, it awards the remedies, plus your reasonable costs and attorney's fees when the manufacturer used counsel. You have 60 days to accept or reject the decision in writing, and silence counts as rejection. After you accept, the manufacturer has 40 days to comply or 30 days to appeal. If neither happens, the Attorney General may fine it up to $1,000 a day, to a maximum of $100,000.

Trial de novo

Either side can request a new trial in superior court under RCW 19.118.100. If you reject the decision, you have 120 days from rejection to file. If you win in court, you recover the award, attorney's fees and costs. You can also get $25 a day for each day after the 40-day period that the manufacturer didn't comply with a board award and didn't give you a comparable loaner. If the appealing party acted without good cause or to harass, the court must at least double the total award and may triple it.

A violation of the chapter is an unfair or deceptive act under the Consumer Protection Act, chapter 19.86 RCW. Waivers of your rights are void.

Returned vehicles

Under RCW 19.118.061, a manufacturer can't resell a vehicle found to have a serious safety defect unless the defect is fixed and warranted. Before resale, a returned vehicle gets a title brand from the Department of Licensing, a window disclosure notice from the Attorney General, and a signed disclosure form identifying the nonconformity. A $3 arbitration fee collected at each new-vehicle sale or lease funds the program under RCW 19.118.110.
