How Montana's lemon law works

Montana's new motor vehicle warranty law is Title 61, chapter 4, part 5 of the Montana Code Annotated. When a manufacturer can't repair a defect covered by its express warranty after a reasonable number of tries, the law requires it to replace the vehicle or buy it back. The state Department of Justice also runs an arbitration program, so a buyer can bring a claim to a state arbitrator instead of going straight to court. Below is a summary of what each section says.

Who and what is covered

Under § 61-4-501, a "consumer" is someone who buys or leases a passenger motor vehicle for personal, family or household use, other than for resale or re-lease. The definition also covers anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while an express warranty still applies, and anyone else the warranty's terms allow to enforce it. A vehicle damaged by abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes falls outside the definition.

A "motor vehicle" is self-propelled, built mainly to carry people or property on public highways, and sold or registered in Montana. The definition includes the non-living portion of a motor home. It excludes trucks rated at 15,000 pounds gross vehicle weight or more, along with the parts of a motor home designed, used and maintained mainly for living in, such as appliances, fixtures and furnishings.

The warranty period: two years or 18,000 miles

For this law, the warranty period ends two years after the new vehicle is first delivered to the consumer or after its first 18,000 miles, whichever comes first. A manufacturer's own warranty may run longer. The lemon law clock, though, follows the statute.

Section 61-4-502 protects buyers whose problem straddles that deadline. If you tell the manufacturer or its agent in writing, during the warranty period, that the vehicle doesn't conform to its express warranty, the warrantor has to make or pay for the repairs even after the period ends. The period is also extended for any time repair services weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The presumption: four repairs or 30 business days

Section 61-4-504 says a reasonable number of repair attempts is presumed in either of two cases during the warranty period:

  • The same nonconformity was repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer, and the problem continues.
  • The vehicle was out of service for nonconformities for a cumulative 30 or more business days after the manufacturer, agent or dealer was notified.

The defect has to substantially impair the vehicle's use and market value or its safety. Under § 61-4-506, a manufacturer can defend a claim by showing the problem doesn't substantially impair use, market value or safety, or that it came from the consumer's abuse, neglect or unauthorized modification.

Written notice comes first

Written notice is central to Montana's process. Section 61-4-502 says the presumption may not apply against a manufacturer that hasn't received prior written notice from or for the consumer and had a chance to cure the defect. In turn, manufacturers have to state clearly and conspicuously in the warranty or owner's manual that written notice is required before you can qualify for a refund or replacement. They also have to give the name and address where the notice should go. Send it by a method you can prove, and keep copies of every repair order.

Replacement or refund

Under § 61-4-503, the manufacturer must replace the vehicle with a new one of the same model and style and equal value. If that model isn't available, a vehicle of comparable market value can be substituted. As an alternative, the manufacturer may take the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price, plus reasonable collateral charges and incidental damages, minus a reasonable allowance for use. Collateral charges include sales tax, property tax, license and registration fees, and fees in lieu of tax. The refund is split between the consumer and any lienholder according to their interests.

The use allowance is a formula: the total contract price multiplied by the miles driven before the manufacturer accepted the vehicle's return, divided by 100,000. Miles counted are those driven before the first written notice of the defect, plus any later miles while the vehicle wasn't out of service for the defect. On a $40,000 contract with 9,000 qualifying miles, the deduction would be $3,600.

Dealers aren't liable under the law. Section 61-4-505 says it creates no consumer claim against a dealer under § 61-4-503, and a dealer doesn't owe the manufacturer for refunds unless its repairs didn't follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Manufacturer programs and state arbitration

A manufacturer may run an informal dispute settlement procedure under the federal rules in 16 C.F.R. part 703. It has to submit that procedure to the Department of Justice for certification under § 61-4-511. A certified program must let the consumer present evidence in Montana at a reasonably convenient location. It can't delay a decision past 60 days or delay performance past 10 days after the decision, though a replacement or refund may take 30 days. It also can't require more than one inspection of the vehicle. Under § 61-4-507, if the manufacturer has a certified program, you must use it first before you can claim a replacement or refund.

The Department's own arbitration under § 61-4-515 is open when the manufacturer doesn't have a certified program. Hearings are held in Montana, at a place reasonably convenient to the consumer. Section 61-4-520 also lets a consumer request a fresh hearing before a department arbitrator after a manufacturer process that didn't follow its certified procedure and the federal rules. The manufacturer's findings can be used as evidence.

  • Filing: you file a notice with the Department, then return its form and a $100 filing fee within 5 days of receiving the form. You choose to give testimony either orally or in writing. If the complaint doesn't allege a warranty violation, the fee is refunded.
  • Manufacturer's share: the manufacturer returns its own form within 15 days with a $750 fee.
  • Investigation: the Department gathers the information and can subpoena witnesses and records. At the arbitrator's request, it can send the file to a certified independent technical expert.
  • Decision: the arbitrator must decide within 60 days after the Department accepts the complaint. Remedies can include repair, replacement, a refund, remedies under the warranty or the federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, and reimbursement of costs. The decision sets a performance date, and the Department follows up within 10 working days after it.

A party that doesn't accept the decision follows the state's Uniform Arbitration Act in Title 27, chapter 5. If a court finds an appeal was brought without good cause, it may award the other side costs and reasonable attorney fees.

Resale, records and penalties

Under § 61-4-525, a returned vehicle that needed replacement or refund can't be resold in Montana without a clear written disclosure that it was returned. The Department keeps an index of disputes by brand and model. At least every 6 months it compiles statistics on compliance with decisions and the number of refunds and replacements, and it considers them when licensing manufacturers (§ 61-4-526). A violation of the law counts as an unfair or deceptive trade practice under § 61-4-533. The law also doesn't limit your rights under other laws, including Montana's Uniform Commercial Code warranty rules.
