Texas's financial responsibility law

Chapter 601 of the Texas Transportation Code is the Motor Vehicle Safety Responsibility Act. Section 601.051 says you may not operate a motor vehicle in Texas unless financial responsibility is established for it. You can meet that requirement with a liability insurance policy, a surety bond, a deposit of cash or securities with the comptroller or a county judge, or self-insurance. The sections below cover the liability policy route.

Section 601.052 exempts a few vehicles, including collector vehicles at least 25 years old (or former military vehicles) used only for exhibitions, club activities and parades, when the owner files an affidavit with the Department of Public Safety. Neighborhood electric vehicles, golf carts and off-highway vehicles are also exempt while operated only where state law allows them.

Minimum liability limits

Since January 1, 2011, § 601.072 has required at least:

  • $30,000 for bodily injury to or death of one person in one collision
  • $60,000 for bodily injury to or death of two or more people in one collision
  • $25,000 for damage to or destruction of other people's property in one collision

A policy may exclude the first $250 of liability for injury to one person, the first $500 for injury to two or more people, and the first $250 for property damage. The law also directs the Texas Department of Insurance to run an outreach program in English and Spanish explaining these requirements.

Uninsured motorist coverage and PIP

Under Texas Insurance Code § 1952.101, an insurer can't issue an auto liability policy in Texas without including uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, in at least the Chapter 601 limits. The coverage protects you when an uninsured or underinsured driver owes you damages for bodily injury, death or property damage. If the at-fault driver is unknown, § 1952.104 requires actual physical contact between that driver's vehicle and you or your property before the coverage pays.

Section 1952.152 also requires personal injury protection (PIP) in every policy. Insurers don't have to provide more than $2,500 per person in PIP. Under § 1952.151, PIP pays reasonable expenses incurred within three years of the crash for:

  • necessary medical, surgical, X-ray, dental, ambulance, hospital, nursing and funeral services
  • lost income, for someone who was earning income
  • essential household services, for someone who wasn't

PIP covers the named insured, household members, and authorized drivers and passengers of the insured's vehicle. It pays regardless of who caused the collision and regardless of other medical or wage benefits you have. The insurer must pay within 30 days of receiving satisfactory proof of a claim. If it doesn't, you can sue and recover reasonable attorney's fees, a 12% penalty and interest.

Any named insured can reject either coverage in writing. A rejection carries over to renewals and reinstated policies with the same insurer or an affiliate unless you later ask for the coverage in writing.

Proof of insurance

When a peace officer or someone involved in a collision with you asks, § 601.053 requires you to show evidence of financial responsibility. That can be the policy or a photocopy, the standard proof of insurance card prescribed by the Texas Department of Insurance, an image of that card on your phone, or an insurance binder. Showing proof on a phone doesn't give an officer consent to look at anything else on the device.

If you can't show proof, the law presumes you were driving uninsured. That presumption doesn't apply if the officer confirms your coverage through the financial responsibility verification program set up under Subchapter N. An officer can't write a citation for driving without insurance without first trying to verify coverage through the program.

The county tax assessor-collector also won't register a vehicle without evidence of financial responsibility, under § 502.046. Under § 502.357, a $1 fee collected at each registration and renewal goes partly to the TexasSure Fund, which covers the amounts appropriated to the Texas Department of Insurance from it, with the rest going to the state highway fund.

Penalties

Under § 601.191, a first conviction for driving without financial responsibility is a misdemeanor with a fine of $175 to $350. The court can lower the fine below $175 for a first offender who can't afford it. A later conviction carries a fine of $350 to $1,000.

On a second or later conviction, § 601.231 requires DPS to suspend your driver's license unless you file and keep proof of financial responsibility with the department until the second anniversary of that conviction. Section 601.261 also requires the court to order the sheriff to impound the vehicle you were driving if you owned it at the time of the offense and still own it on the date of conviction.

If you had valid coverage but couldn't show it, § 601.193 makes that a defense. Bring the document to court, and once the court verifies it, the charge must be dismissed.

Premiums and uninsured drivers

The NAIC's 2024 Average Premium Supplement, Table 5, lists a combined average of $1,917.05 in Texas for liability, collision and comprehensive. The Insurance Research Council's estimate puts Texas's share of uninsured drivers at 14.5%, 19th in the nation. Because uninsured motorist coverage and PIP are both included unless you reject them, review the rejection forms carefully before you sign.
