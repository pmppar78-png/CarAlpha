Indiana's Motor Vehicle Protection Act

Indiana's lemon law is Indiana Code chapter 24-5-13, which the Attorney General calls the Motor Vehicle Protection Act. It applies to vehicles sold, leased, transferred or replaced by a dealer or manufacturer in Indiana. If a covered vehicle has a defect that the manufacturer or its dealers can't fix after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must take the vehicle back. Then, at your choice, it must refund what you paid or give you a replacement of comparable value within 30 days.

The Attorney General's lemon law flyer boils eligibility down to four questions. Did you buy or lease a car or light truck for personal use, from an Indiana dealer, within 18 months of the date it first left the dealership, and with less than 18,000 miles?

Vehicles and buyers covered

Under § 24-5-13-5, a covered vehicle is self-propelled, has a declared gross vehicle weight under 10,000 pounds, is meant mainly for public highways, and must be registered before use. It must be sold to a buyer in Indiana and registered here, or sold in Indiana to a nonresident. These vehicles are excluded:

  • Conversion vans and motor homes
  • Farm tractors and other farm production machinery
  • Road-building equipment, truck tractors and road tractors
  • Motorcycles, motor-driven cycles and snowmobiles
  • Vehicles designed mainly for off-road use

A "buyer" is anyone who contracts in Indiana to buy, lease or take transfer of a covered vehicle for any purpose other than resale or sublease. A lease must run longer than four months to count.

A "nonconformity" is any defect or condition, or combination of them, that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety, or that makes it fail to meet the manufacturer's warranty. Indiana also counts any vehicle in which methamphetamine was made within the past two years as having a nonconformity. Dealers and sellers have to disclose that history in writing before the sale.

The term of protection

Indiana's window, called the "term of protection" in § 24-5-13-7, starts on the date of original delivery and ends after 18 months or 18,000 miles, whichever comes first. If the manufacturer gives you a replacement vehicle under the law, a new term starts when the replacement is delivered.

What matters is when you report the defect. Under § 24-5-13-8, if you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer within the term, they must make the needed repairs, even if the repairs happen after the term ends.

When enough attempts have been made

Section 24-5-13-15 treats a reasonable number of attempts as having been made in either of these situations:

  • The nonconformity has been subject to repair at least four times by the manufacturer, its agents or its authorized dealers, and it still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair of any nonconformity for a total of at least 30 business days, and the nonconformity still exists

A "business day" is any day except Sunday or a legal holiday, so Saturdays count. The 30-day count is extended for time when repairs aren't available as a direct result of a strike, civil unrest, fire, natural disaster, terrorist attack, act of God or act of war. During such an event, the manufacturer or dealer must provide a free vehicle while yours is in for repair. The manufacturer has the burden of proving that the event directly caused its failure to make the repair.

Dealers can't refuse to diagnose or repair a vehicle to avoid lemon-law liability. Under § 24-5-13-16, they must give you a written repair order every time you bring the vehicle in, listing all the work done, including examinations, parts and labor. The Attorney General recommends asking for a copy every time, because those orders are your evidence.

Notifying the manufacturer

Under § 24-5-13-9, you must first notify the manufacturer of your claim if the manufacturer has made the required disclosure. The disclosure must state clearly in the warranty or owner's manual that written notice of the defect is required before you can get a refund or replacement, and it must give the name and address to send it to. If the manufacturer didn't make that disclosure, you don't have to notify it. The Attorney General suggests including copies of all your repair orders with the notice.

Certified dispute programs

Section 24-5-13-19 says the chapter doesn't apply to a buyer who hasn't first used the manufacturer's informal dispute procedure, if two conditions are met. The Attorney General must have certified that the procedure complies with the federal rules in 16 CFR part 703 and with the state's certification rules, including oral hearings. You also must have received adequate written notice of the procedure, which can be done by putting it in the written warranty.

The Attorney General's summary: if the manufacturer has a certified program, you must use it before suing. If notice isn't required and there's no program, you can go straight to court without notifying the manufacturer.

How a refund is calculated

For a purchased vehicle, § 24-5-13-11 sets the refund at the full contract price, including credit for any trade-in, minus a use allowance. The allowance is the contract price multiplied by the miles driven before the manufacturer accepted the return, divided by 100,000. On a $40,000 vehicle returned at 12,000 miles, the deduction would be $4,800. The manufacturer must also reimburse:

  • All sales tax
  • The unused part of any prepaid registration fee and excise tax
  • Finance charges actually paid
  • The cost of options the dealer added
  • Necessary towing and rental costs caused by the defect

The refund is paid to you and any lienholder according to the title records. You can keep driving the returned vehicle until the refund or replacement is offered, but any extra miles count toward the use allowance.

For a leased vehicle, § 24-5-13-11.5 gives you back your deposit and lease payments, including trade-in credit, minus a use allowance based on your total lease obligation and miles divided by 100,000. The lessor receives its purchase cost, fees, sales tax and 5% of the purchase cost, less what you paid.

If you choose a replacement, the manufacturer must cover the registration transfer fees and sales tax that result. If the manufacturer or its finance arm financed the original vehicle, it can't require you to sign refinancing terms that are less favorable than the original loan.

Manufacturer defenses

A manufacturer can defend a claim under § 24-5-13-18 by showing that the defect doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use, value or safety, or that it was caused by your abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration.

Lawsuits and deadlines

You can sue in any circuit or superior court. Under § 24-5-13-23, a lemon-law suit must be filed within two years of the date you first reported the defect. That clock pauses while a certified dispute procedure is underway. A buyer who wins recovers court costs and attorney's fees based on the attorney's actual time. The law doesn't limit your rights under other laws, and it doesn't make the dealer liable, since the claim is against the manufacturer.

Branded titles for buybacks

The Attorney General notes that a manufacturer must get a new title branded "Manufacturer Buyback-Disclosure on File" for every vehicle it replaces or repurchases under the lemon law, and the brand stays with the vehicle permanently. The first time a dealer resells one, it must give the buyer written notice of the buyback and a 12-month or 12,000-mile manufacturer's warranty.

The Attorney General's role

The Consumer Protection Division can take enforcement action over buyback disclosure and title branding violations. However, the lemon law doesn't let the Attorney General represent you in seeking a refund or replacement. The division is at 302 W. Washington St., 5th Floor, Indianapolis, IN 46204, and can be reached at (800) 382-5516.
