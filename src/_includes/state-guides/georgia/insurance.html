What Georgia law makes you carry

25/50/25: $25,000 bodily injury per person, $50,000 bodily injury per incident, and $25,000 property damage per incident.

The Office of the Commissioner of Insurance and Safety Fire (OCI) lists those three numbers as the floor for driving on Georgia's public roads. Liability coverage pays other people on your behalf when you injure them or damage their property. It does nothing for your own car or your own medical bills, and you're free to buy higher limits than the minimum.

Georgia uses a tort (at-fault) system: the driver who causes a crash, and that driver's insurer, pay for the damage. If the bills from a serious crash go beyond your limits, the injured person can come after you personally for the difference. A $50,000 per-incident cap shared among several injured people doesn't stretch far once hospital stays are involved.

Uninsured motorist coverage in Georgia

OCI notes that some drivers ignore the law or let their coverage lapse without realizing it, and those are the people uninsured motorist (UM) coverage is meant for. Under O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11, a liability policy issued on a car garaged in Georgia must include UM coverage unless you reject it in writing. The limits have to be at least 25/50/25, or match your own liability limits if those are higher, though you can choose lower UM limits in writing.

Georgia UM comes in two forms, and the difference matters after a crash with a driver who carries only the minimum:

  • Added on. Your full UM limit is available on top of whatever the at-fault driver's liability policy pays. This is the default unless you choose otherwise.
  • Reduced by. Your UM limit is reduced by the amount the at-fault driver's insurer pays. It usually costs less and pays less.

If you see a "reduced by" selection or a UM rejection form at signing, read it before you sign it, since the choice usually stays with the policy at renewal.

Coverage for your own car

Georgia doesn't require physical damage coverage. OCI describes two kinds. Comprehensive, sometimes called "other than collision," handles theft, vandalism and fire. Collision pays for damage when your car hits another vehicle or an object. If you financed or leased the car, your lender or leasing company will usually make you carry both. OCI also points out that you can claim under your own physical damage coverage even when someone else may have caused the accident, which can get your car fixed sooner while fault is sorted out.

How Georgia checks that you're insured

The Department of Revenue (DOR) requires owners and lessees to keep continuous Georgia liability coverage on every registered vehicle. Insurers send policy information to DOR electronically, and that electronic record is what counts as proof when you register, renew, transfer or replace a plate. A paper card alone isn't accepted at the tag office for most vehicles. You still have to keep an insurance card in the car, because you may need it when driving in other states or trading information after a crash.

Fleet, self-insured and IRP-registered vehicles are the exceptions, and their cards or certificates must stay in the vehicle. If you've just bought a car, DOR accepts a bill of sale dated within 30 days of purchase together with a binder page from a Georgia-licensed insurer. You can confirm DOR has your coverage on file with the status lookup in DRIVES e-Services.

Lapses, fines and suspension

DOR counts it as a lapse when 10 or more days pass between the end of one policy and the start of the next, or when a policy ends and no new coverage reaches DOR within 30 days. Insurers have 30 days to report a new policy, so switching companies with no gap between the end date and the new start date doesn't trigger anything.

When a lapse does happen, the posted fees and fines schedule works like this:

  • The lapse fine is $25 for any lapse while the vehicle is actively registered.
  • If the fine goes unpaid for 30 days, the registration is suspended.
  • Reinstating a suspended registration costs $60 for a first or second offense and $160 when there have been more than two offenses within five years.

While a registration is suspended, the county won't issue, transfer, replace or renew a plate, and the car can't be driven. DOR says that driving on a suspended, revoked or cancelled registration is a criminal offense that brings additional penalties. Lapse and reinstatement fines are paid at the County Tag Office where you live.

The simplest way to avoid all of this when you sell a car, it's totaled or repossessed, or it's parked for good is to cancel the registration before dropping the insurance. That way DOR's records never show a registered vehicle with no coverage.

What Georgians pay

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners' most recent auto insurance database report puts Georgia's 2024 average combined premium (liability, collision and comprehensive) at $1,972.56. The average expenditure, which reflects what drivers actually bought, was $1,757.98. In 2020 the combined average was $1,426.38, so the cost of full coverage has climbed by more than $500 in four years.

The Insurance Research Council estimated that 19.0% of Georgia drivers were uninsured, ranking the state 11th highest in the country. With nearly one driver in five uninsured, UM coverage is worth pricing carefully.

OCI lists the usual rating factors: driving record, where you live, age and gender, marital status, prior insurance, how you use the car, and its make and model. Discounts to ask about include multiple vehicles, driver education, good student, safety and anti-theft devices, low mileage, good driver or renewal credit, and bundling auto with home. When you compare quotes, give every company identical information so the prices actually line up.

Checking a company and filing a complaint

Before you sign an application, OCI recommends confirming that both the company and the agent are licensed in Georgia. If you have a problem with an insurer or agent, or with how a claim is being handled, OCI's Consumer Services Division takes consumer complaints online.
