Colorado's lemon law after the 2024 rewrite

Colorado's lemon law is Article 10 of Title 42, "Motor Vehicle Warranties" (C.R.S. §§ 42-10-101 to 42-10-110). It was overhauled by Senate Bill 24-192. The changes apply to vehicles sold or leased on or after August 7, 2024. If you bought before then, the older and stricter rules still apply. Those gave you only the warranty term or one year, required four repairs, and allowed 30 business days out of service. This guide covers the current law.

What vehicles and buyers are covered

Under § 42-10-101, the law protects a "consumer." That's the buyer of a vehicle used for personal, family, or household purposes, anyone the car is transferred to for those purposes while the factory warranty lasts, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty. A covered "motor vehicle" is a private passenger vehicle sold in Colorado, including pickups and vans, built mainly for public roads and for carrying no more than ten people. The law excludes:

  • motor homes
  • vehicles with three or fewer wheels on the ground, so motorcycles and three-wheelers are out
  • vehicles modified for commercial use.

The law doesn't cover used cars (§ 42-10-110), except for the buyback disclosure rules described below. Licensed used-car dealers are outside it too. Business-use vehicles don't qualify, because the definition of consumer is limited to personal, family, or household use.

The repair window

If you report a defect to the manufacturer, its agent, or a dealer within the first 24,000 miles or two years after delivery, whichever comes first, the manufacturer has to make the repairs needed to bring the car into line with the warranty. That holds even if the repairs happen after the window closes (§ 42-10-102). This applies to both ordinary warranty defects and safety-based nonconformities, which the law defines as problems likely to cause death or serious injury if the car is driven, or that create a risk of fire or explosion.

When it's presumed to be a lemon

Under § 42-10-103(2), the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of repair attempts if, within 24,000 miles or two years:

  • the same defect has been repaired three or more times and is still there
  • a safety-based defect has been repaired two or more times and is still there
  • the car has been out of service for repairs for a total of 24 or more of the repairer's business days.

Those time periods are extended for any stretch when repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, a fire, a flood, or another natural disaster.

The defect has to substantially impair the car's use and market value, or its safety. The manufacturer has two affirmative defenses (§ 42-10-104). It can argue the defect doesn't cause that level of impairment, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes by the owner.

The certified-mail notice is required

This is the step that sinks the most Colorado claims. The presumption doesn't apply until the manufacturer has received written notice by certified mail from you or on your behalf. The notice has to say the defect has been through one or more repair attempts and is still there. The manufacturer then gets ten business days after receiving it to try a final fix. That attempt counts as one of your repairs toward the three-repair or two-repair totals.

Dealers have to put a form in every owner's manual, with the manufacturer's name and business address, that you can use for this notice. The form also has to state clearly that certified-mail notice is required to get a remedy. Use it, or send a letter to the same address. Keep the certified-mail receipt and the green return card.

Refund or replacement

If the defect can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer chooses whether to replace the car with a comparable vehicle or take it back. A refund covers the full purchase price, including sales tax, license and registration fees, and similar government charges, minus a reasonable allowance for your use. The refund is split between you and any lienholder according to your interests.

SB 24-192 replaced the old open-ended "reasonable allowance" with a formula, § 42-10-103(4). Take the total contract price, or the lessee's cost, and multiply it by a fraction with 100,000 as the denominator. The numerator is two numbers added together: the miles driven before you first brought the car in for the defect, plus any miles driven later while the car wasn't in the shop for it. For example, take a $45,000 car with 3,000 miles before the first repair visit and 2,000 more between visits. The offset would be $45,000 × 5,000 ÷ 100,000, or $2,250.

Manufacturer dispute programs

Colorado has no state-run arbitration board. If the manufacturer has set up or joined an informal dispute settlement program that substantially complies with the FTC's 16 CFR Part 703 rules, you have to use it before you can claim a refund or replacement (§ 42-10-106). Your warranty booklet will tell you whether such a program exists. Time spent in that program doesn't count against your deadline to sue.

Deadline to sue and attorney fees

A lawsuit under the lemon law has to be filed within 30 months of the original delivery (§ 42-10-107). The 30 months is paused while you're in the manufacturer's arbitration program and while the car is out of service for repairs. Under § 42-10-103(3), the court awards reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing side. That cuts both ways: if you lose, you could be ordered to pay the manufacturer's fees. Talk to a lawyer about the strength of your case before you file.

Buying a used car that was a lemon

The 2024 law added protections for later buyers, and these apply to used cars too:

  • Before reselling a returned car in Colorado, the manufacturer or dealer has to notify the Department of Revenue and get a "Lemon Law Buyback" branded title. It also has to put a "Lemon Law Buyback" decal on the driver's door pillar (the B-pillar), and no seller may remove it (§ 42-10-109).
  • Any seller who knows, or should know, that a car is a buyback has to disclose it clearly before the sale.
  • A dealer selling a buyback to a retail customer has to either allow an inspection by a qualified third party you choose, or give you a seven-day free look. During that week you can return the car for a refund of everything you paid, minus shipping. The dealer has to tell you about this right in writing before you buy (§ 42-10-108).

Failing to follow the act is also grounds for discipline against a manufacturer or distributor's license.

Where to get help

Colorado Attorney General, Consumer Protection: Ralph L. Carr Colorado Judicial Center, 1300 Broadway, 9th Floor, Denver, CO 80203; 720-508-6000. File complaints at coag.gov/file-complaint. The AG's Stop Fraud Colorado site has consumer resources, but lemon-law claims are private claims.
