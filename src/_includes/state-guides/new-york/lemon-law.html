New York's New Car Lemon Law

New York's new car lemon law is General Business Law § 198-a. It adds a statutory warranty on top of the manufacturer's: a new vehicle bought and registered in the state is covered against material defects for the first 18,000 miles or two years after the original delivery, whichever comes first. A separate law, § 198-b, covers used cars bought from dealers. The full text of § 198-a is on the New York State Senate site.

Who and what is covered

Under § 198-a(a)(1), a "consumer" is a buyer, lessee or transferee who didn't acquire the vehicle for resale and who uses it mainly for personal, family or household purposes. Anyone else the warranty lets enforce it also qualifies. Leases are covered when the written lease makes the lessee responsible for repairs. The definition of "motor vehicle" excludes off-road vehicles and covers a vehicle that had a manufacturer's express warranty at original delivery and was either bought, leased or transferred in New York within the first 18,000 miles or two years, or is registered in New York. A later owner who buys the car during that period inherits the rights, according to § 198-a(i).

Reporting a defect

Report the problem to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer within the 18,000-mile or two-year period. A dealer or agent that receives the report has seven days to send written notice to the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested, saying whether repairs have started. The repair has to be done at no charge, even if the work happens after the period ends, as long as the report came in time.

If the dealer refuses to start repairs within seven days of your report, § 198-a(b)(2) lets you send written notice of the refusal straight to the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested. The manufacturer then has 20 days from receipt to begin repairs. If it doesn't, you can choose a refund or a comparable replacement without waiting for more repair attempts.

When the presumption applies

Section 198-a(d) presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the first 18,000 miles or two years, whichever is earlier:

  • The same nonconformity, defect or condition has been subject to repair four or more times and still exists, or
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a cumulative 30 or more calendar days.

The warranty term, the two-year period and the 30-day count are all extended for any time repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

Refund or replacement

If the defect substantially impairs the vehicle's value to you and can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, § 198-a(c) gives you the choice between a comparable replacement vehicle and a refund. A refund covers the full purchase price, or the lease price, plus any trade-in allowance and fees and charges such as license and registration fees and similar government charges.

The deduction for use only starts after 12,000 miles. The mileage deduction formula in § 198-a(a)(4) multiplies the miles over 12,000 by the purchase price and divides the result by 100,000. The manufacturer can also deduct a reasonable amount for damage beyond normal wear. You can return the vehicle to the dealer that sold it or to the one that tried to repair it, with no extra shipping charges. Refunds have to come with the form for reclaiming state and local sales tax from the Department of Taxation and Finance.

For leased vehicles, the lessee gets back the capitalized cost, meaning the deposit and rental payments made minus service fees. The lessor receives the lease price less those payments. The lease ends on the date of the arbitrator's decision, with no early termination penalty.

Manufacturer defenses

A manufacturer can defend a claim by showing that the defect doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's value, or that it came from abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Arbitration options

If the manufacturer runs an informal dispute program, you have to use it before claiming a refund or replacement under § 198-a(c). The program must meet the Federal Trade Commission's rules, and an arbitrator who awards a refund or replacement can't reduce it below what the statute allows.

Under § 198-a(k), you can instead take the dispute to the Attorney General's alternate arbitration program by paying the filing fee, and the manufacturer has to take part. A professional arbitrator appointed under the Attorney General's regulations hears the case, and the New York State Dispute Resolution Association administers it. NYSDRA lists a $250 filing fee for new cars and motor homes, refunded if the arbitrator rules in your favor. Under 13 NYCRR 300.4, you pick refund or replacement on the request form and can ask for a hearing on documents only.

Once you accept an arbitrator's decision, the manufacturer has 30 days to comply. After that it owes $25 for each business day of delay, up to $500, unless you caused the delay, for example by ordering a replacement with options that aren't comparable.

Going to court

A lawsuit under § 198-a has to be filed within four years of the original delivery. A court may award reasonable attorney's fees to a consumer who wins, including in a proceeding that arises from an Attorney General arbitration, and to a consumer who has to hire a lawyer to collect an award. Using a manufacturer's program doesn't stop you from seeking other remedies, and § 198-a(f) keeps every right available under other laws.

Motor homes

Section 198-a(n) covers motor homes but not their living quarters, such as plumbing, the furnace, the generator, the roof air conditioner and the side entrance door. If the same defect has been repaired twice, or the motor home has been out of service for 21 days, you must notify the manufacturer or dealer by certified mail. You can then proceed after three repair attempts or 30 days out of service. That extra notice step applies only if the manufacturer or dealer gave you the rule in writing and you acknowledged receiving it.

The Bill of Rights notice

At the time of purchase or lease from an authorized dealer, the dealer must give you a "New Car Lemon Law Bill of Rights" printed in bold type of at least eight points. It sums up the 18,000-mile or two-year coverage, the four-attempt and 30-day tests, the 20-day certified-mail rule, the arbitration choices and the rule that no contract can waive these rights. Any agreement that waives the rights, or charges you extra for them, is void under § 198-a(i).

Returned vehicles

A manufacturer that takes back a vehicle must notify the Department of Motor Vehicles commissioner. Before reselling it, wholesale or retail, it must disclose under Vehicle and Traffic Law § 417-a that the vehicle was returned for not conforming to its warranty.
