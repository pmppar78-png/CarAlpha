Delaware's lemon law at a glance

Delaware's lemon law is Chapter 50 of Title 6 of the Delaware Code, "Automobile Warranties" (6 Del. C. §§ 5001–5009). It's short, and its time limit is tight. Your protection lasts only for the warranty term or one year after delivery, whichever ends first. There's no mileage cutoff, but a year passes quickly. If your new car keeps going back to the shop, act early. The Attorney General's Consumer Protection Unit (CPU) enforces the law, and violating it is also an unlawful practice under the state Consumer Fraud Act, 6 Del. C. § 2513.

Which cars and buyers qualify

An "automobile" under § 5001 is any passenger motor vehicle bought or leased in Delaware, or registered by the Delaware DMV. So a car bought across the line in Maryland or Pennsylvania can still be covered once it's registered here. The living quarters of a motor home are excluded. The law applies only to manufacturers that normally distribute at least 10 new cars a year to dealers, and it covers new vehicles only. The DMV and CPU both stress that it doesn't cover used cars.

A protected "consumer" is:

  • anyone who buys the car for a purpose other than resale
  • anyone the car is transferred to while the express warranty is still in effect
  • anyone entitled to enforce the warranty under its terms.

A "nonconformity" is a defect or condition that substantially impairs the car's use, value, or safety.

The duty to repair

If you report a warranty defect to the manufacturer, its agent, or a dealer during the warranty term or within one year of delivery, whichever comes first, the manufacturer must make or arrange all needed repairs within a reasonable time. That's true even if the repairs happen after the period ends (§ 5002). Reporting the problem inside the window is what preserves your rights, so make sure every visit produces a written repair order.

When the law presumes a lemon

Under § 5004, the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if, during that same warranty-or-one-year period:

  • substantially the same defect has been repaired four or more times and is still there
  • the car has been out of service for repair of defects for more than 30 calendar days in total since delivery.

Delaware has a specific rule about the 30-day count. It starts only on the first day you bring the car in and the manufacturer, agent, or dealer writes up a document describing the defect. A verbal complaint at the service desk doesn't start the clock. The 30 days can be extended only when repairs are impossible for reasons outside the manufacturer's control, such as war, invasion, a strike, a fire, a flood, or another natural disaster.

Unlike many states, Delaware has no separate, shorter rule for safety defects. A brake or steering problem has to meet the same four-attempt or 30-day test.

Direct written notice to the manufacturer

The presumption doesn't apply against a manufacturer until it has received direct written notice from you or someone acting for you, and has had a chance to fix the car itself (§ 5004(b)). Telling the dealer isn't enough. Send a letter to the manufacturer's customer relations address listed in your warranty booklet. Describe the defect and the repair history, and keep proof that it was delivered.

The law also protects you if the manufacturer doesn't respond. If it doesn't try the repair itself or arrange for one, it loses the right to argue that the dealer did the work badly or caused the problem.

Replacement or refund, and your right to choose a refund

When the defect still isn't fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, § 5003 requires the manufacturer to either replace the car with a comparable new car you find acceptable, or buy it back. Delaware gives you something many states don't: an unqualified right to turn down a replacement and demand a refund instead.

Refunds. The manufacturer takes the car back and refunds the full purchase price, including all trade-in credits and allowances. It also reimburses related costs such as sales tax, registration fees, and dealer prep fees. Two deductions are allowed:

  • a use allowance of no more than the full purchase price times the miles driven before you first reported the defect, divided by 100,000
  • an allowance for damage beyond normal wear and tear, not counting damage caused by the defect itself.

For example, if you paid $38,000 and first reported the problem at 2,500 miles, the use deduction can't be more than $38,000 × 2,500 ÷ 100,000, or $950. Miles you drive after that first report don't count against you. The money is paid to you and to any lienholder according to each one's interest.

Replacements. If you accept a new car, the manufacturer has to reimburse incidental costs of the swap, such as dealer prep, registration transfer fees, and sales tax. If the manufacturer or its finance arm financed the original car, it can't make you refinance the replacement on terms worse than your original loan.

A manufacturer can't make its dealer pay for the buyback unless the dealer's repairs didn't follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Defenses and attorney fees

Under § 5006, a manufacturer can defend a claim by showing the defect doesn't substantially impair use, value, or safety, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications by someone other than the manufacturer, its agent, or a dealer. In court, the judge may award a winning consumer costs and reasonable attorney fees (§ 5005). That's at the judge's discretion, not automatic. If the court finds a case was brought in bad faith or is frivolous, it can order the consumer to pay the manufacturer's fees.

Manufacturer arbitration programs

Delaware has no state lemon-law arbitration board. Manufacturers can run their own informal dispute programs, but you have to use one first only if CPU has given it an annual certificate of approval (§ 5007). If the program isn't certified, you can go straight to court. To be certified, a program has to meet the federal 16 CFR Part 703 rules and also:

  • Hold its hearings in Delaware.
  • Decide within 65 days after you give written notice of the dispute.
  • Carry out an accepted award within 30 days.
  • Require no more than one inspection and one more repair attempt on the same defect.
  • Consider replacement and refund as remedies.

Manufacturers have to file every decision with CPU within 30 days. CPU says you can file a complaint at any point, even while a manufacturer's program is reviewing your case. Your rights under this chapter add to your other warranty rights and don't replace them (§ 5008).

Buying a car online

CPU has also warned about problems with cars bought online. These include sellers who take payment and never deliver a car, cars in much worse condition than advertised, and out-of-state sellers without a Delaware license. Another warning sign is a seller who keeps issuing temporary tags because it never actually had the title, which is illegal in Delaware. Before you pay, check the DMV's list of licensed dealers. Ask to see the title and confirm the VIN matches, and look for brands such as "flood," "salvage," or "rebuilt." If you can't inspect the car yourself, ask for a local mechanic's report.
