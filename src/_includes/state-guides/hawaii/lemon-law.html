How Hawaii's lemon law works

Hawaii's Motor Vehicle Express Warranty Enforcement law, HRS chapter 481I, gives the buyer or lessee of a defective new vehicle a way to get a replacement or a full refund when the manufacturer can't fix a warranty problem. In the chapter's statement of intent, the legislature calls a car a major consumer purchase and says a defective one creates a hardship. It also wants good-faith warranty complaints resolved by the manufacturer within a set period of time.

The law is administered through the State Certified Arbitration Program (SCAP), which is run by the Regulated Industries Complaints Office (RICO) within the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs. The state runs this arbitration itself, and the arbitrators don't work for the manufacturer. Chapter 481I adds to your other legal rights and doesn't take any away.

Vehicles and owners the law protects

A "consumer" under § 481I-2 is anyone who buys the vehicle for a purpose other than resale, a qualifying lessee, anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the express warranty is still running, and anyone else entitled to enforce that warranty. Leases count if they run one year or longer and make the lessee responsible for repairs, or if they are lease-purchase agreements.

The vehicle must be self-propelled and used mainly for personal, family or household purposes. The definition also reaches:

  • Motorcycles, though not motor scooters or mopeds
  • Dealer demonstrators
  • An individually registered vehicle used for both business and personal purposes
  • A vehicle owned or leased by a sole proprietorship, corporation or partnership that buys or leases no more than one vehicle a year and also uses it personally

Vehicles over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating are excluded.

The defect must be a "nonconformity," meaning a failure to meet the express warranty that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety. "Substantially impairs" means the vehicle is unfit, unreliable or unsafe for normal use, or its value is significantly reduced. Problems caused by an accident, abuse, neglect or modification by someone other than the manufacturer or its dealers don't count, and the manufacturer can raise abuse, neglect or unauthorized changes as a defense.

How long protection lasts

The lemon law rights period ends at the earliest of three points: the end of the manufacturer's express warranty, two years after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, or the first 24,000 miles. The period, and the 30-business-day count below, are extended for any time repair service isn't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

What matters is reporting the problem in writing to the manufacturer, its agent, distributor or authorized dealer before the period runs out. Once you've done that, the manufacturer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity, even if the work happens after the period ends. Any lawsuit under § 481I-3 must be filed within one year after the rights period expires.

When the repair attempts are enough

If the manufacturer can't fix a defect after a reasonable number of documented attempts, it must replace the vehicle or take it back and refund you. Hawaii presumes the number of attempts was reasonable if, during the rights period:

  • The same nonconformity has been examined or repaired at least three times and is still there
  • A nonconformity likely to cause death or serious bodily injury has been examined or repaired at least once and is still there
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a cumulative total of 30 or more business days, for one or more nonconformities

A "business day" is any day the manufacturer's authorized dealers normally have their service departments open. The presumption only applies after the manufacturer has received a written report of the problem from you and has had a reasonable chance to repair it. Dealers have their own duty here too. After a second notice of the same problem, or once the car has been out of service for more than 20 business days, the dealer must notify the manufacturer.

Your obligation to notify the manufacturer applies only if you were given the written SCAP notice and statement of rights described below. Even so, sending the manufacturer a dated letter is the safest way to start the presumption.

What a refund or replacement includes

A refund covers the full purchase price, including charges for undercoating, dealer preparation, transportation and installed options. It also covers collateral charges and incidental charges, minus a reasonable offset for your use. Collateral charges include finance and interest charges, general excise tax, license and registration fees and title charges. Incidental charges are costs the defect directly caused, such as towing and alternative transportation, but not loss of use, lost income or personal injury. The purchase price includes any net trade-in allowance. For a second or later buyer, it's that buyer's price, capped at what the original buyer paid.

The use offset is 1% of the purchase price for every 1,000 miles you drove before the first trigger: the third repair attempt for the same defect, the first attempt on a dangerous defect, or the 30th cumulative business day out of service. On a $40,000 vehicle with 6,000 miles at that point, the offset would be $2,400. The manufacturer can also subtract for damage beyond normal wear and tear that has nothing to do with the defect.

A replacement must be identical or reasonably equivalent to your vehicle as it was when you got it, including any service contract and factory or dealer options. The manufacturer pays the general excise tax and the license and registration fees on the replacement. Refunds go to you and any lienholder according to their interests, and lease refunds are split between lessor and lessee under DCCA rules.

Paperwork the dealer owes you

During the rights period, each time your vehicle comes back from warranty diagnosis or repair, you're entitled to a complete, itemized and legible repair order. It must describe the problem you reported, the diagnosis, the parts and labor supplied, the date and odometer reading when the vehicle came in, and the date it was ready for pickup. You sign it and get a copy. On request, the manufacturer must also give you copies of any inspection, diagnostic or test-drive reports and any technical service bulletins about the problem for your year and model. Once it gets your written report, it must tell you about those bulletins and your right to copies.

At the time of sale, the dealer must give you a written, DCCA-approved notice explaining the State Certified Arbitration Program and your rights in plain language. The notice has to say that written notice to the manufacturer is required before you can get a refund or replacement, and give the name and address to send it to.

State Certified Arbitration Program

Under § 481I-4, DCCA runs the program in line with the Federal Trade Commission's dispute-resolution rules (16 CFR part 703) and may contract with an independent arbitration organization to hear cases. The arbitrator must have no ties to manufacturers or dealers. Filing costs the consumer $50 and the manufacturer $200, and a decision in your favor must refund your $50.

A decision is due within 45 days after you invoke the program. If unforeseen circumstances delay it, the decision is still valid. It must set a date for completing whatever remedy is ordered. If you agree to be bound by the program, the manufacturer is bound too, and the prevailing party may be awarded reasonable attorney's fees.

If you choose nonbinding arbitration, either side can demand a new trial in court within 30 calendar days after the award is served. The award can't be used as evidence at that trial. A party that demands trial and doesn't improve its result by at least 25% has to pay the other side's reasonable trial costs and attorney's fees. If no one demands trial within 30 days, the award becomes binding. A manufacturer that fails to comply on time with a binding decision is presumed to have committed an unfair or deceptive practice, unless it shows good faith or another listed excuse.

Buying a returned lemon

A vehicle returned under this chapter, or through a judgment, settlement or arbitration award in any state, can't be resold, leased or auctioned until three conditions are met. The defect must be fixed. The defect must be disclosed on a separate document, in 10-point capital letters, signed by both the manufacturer and the buyer. And the manufacturer must give the new buyer a written warranty covering the defect if it comes back within one year or 12,000 miles of the resale, whichever comes first. Breaking these rules is prima facie evidence of a deceptive practice under chapter 480.
