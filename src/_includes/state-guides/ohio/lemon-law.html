Ohio's lemon law

Ohio's lemon law is Ohio Rev. Code §§ 1345.71 through 1345.78, part of the Consumer Sales Practices chapter. It requires a manufacturer to repair a new vehicle that doesn't conform to its written warranty. If the problem can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must replace the vehicle or refund the full purchase price, at the consumer's option. The sections are published by the Legislative Service Commission, starting with the presumption in § 1345.73.

Who and what is covered

Section 1345.71 defines a consumer as any of these:

  • A buyer of a motor vehicle, other than for resale
  • A lessee under a lease charged at a periodic rate for a term of 30 days or more, where the title is in someone else's name
  • Anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the express warranty still applies, and
  • Anyone else the warranty lets enforce it.

A covered "motor vehicle" is a passenger car or noncommercial motor vehicle. For a motor home, coverage extends to the parts other than the permanently installed cold storage, cooking, eating and sleeping facilities. Mobile homes, recreational vehicles and manufactured homes are excluded.

A "nonconformity" is a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety to you and doesn't conform to the manufacturer's or distributor's express warranty. Any one of those three is enough.

The disclosure at purchase

Section 1345.74(A) requires the manufacturer, directly or through its dealer, to give you a separate written statement at the time of purchase, in 10-point capital letters, that begins: "IMPORTANT: IF THIS VEHICLE IS DEFECTIVE, YOU MAY BE ENTITLED UNDER STATE LAW TO A REPLACEMENT OR TO COMPENSATION." For a lease, the statement comes when the lease is signed. Under § 1345.74(B), every time the vehicle comes back from service or repair, the manufacturer or dealer must give you a fully itemized written statement of all work performed, including parts and labor.

The repair duty

Under § 1345.72(A), if you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer during the first year after original delivery or the first 18,000 miles, whichever comes first, the manufacturer has to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity. That duty continues even if the repairs happen after the year or the mileage has passed.

When a "reasonable number of attempts" is presumed

Section 1345.73 presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts if any of these happen during the first year or 18,000 miles, whichever is earlier:

  1. Substantially the same nonconformity has been subject to repair three or more times and still exists or comes back
  2. The vehicle is out of service for repair for a cumulative 30 or more calendar days
  3. There have been eight or more attempts to repair any nonconformity, whether or not they involve the same problem, or
  4. There has been at least one attempt to fix a nonconformity that's likely to cause death or serious bodily injury if the vehicle is driven, and the problem still exists or comes back.

Each of these periods is extended for any time the vehicle couldn't reasonably be repaired because of war, invasion, civil unrest, a strike, fire, flood or a natural disaster. During such an extension, the manufacturer must arrange for you to use another vehicle at no cost.

Refund or replacement

Under § 1345.72(B), if the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer can't correct the nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must, at your option, either replace the vehicle with a new one acceptable to you or take it back and refund:

  • The full purchase price, and
  • All incidental damages, including lender or lessor fees for making or canceling the loan or lease and expenses caused by the nonconformity, such as towing, rental vehicles, meals and lodging.

Section 1345.71(F) defines the full purchase price for a sale as the contract price, including transportation, undercoating, dealer-installed options and accessories, dealer services, preparation and delivery charges, plus finance, credit insurance, warranty and service contract charges, sales tax, license and registration fees and other government charges. For a lease, it covers the capitalized cost reduction, security deposit, taxes, title fees, all monthly payments, the residual value and the same finance, insurance, warranty and service contract charges.

Loans and leases

A refund is paid by a check made out jointly to you and any lienholder shown on the title, or the lessor. The lender or lessor can deduct what you still owe, including refunded cancellation fees, and must send you the rest right away and cancel the lien or lease. If you choose a replacement, the manufacturer notifies the lienholder or lessor. If the same lender agrees to finance or lease the new vehicle, it releases its lien on the old one. If it doesn't, the original loan or lease stays in effect until it's satisfied.

Arbitration through an approved program

Section 1345.77 directs the Attorney General to adopt rules for qualifying informal dispute resolution programs. They're supervised by the consumer protection division of the Attorney General's office and must meet at least the federal Magnuson-Moss requirements. If a qualified program exists and you got timely written notice of it, with a description of how it works, you have to try it before suing. You can go to court if no such program exists, if you're unhappy with its decision, or if the manufacturer or dealer doesn't promptly carry out the decision. Violating the program rules is an unfair and deceptive act under § 1345.02.

Going to court

Under § 1345.75, you can sue a manufacturer that doesn't comply with § 1345.72 in a court of common pleas or another court with jurisdiction. Along with the refund or replacement, you can recover reasonable attorney fees and all court costs. The lawsuit must be filed within five years of original delivery. Any limitation period under federal or Ohio law is paused from the date you file a complaint with a qualified dispute program until the date it decides. These remedies add to any others you have under law.

It's an affirmative defense that the nonconformity resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration by anyone other than the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer. Section 1345.72(C) says the law doesn't make dealers liable or give buyers a claim against them.

Buyback titles and resale

A "buyback" is a vehicle a manufacturer replaced or repurchased after the consumer asserted a warranty nonconformity, whether by court judgment, dispute program decision or settlement, in Ohio or elsewhere. Under § 1345.76, the manufacturer must get the title and, within 30 days and before transferring it, apply to the county clerk of courts for a buyback title stamped "BUYBACK: This vehicle was returned to the manufacturer because it may not have conformed to its warranty." Every later title carries the same words.

A buyback can't be resold or leased in Ohio unless the manufacturer provides the original express warranty for the longer of the remaining original term or 12,000 miles or 12 months after resale, whichever of those is earlier. The buyer must also receive, before signing anything, a separate 10-point capital-letter warning listing each defect on its own line. A vehicle returned for a defect likely to cause death or serious bodily injury may not be sold, leased or driven in Ohio at all. Under § 1345.78, violating these resale rules is an unfair and deceptive act, and the Attorney General investigates false buyback title applications.
