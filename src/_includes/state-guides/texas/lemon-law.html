Texas's lemon law

The Texas lemon law is Subchapter M of Chapter 2301 of the Texas Occupations Code, sections 2301.601 through 2301.613. The Texas Department of Motor Vehicles (TxDMV) administers it. Under § 2301.607, you have to exhaust the TxDMV process before a refund or replacement is available. The department describes the process as one that can be less complicated and less expensive than a lawsuit.

Under § 2301.604, if a manufacturer, converter or distributor can't fix a warranty defect after a reasonable number of attempts, and the defect creates a serious safety hazard or substantially impairs the vehicle's use or market value, it must replace the vehicle or take it back and refund the purchase price. It must also reimburse your reasonable incidental costs from losing use of the vehicle.

Vehicles and owners covered

TxDMV says the law covers new cars, trucks, vans, motorcycles, all-terrain vehicles, motor homes, towable recreational vehicles (TRVs) and neighborhood electric vehicles with a defect covered by the manufacturer's written warranty. Demonstrators that have never been titled count as new. The department lists repossessed vehicles, non-travel trailers, boats and farm equipment as outside the law, along with minor problems such as rattles or radio static that don't substantially impair use or value.

Section 2301.601 defines an owner as someone entitled to enforce the manufacturer's warranty who:

  • bought the vehicle at retail from a license holder
  • is a lessor or lessee, other than a sublessee, who bought or leased it from a license holder
  • lives in Texas and registered the vehicle in Texas
  • bought or leased it at retail and is an active-duty service member stationed in Texas when the proceeding begins, or
  • received the vehicle by transfer or assignment from one of those people, lives in Texas and registered it in Texas

The manufacturer's duty to make warranty repairs continues after the warranty expires if the problem was reported during the warranty term, or if a presumption under § 2301.605 has already been created.

Three tests for a reasonable number of attempts

Section 2301.605 creates a rebuttable presumption that enough repair attempts have been made if one of these happens before the warranty expires or before 24 months or 24,000 miles after delivery, whichever comes first:

  • Four-times test: the same nonconformity continues after being subject to repair four or more times.
  • Serious safety hazard test: a nonconformity that creates a serious safety hazard continues after two or more repair attempts.
  • 30-day test: a nonconformity that substantially impairs use or market value still exists, and the vehicle has been out of service for repair for a total of 30 or more days.

A serious safety hazard is a life-threatening malfunction or nonconformity that substantially impedes your ability to control or operate the vehicle, or creates a substantial risk of fire or explosion. Days when the manufacturer or distributor lent you a comparable vehicle while a franchised dealer repaired yours don't count toward the 30 days. All of these periods are extended for any time repair services weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster. TxDMV notes that the mileage limits generally don't apply to TRVs or vehicles without an odometer.

Written notice and a final chance to repair

Under § 2301.606(c), the department can't order a refund or replacement unless the manufacturer, converter or distributor received written notice of the defect from you, someone on your behalf, or the department, and had an opportunity to cure it. TxDMV recommends sending the notice by certified mail and publishes a sample letter.

Keep every repair order. TxDMV advises having each problem fully described on the repair order, keeping a log of recurring problems with the date, time and location, and storing all your records in a safe place.

Filing a complaint with TxDMV

A lemon law complaint costs $35. Under 43 Tex. Admin. Code § 224.232, the fee is nonrefundable, but a complainant who wins is entitled to reimbursement from the losing party. The proceeding officially begins on the date the department receives the fee. You can file by mail or through the Motor Vehicle Dealer Online Complaint System, which TxDMV recommends for the fastest processing.

The deadline in § 2301.606(d) is six months after the earliest of the warranty's expiration, 24 months after delivery, or 24,000 miles. TxDMV urges filing as soon as you realize the dealer is having trouble fixing the vehicle.

After you file, TxDMV staff review the complaint for completeness and eligibility, and a case advisor tries to settle it through nonbinding mediation. Section 224.238 requires both sides to take part in good faith. If mediation doesn't resolve it, the case goes to a hearing before an independent hearings examiner, where you can present evidence, bring the vehicle for inspection and call witnesses. TxDMV says the examiner issues a written decision within 60 days after the hearing closes.

If no final order has been issued by the 151st day after you filed, § 2301.607 requires the department to notify both sides by certified mail that you may file a civil action. The 150-day period is extended if you ask for a delay or cause one.

Refund, replacement or repair

For a refund, TxDMV says the manufacturer buys back the vehicle for the purchase price, including taxes, title and license fees, minus a deduction for use calculated by formula from the miles on the vehicle at the time of the hearing. Interest paid on a loan isn't included. The department posts repurchase calculation spreadsheets for purchased vehicles, leased vehicles and TRVs. A refund on a financed vehicle is split between you and the lienholder according to each one's interest.

For a replacement, the manufacturer provides a comparable vehicle acceptable to you, usually the same make, model and accessories, minus the mileage used. You pay for any upgrades. If you don't qualify for a refund or replacement, the department can still order repairs and reimbursement of out-of-pocket costs for repairs the warranty should have covered.

In a lease case, the order may end the lease and divide refunds and the use allowance between lessee and lessor. An order can make a franchised dealer pay only for an item or option the dealer added, and only to the extent it contributed to the defect.

The manufacturer can defend by proving the defect resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration, or that it doesn't substantially impair use or market value.

Warranty performance claims

Section 2301.204 lets an owner complain about warranty-covered defects in writing to the dealer, manufacturer, converter or distributor and send a copy to the department. These repair-only claims have no filing fee, under § 224.232(f). TxDMV says a used vehicle may qualify if it's still under the manufacturer's original warranty, or if the defect began and was reported while that warranty was in effect and still exists. An extended service contract doesn't count.

Rehearing, appeals and other remedies

Either side can ask TxDMV for a rehearing of the final order. After that, § 2301.609 gives a party affected by the order judicial review under the substantial evidence rule in a Travis County district court.

The subchapter doesn't limit rights you have under other laws. A contract term that excludes or changes these remedies is void unless it's part of a settlement agreement with the manufacturer. Once you've exhausted the TxDMV process, refund or replacement is available in an action against a manufacturer, converter or distributor under Chapter 17 of the Business and Commerce Code, but it can't be awarded in an action against a seller under Chapter 2 or 17.

Resale of repurchased vehicles

Under § 2301.610, a vehicle repurchased or replaced by order must be restored to factory specifications for the defect and come with a new 12-month, 12,000-mile warranty. A disclosure statement, issued through the franchised dealer, must go with it through the next retail sale, with a TxDMV toll-free number buyers can call about the original defect. TxDMV publishes an annual report listing ordered repurchases and replacements by brand and model, and new-vehicle dealers must post the department's lemon law notice where customers pay for repairs.
