Virginia's liability minimums

Virginia sets its minimum liability limits in Va. Code § 46.2-472. For every owner's policy effective on or after January 1, 2025, coverage must be at least:

  • $50,000 for bodily injury to or death of one person in any one accident
  • $100,000 for bodily injury to or death of two or more people in any one accident
  • $25,000 for injury to or destruction of other people's property in any one accident

Policies effective from January 1, 2022 through December 31, 2024 carried lower limits of $30,000, $60,000 and $20,000, and the DMV's Insurance Requirements page lists both sets. The policy has to cover liability arising from the vehicle's ownership, use or operation in Virginia, anywhere else in the United States, or in Canada. It insures the named person and anyone using the vehicle with the named insured's permission.

Your insurer has to be authorized to do business in Virginia. The DMV notes that the requirements also apply to antique motor vehicles, and that self-insurance and surety bonds are options for owners of business vehicles.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage

Under Va. Code § 38.2-2206(A), every liability policy issued or delivered in Virginia, or on a vehicle principally garaged or used here, must include uninsured motorist coverage. The coverage pays what you're legally entitled to recover from the owner or operator of an uninsured vehicle, with limits no lower than the § 46.2-472 minimums.

By default, the uninsured motorist limits equal your liability limits. Any one named insured can reject the extra coverage above the minimum by notifying the insurer, and that choice binds everyone on the policy.

The same endorsement must include underinsured motorist coverage at the same limits as the uninsured coverage. It pays without a credit for the at-fault driver's available liability coverage, unless a named insured signs an election to reduce the payments by that amount.

The endorsement also has to provide at least $20,000 for damage to your own property in any one accident. It may exclude the first $200 of the loss when the accident involves an owner or operator who can't be identified.

The statute treats a vehicle as uninsured in several situations:

  • It has no liability insurance at the § 46.2-472 amounts, and no bond, deposit or self-insurance in its place.
  • Its insurer denies coverage for any reason, including the insured's failure to cooperate.
  • Its owner or operator is immune from liability for negligence.
  • Its owner or operator is unknown.

A vehicle is underinsured to the extent that the coverage available for your injuries or damage is less than your damages, up to the amount of your underinsured motorist coverage.

Insurance at registration and DMV verification

Under § 46.2-706(A), everyone applying to register a vehicle has to certify to the Commissioner that it's an insured motor vehicle, or that its owner holds a certificate of self-insurance under § 46.2-368. The DMV says you have to make the same certification to buy license plates or decals.

The DMV runs an electronic verification program with the insurance industry, comparing its owner and vehicle records with liability information that insurers send in. If it finds no record of coverage, it may require you to verify your insurance. Refusing or neglecting to provide the information within 30 days, or an insurer's notice that the policy isn't in effect, leads to suspension under § 46.2-706(B).

The DMV advises drivers to keep proof of insurance in the vehicle at all times.

If your coverage ends

According to the DMV, if your liability coverage ends or is canceled during the registration period, you must do one of three things:

  • Reinsure the vehicle.
  • Deactivate your license plates.
  • Permanently surrender the plates to the DMV.

Section 46.2-707 makes it a Class 3 misdemeanor to fail to surrender the plates of an uninsured vehicle right away, unless the registration has been deactivated under § 46.2-646.1.

Penalties

Under § 46.2-707, an owner who drives an uninsured vehicle licensed or subject to registration in Virginia, or lets someone else drive it, is guilty of a Class 3 misdemeanor. The same applies to a non-owner who drives it knowing it's uninsured, and to anyone who gives false evidence of insurance. Those rules don't apply if the owner had good cause to believe, and did believe, that the vehicle was insured.

After a conviction, the Commissioner suspends the owner's driver's license and all registrations and plates. To get them back, you pay a $600 noncompliance fee and furnish proof of future financial responsibility. The DMV describes that proof as an SR-22 certificate filed for three years, plus any reinstatement fee, and says eligible owners can pay the statutory fee through a payment plan. Under § 46.2-708, the same suspension and fee apply when an uninsured vehicle is involved in a reportable accident causing death, injury or property damage.

A suspension under §§ 46.2-706 or 46.2-708 doesn't take effect until the Commissioner offers an administrative hearing. The request generally has to reach the DMV within 180 days of the order. After three years from the suspension, the Commissioner may release you from the proof requirement.

What coverage costs

The NAIC's 2024 Average Premium Supplement, Table 5, lists a combined average of $1,358.32 in Virginia for liability, collision and comprehensive. The Insurance Research Council's estimate puts Virginia's share of uninsured drivers at 12.9%, 23rd in the nation. For more about coverage, the DMV points to the State Corporation Commission's automobile insurance resources.
