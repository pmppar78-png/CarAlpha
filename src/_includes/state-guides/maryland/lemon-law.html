Where Maryland's lemon law lives

Maryland's lemon law is Subtitle 15 of Title 14 of the Commercial Law Article. Section 14-1501 has the definitions. Section 14-1502 covers notice, the presumption, refunds, replacements and the deadline to sue. Section 14-1503 deals with excise tax, and Section 14-1504 makes a violation an unfair or deceptive trade practice. The Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division publishes a consumer guide, Your Rights Under Maryland's Lemon Law, with sample letters and a complaint form.

Which vehicles and owners are covered

The law covers vehicles registered in Maryland as a Class A passenger vehicle, Class D motorcycle, Class E truck with a manufacturer's rated capacity of 3/4 ton or less, or Class M multipurpose vehicle. Motor homes are excluded, and so is a fleet purchase of five or more vehicles. The Attorney General's guide says the law applies to new or leased vehicles.

A "consumer" is more than the first buyer. It includes anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the warranty is still running, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty. The Division's guide puts it plainly: even if you aren't the original owner, the law might apply if the original owner bought the vehicle less than 24 months ago. Section 14-1502(j) also says the rights pass to a later owner for as long as the warranties last.

The 18,000-mile, 24-month window

The statute uses its own "manufacturer's warranty period": the first 18,000 miles of operation or 24 months after original delivery to the consumer, whichever comes first. This doesn't extend the manufacturer's actual warranty. If the manufacturer wants miles driven while the vehicle belonged to someone other than the consumer to count toward the 18,000, it has to say so in 12-point bold type in the written warranty.

If repair services aren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster, the warranty, the warranty period and the 30-day out-of-service count are all extended by that time.

Write to the manufacturer by certified mail

When the vehicle doesn't conform to its warranties, Section 14-1502(b) requires you to report the problem during the warranty period. You do this by written notice to the manufacturer or factory branch, sent certified mail with return receipt requested. The seller has to disclose this procedure conspicuously, in writing, when the vehicle is sold or delivered. You must then give the manufacturer or its agent a chance to fix the problem. The repair is free, even if it happens after the warranty period ends, and it must be completed within 30 days of the manufacturer receiving your notice.

The Consumer Protection Division recommends not waiting. If the dealer has already tried once or twice and you believe the problem substantially impairs the vehicle's use and market value, the guide says to write to the manufacturer right away. You don't have to wait for the fourth repair attempt or for 30 days out of service. Its sample letters cover three situations: a vehicle that doesn't yet qualify, a first letter about a vehicle that does, and a follow-up letter. Each letter should give the year, make, model and VIN, the dealer and purchase date, the repair dates, and copies of the repair orders. The Division also asks you to send it a copy along with its complaint form.

When the manufacturer has had enough tries

Under Section 14-1502(d), the manufacturer is presumed to have made a reasonable number of attempts if, within the warranty period, any of these happened:

  • The same nonconformity, defect or condition was repaired 4 or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle was out of service for repair of one or more problems for a total of 30 or more days.
  • A brake or steering failure was repaired at least once, the manufacturer was notified and had a chance to fix it, and the repair doesn't bring the vehicle into compliance with Maryland's safety inspection laws.

Dealers have their own duty. When a vehicle comes to the same dealer a fourth time for the same problem, or has been out of service for a total of 20 days, the dealer must notify the manufacturer by certified mail within 7 days and send a copy to the Motor Vehicle Administration. If a dealer fails to do this, it doesn't take away any of your rights.

Refund or replacement

If the defect substantially impairs the vehicle's use and market value and can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, you choose the remedy. One option is a comparable replacement vehicle that's acceptable to you. The other is a refund of the full purchase price, including license fees, registration fees and similar government charges. Two deductions are allowed: a reasonable allowance for your use, capped at 15% of the purchase price, and a reasonable allowance for damage beyond normal wear that isn't caused by the defect. Refunds are paid to you and to any lienholder, according to their interests on the MVA's ownership records.

The manufacturer has two affirmative defenses. One is that the problem doesn't substantially impair use and market value. The other is that it results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Getting the excise tax back

The manufacturer doesn't refund Maryland's vehicle excise tax; the MVA does. Under Section 14-1503, when a vehicle is taken back under a judgment, arbitration award, settlement or voluntary agreement, you can recover from the MVA the excise tax you paid. The refund is based on the part of the purchase price that was refunded to you. If you get a replacement instead, the MVA credits the original tax against the tax on the new vehicle. The dealer collects only the difference if the new tax is higher, and you can recover the excess if it's lower. The business taking the vehicle back must tell you in writing that you're entitled to this. The Division's guide lists the MVA customer service line, 800-950-1MVA, for excise tax questions.

Manufacturer arbitration is optional

Maryland doesn't run a state arbitration board for lemon claims. Under Section 14-1502(i), if a manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that fully complies with the FTC's rules at 16 CFR Part 703, you may use it before the refund and replacement remedies apply. Using it doesn't stop you from seeking your other legal rights. The Attorney General's guide calls arbitration optional and says the decision binds only the manufacturer, so you can still go to court if you aren't satisfied.

The guide's preparation tips: put your records in chronological order, write a short summary of events, and submit your records and warranty with the application. Ask the program for copies of whatever the manufacturer sends in ahead of time. You may also hire an independent automotive expert and submit a written report, though you'll pay for it and may not be reimbursed even if you win.

Going to court

A lawsuit under Section 14-1502 has to be filed within 3 years of the vehicle's original delivery to the consumer. A court may award reasonable attorney's fees to a winning plaintiff. If a case is brought in bad faith or is frivolous, the court can order that party to pay the other side's fees. Under Section 14-1504, a manufacturer, factory branch or distributor found to have acted in bad faith can be ordered to pay up to $10,000 in damages on top of other remedies. Any purchase agreement that waives or limits these rights is void, and the subtitle doesn't limit other remedies, including implied warranties.

Returned vehicles that are resold

A manufacturer must notify the MVA in writing within 15 days when a vehicle is returned under the law, a judgment, an arbitration award or a voluntary agreement. If a returned vehicle from Maryland or any other state goes to a Maryland dealer, the manufacturer must disclose this in writing on a separate sheet in 10-point capital letters. The disclosure must say the vehicle was returned, describe any defect behind the return, and describe the vehicle's condition. If the vehicle is offered for resale, the seller must give the buyer a copy before the sale and send the signed copy to the MVA.

Help from the Consumer Protection Division

If the manufacturer refuses a refund or replacement, or you want help negotiating, you can file a New Car Warranty (Lemon Law) complaint. The Division will contact the manufacturer for you. Offices are in Baltimore (410-528-8662), Salisbury (410-713-3620), Hagerstown (301-791-4780) and Largo (301-386-6200). For recall and service bulletin information, the guide lists the NHTSA hotline, 800-424-9393.
