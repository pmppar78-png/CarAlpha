Electric vehicles in Montana

The U.S. Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center lists seven Montana state incentives, covering alternative fuel production, heavy-duty vehicle replacement grants and charging plans. None of them is a rebate or tax credit for drivers buying an electric car. What Montana drivers do see are the state's EV registration fees, a tax on public fast charging, and rules for low-speed and medium-speed electric vehicles.

What counts as an EV

Mont. Code Ann. § 61-3-571 defines an electric vehicle as a four-wheeled vehicle built mainly for public roads that runs only on an electric motor powered by a rechargeable battery of at least 20 kilowatt-hours. Low-speed and medium-speed electric vehicles are excluded. A plug-in hybrid has an internal combustion engine plus a rechargeable battery of at least 5 kilowatt-hours. The fee classes are set by unladen weight:

  • Class 1: under 6,000 pounds
  • Class 2: 6,000 to 10,000 pounds
  • Class 3: over 10,000 and up to 26,000 pounds
  • Class 4: over 26,000 pounds

Annual EV and plug-in hybrid fees

Section 61-3-572, enacted in 2023, adds a fee at every initial registration and renewal on top of the standard charges:

  • Class 1: $130 for an EV, $70 for a plug-in hybrid
  • Class 2: $190 for an EV, $100 for a plug-in hybrid
  • Class 3: $340 for an EV, $210 for a plug-in hybrid
  • Class 4: $1,100 for an EV, $700 for a plug-in hybrid

County treasurers send the money to the state highway restricted account. The AFDC notes that EV registration fees for private residents are scheduled to drop by 30% starting July 1, 2028.

Permanent registration

An electric or plug-in hybrid vehicle 11 or more years old can be permanently registered like other light vehicles. In that case § 61-3-573 replaces the annual fee with a one-time charge: $260 for a class 1 EV, $380 for a class 2 EV, $140 for a class 1 plug-in hybrid and $200 for a class 2 plug-in hybrid.

Public charging tax

Under Mont. Code Ann. §§ 15-70-801 through 805, electricity sold by public charging stations with a capacity over 25 kilowatts is taxed at 3 cents per kilowatt-hour. Chargers at private homes and homeowners' associations are exempt. Public stations must register with the Montana Department of Transportation within 30 days of starting operation and post their rates at the site. Under 2025's Senate Bill 228, new public chargers and legacy public chargers needed a meter measuring electricity delivered to the station by July 1, 2025. Legacy stations need a meter measuring electricity delivered to vehicles by July 1, 2028. Owners pay for the meters.

Section 69-8-803 says an entity that operates EV charging equipment isn't a public utility.

Charging plans and grants

Under the federal NEVI Formula Program, the Montana Department of Transportation had to submit an updated EV infrastructure deployment plan for each of fiscal years 2022 through 2026, working with the Energy Office at the Department of Environmental Quality. Separately, DEQ uses Montana's share of the Volkswagen Environmental Mitigation Trust for grants to replace diesel transit buses with all-electric, diesel hybrid, CNG or propane buses. It also funds all-electric replacements for school buses and airport ground support vehicles.

The AFDC's page for NorthWestern Energy lists no current residential vehicle or fuel price incentives.

Low-speed and medium-speed EVs

A low-speed EV has four wheels and a top speed between 20 and 40 mph. It can be driven only on roads posted at 25 mph or less, it can't cross a road posted above 45 mph, and the driver needs a low-speed restricted license. It pays a one-time $25 registration fee under § 61-3-321(12). A medium-speed EV tops out at 45 mph and weighs 5,000 pounds or less. It can use roads posted up to 45 mph and is titled and registered like a light vehicle.

State fleet rules

Vehicles bought for state agencies must meet or exceed the current federal fuel economy standard, and agencies must run programs to cut fuel use (§§ 2-17-416 and 2-17-417). Local governments can't impose penalties, fees or taxes on carbon, including the carbon content of transportation fuels or electricity (§ 7-1-116).

Federal credits

Check the IRS and fueleconomy.gov for the current status of federal clean vehicle credits before you count on one.
