Chapter 90, Section 7N½

The Massachusetts new car lemon law is M.G.L. c. 90, § 7N½. A separate section, § 7N¼, covers used cars and isn't discussed here. The Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation (OCABR) runs the state-certified arbitration program that manufacturers must join when a consumer asks. A violation is an unfair or deceptive act under Chapter 93A, the state's consumer protection statute. Every new vehicle offered for sale or lease in Massachusetts must carry a window sticker listing the buyer's rights under the section, and the rights must also come with the owner's manual materials.

Who and what is covered

The statute covers motor vehicles sold, leased or replaced by a dealer or manufacturer. It excludes motor homes, vehicles built mainly for off-road use, and vehicles used primarily for business. OCABR's consumer guidance describes eligible vehicles as cars, motorcycles, vans or trucks used for personal or family purposes. They must be sold to you in Massachusetts by a licensed dealer; if you bought out of state, you would look to that state's law. Defects that only affect appearance may not qualify.

A "consumer" includes the buyer, a lessee with a lease of at least one year, anyone the vehicle is transferred to while an express or implied warranty is in effect, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty.

The term of protection

Your protection lasts one year or 15,000 miles from the original delivery of the new vehicle, whichever comes first. A replacement vehicle supplied under the law gets a fresh term of one year or 15,000 miles from its own delivery. A "nonconformity" is any defect or malfunction, or combination of them, that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety. If you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer during the term, they must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity with its warranty.

Three repairs or 15 business days, then a final chance

Under § 7N½(4), the manufacturer is deemed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the term of protection:

  • the same nonconformity was repaired three or more times and still exists or came back, or
  • the vehicle was out of service for repairs for a total of 15 or more business days.

A "business day" is any day the service departments of the manufacturer's authorized dealers are normally open. After either limit is reached, the manufacturer gets one more opportunity to fix the problem, lasting no more than seven business days, even if that chance comes after the term has ended. It begins on the day the manufacturer first knew, or should have known, that a limit was met. OCABR's guidance says that at that point you should contact the manufacturer and give it the seven days to repair, replace or repurchase the vehicle. Keep records of every contact and repair.

The term, the 15-day count and the final opportunity are all extended during war, invasion, fire, flood or other natural disaster that makes repairs unavailable. They're also extended during a strike, as long as the manufacturer or dealer gives you free use of a vehicle while yours is out of service. The manufacturer has the burden of proving that an event directly caused its failure to repair, and extensions for overlapping events don't add together.

Refund or replacement, and what's included

When the manufacturer can't cure the problem, it must take the vehicle back. For a purchase, it refunds the full contract price, including all credits and allowances for a trade-in. For a lease, it refunds all lease payments you made. It may instead offer a replacement, but you have an unqualified right to reject the offer and demand a refund. A refund also covers incidental costs: sales tax, the registration fee, finance charges and the cost of dealer-added options. If towing or a rental car wasn't provided free, the manufacturer reimburses reasonable towing and rental costs caused by the defect, whether you get a refund or a replacement. With a replacement, it covers registration transfer fees and any sales tax caused by the swap. A leased vehicle is replaced with an identical model for the rest of the lease, and the manufacturer can't require new financing or lease terms beyond what you originally agreed to.

Two amounts can be deducted. One is any cash award you accepted earlier from the manufacturer to settle the dispute. The other is a use allowance with a set formula: the contract price (or your total lease payments) multiplied by the miles driven before the manufacturer accepted the return, divided by 100,000. For motorcycles, the divisor is 25,000. You may keep driving the vehicle until you're offered a full refund or an acceptable replacement, but those miles count toward the allowance.

The manufacturer can defend a claim by showing that the problem doesn't substantially impair use, value or safety. It can also show that the problem came from owner negligence, an accident, vandalism, repairs by someone other than the manufacturer or an authorized dealer, or unauthorized substantial modifications.

State-certified arbitration

Every manufacturer must take part in state-certified arbitration if you request it within 18 months of the vehicle's original delivery. A manufacturer can't require you to notify it directly before you use the program. The arbitrator is a professional appointed by OCABR's director, and must issue a written finding within 45 days after the director receives your request.

Under the program's rule, 201 CMR 11.02, the request must say that the defect substantially impairs use, value or safety, and that it wasn't caused by you. It must also say the vehicle had three repair attempts or 15 business days out of service, and that you gave the manufacturer its seven-day final opportunity. It must include a narrative and a chronology of repairs.

The OCABR application asks for these documents:

  • the purchase contract and the original and current registrations
  • the final-repair-opportunity letter and the manufacturer's response
  • a finance statement showing principal and interest paid
  • repair orders, the warranty and receipts for incidental costs
  • all messages with the dealer and manufacturer

Mail two collated copies to the Lemon Law Arbitration Program, OCABR, 1 Federal Street, Suite 0720, Boston, MA 02110-2012. There's no application fee, but you pay the arbitrator $300 within 10 business days of the hearing notice. That amount is added to the award if you win. Hearings are held by video on Microsoft Teams, and OCABR's phone number is (617) 973-8700.

After the arbitrator decides

If the arbitrator rules in your favor, the manufacturer has 21 days either to deliver the refund or replacement, including incidental costs, or to appeal to superior court. An appeal requires a bond equal to the award plus $2,500 for your expected attorney's fees. If the court upholds the award, you also recover $25 a day for each day the vehicle was out of use after its return, unless the manufacturer lent you a comparable vehicle for free. A prevailing consumer is awarded reasonable attorney's fees and costs. If the court finds the appeal had no reasonable basis or was frivolous, the total award is doubled.

A manufacturer that neither appeals nor delivers, and doesn't give you an estimated delivery date for a replacement (no more than 60 days out), can be fined $5,000 a day starting on day 22, up to $50,000 per violation. If 81 days pass with nothing paid, the Attorney General must go to court. Failing to follow an award or appeal on time also entitles you to at least double your actual damages under Chapter 93A, unless the manufacturer shows the failure was beyond its control.

If you're unhappy with the arbitrator's finding, you can still file a Chapter 93A claim. The arbitrator's findings of fact are prima facie evidence in any later case between the parties.

Returned vehicles

A vehicle returned under § 7N½ can't be resold in Massachusetts without a clear, conspicuous written disclosure that it was returned, in a form set by the Attorney General. The law doesn't create claims against dealers, except in limited cases such as faulty dealer preparation or repairs, or dealer warranties that go beyond the manufacturer's.
