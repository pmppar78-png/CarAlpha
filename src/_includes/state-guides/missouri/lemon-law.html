Missouri's new motor vehicle lemon law

Missouri's lemon law is in Mo. Rev. Stat. §§ 407.560 to 407.579, part of the chapter on merchandising practices. It applies to new vehicles sold after January 1, 1985. When a manufacturer can't fix a warranty defect after a reasonable number of tries, it must replace the vehicle or take it back for a refund. With one narrow exception in the definitions section, the law doesn't limit any other rights you have at law or in equity. You can read each section on the Missouri Revisor of Statutes site.

Who and what is covered

A consumer is someone who buys a new motor vehicle, not for resale, and uses it mainly for personal, family or household purposes. The definition also covers anyone the vehicle is transferred to for those purposes while an express warranty applies, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it.

A new motor vehicle is one being transferred for the first time from a manufacturer, distributor or new vehicle dealer. It can't have been registered or titled in Missouri or any other state, and it must be offered by a dealer franchised for that make. Demonstrators and lease-purchase vehicles count if a manufacturer's warranty came with the sale. These are excluded:

  • commercial motor vehicles
  • off-road vehicles
  • mopeds and electric bicycles
  • motorcycles
  • recreational motor vehicles, although their chassis, engine, powertrain and component parts are covered

The law enforces the manufacturer's express warranty, meaning a written promise about materials or workmanship, or that the vehicle will perform at a set level for a set time.

Reporting the problem in time

Under § 407.565, you must report the nonconformity to the manufacturer or its agent, such as the dealer, during the express warranty or within one year of the original delivery, whichever ends first. Once you do, the manufacturer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty, even if the work is done after that period ends. The statute sets no separate mileage limit, but a warranty that runs out by mileage before the year is up ends the window then. Because the one-year period runs from the original delivery, someone who takes over the car during the warranty is working with the time that's left on that same clock.

Under § 407.573(1), the warranty or one-year period may be extended if you reported a problem that still hasn't been repaired when the period expires.

The presumption: four repairs or 30 working days

Section 407.571 presumes a reasonable number of attempts if, within the warranty or the first year, whichever ends first, either of these is true:

  • The same nonconformity has been subject to repair four or more times by the manufacturer or its agents, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair of the nonconformity for a total of 30 or more working days since delivery.

Time spent on routine maintenance the manufacturer prescribes doesn't count toward the 30 days. The 30 days can be extended for any time repair service wasn't available because of conditions outside the manufacturer's control. The defect must impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety to you.

Written notice and the final repair

The manufacturer must provide information about consumer complaint remedies with every new vehicle. Before you use the lemon law, § 407.573(2) requires you or your representative to notify the manufacturer in writing that the vehicle needs repair, so the manufacturer has a chance to cure the defect. The process then works like this:

  1. The manufacturer must immediately direct you to a reasonably accessible repair facility at a franchised new vehicle dealer.
  2. Once you deliver the vehicle there, the manufacturer has ten calendar days to bring it into conformity with the warranty.
  3. If you report that it still isn't fixed, the manufacturer must tell you whether it has an informal dispute settlement procedure under § 407.575, unless it already told you.

The ten-day clock starts when you deliver the vehicle to the authorized repair facility, so note that date on the repair order. Send the letter so you can prove it arrived, and keep copies of it and of every repair order, including the dates the vehicle went in and came out.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer, through its dealer or agent, can't correct a defect or condition that impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety after a reasonable number of attempts, § 407.567 requires it to do one of two things:

  • replace the vehicle with a comparable new vehicle, meaning an identical or reasonably equivalent one, that's acceptable to you
  • take title and refund the full purchase price, plus all reasonably incurred collateral charges

Missouri's statute says the manufacturer makes that choice "at its option."

Collateral charges are costs beyond the manufacturer's sticker price, and the statute specifically includes sales tax, license, registration and title fees, and motor vehicle inspections. In either case the manufacturer subtracts a reasonable allowance for your use of the vehicle. The statute doesn't set a per-mile formula. Refunds go to you and any lienholder of record, according to their interests.

Once it takes title, the manufacturer can ask the Department of Revenue to reimburse the sales tax and license, registration and title fees it refunded to you. It can instead give you the paperwork and have you apply to the Department for that refund directly, along with a statement that it didn't refund those amounts to you.

Manufacturer dispute programs

Missouri doesn't run a state lemon-law arbitration board. Under § 407.575, if a manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that fully complies with the FTC rules at 16 C.F.R. part 703, you must use it first before the refund and replacement provisions apply to you.

Lawsuit deadlines

Under § 407.573(3), a lawsuit must be filed by the earlier of six months after the express warranty expires or 18 months after the original delivery. If you used a manufacturer's dispute program, you have 90 days after the panel's final action instead. With an 18-month cap, it helps to start the notice and dispute steps early.

Attorney fees, both ways

Under § 407.577, a consumer who follows the law's steps and finally wins in court recovers reasonable costs and expenses, including attorney fees based on actual time spent. The statute also has a risk for consumers. You'll owe the manufacturer's costs and reasonable attorney fees if a court finds your claim was filed in bad faith, only to harass, or without a substantial justifiable legal or factual issue. You'll also owe them if your final recovery isn't at least 10% more than a settlement offer the manufacturer made before you sued. Weigh any written offer carefully with that rule in mind.
