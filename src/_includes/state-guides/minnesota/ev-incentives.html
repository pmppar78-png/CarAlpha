EV incentives and rules in Minnesota

Minnesota's state rebate for buying an electric vehicle has run out of money. There's currently no state purchase rebate or tax credit for drivers. Owners of all-electric cars and plug-in hybrids pay a registration surcharge every year, and new taxes and licensing rules for public chargers take effect in 2027. The details below come from the Minnesota Department of Commerce, state statutes, and the U.S. Department of Energy's Alternative Fuels Data Center.

The 2023 EV rebate program

In 2023 the Legislature appropriated $15 million for rebates under Minn. Stat. § 216C.401. The program paid $2,500 toward a new EV with a base MSRP of $55,000 or less, or $600 toward a used EV priced at $25,000 or less before taxes and fees. Commerce says it sent more than 6,600 rebates between February 7, 2024 and January 3, 2025, reaching 75 of the state's 87 counties. The department now says all funds have been claimed and it isn't taking new applications. Recipients got a Form 1099, and the Department of Revenue says the rebate must be included in federal adjusted gross income.

MnDOT's EV toll credit pilot, which gave $250 for an EV or $125 for a plug-in hybrid toward E-ZPass toll lanes, covered vehicles bought or leased through October 31, 2025, and has expired.

Annual registration surcharges

Under Minn. Stat. § 168.013, an all-electric vehicle pays the greater of 0.5% of its age-adjusted MSRP or a minimum. That minimum is $150 for registration periods beginning January 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027, and $100 starting July 1, 2027. Plug-in hybrids pay 0.25%, with minimums of $75 and then $50. These surcharges are on top of the regular registration tax.

Charger rules starting in 2027

Laws passed in the 2025 special session change how public charging is taxed and regulated:

  • Beginning July 1, 2027, public charger operators pay an excise tax of 5 cents per kilowatt-hour used to charge EVs. Free chargers and chargers under 50 kilowatts are exempt.
  • Also from July 1, 2027, public charger operators need an annual license from the Department of Revenue.
  • Retail chargers must show the price per kilowatt-hour, the power level and any extra fees. Commerce inspects them every year for a fee of $100 per port.
  • Damaging or stealing chargers and other energy transmission equipment can bring prison time and a fine of up to $10,000.

A working group of five state commissioners was required to report by February 13, 2026 on electricity as a vehicle fuel, including EV surcharges.

Home charging rates

Each public utility that sells electricity at retail must offer residential customers an EV charging tariff, approved by the Public Utilities Commission, under Minn. Stat. § 216B.1614. The tariff must include a time-of-day or off-peak rate and let you choose the utility's normal energy mix or entirely renewable sources. Reselling electricity for EV charging doesn't make a business a public utility. State law also says chargers installed here must be able to charge any make or model.

Grants for chargers and school buses

The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency offers grants of up to $150,000 for dual-port public DC fast chargers rated at 150 kilowatts or more, with a 20% match, funded by the Volkswagen settlement. Commerce's electric school bus grants can cover up to 95% of bus and infrastructure costs for public, private and charter schools.

Clean Cars Minnesota

Minnesota has adopted California's vehicle emissions standards. Since January 1, 2024, they've applied to passenger cars, light-duty trucks and medium-duty vehicles, and manufacturers must meet the zero-emission vehicle production and sales requirements. Minnesota is also part of the REV Midwest agreement with Illinois, Indiana, Michigan and Wisconsin on regional charging and fleet electrification.

Low-speed electric vehicles

Neighborhood electric vehicles with a top speed of 20 to 25 mph, and medium-speed EVs with a top speed of 35 mph, can use roads with speed limits of 35 mph or less if they meet federal safety standards. They can cross faster roads. Local road authorities can restrict them further.
