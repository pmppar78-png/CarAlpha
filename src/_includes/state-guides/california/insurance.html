California's minimum liability coverage

California raised its minimum liability limits for the first time in 56 years, under Senate Bill 1107 (Chapter 717, Statutes of 2022). For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025, the floor is:

30/60/15: $30,000 for injury or death of one person, $60,000 for injury or death of two or more people in one accident, and $15,000 for property damage.

Those figures come from the DMV and the Department of Insurance's auto insurance guide. The old limits were 15/30/5, so if a site still lists those, it's out of date. The law also schedules a second increase. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2035, the minimum becomes 50/100/25, according to the Senate Insurance Committee's bill summary.

Collision or comprehensive coverage alone doesn't satisfy the law. Instead of a policy, the DMV accepts a $75,000 cash deposit with the DMV, a $75,000 surety bond, or a DMV self-insurance certificate. For deposits and self-insurance, call the DMV Insurance Unit at (916) 657-6677, option 3.

California is an at-fault state. The driver who causes a crash is responsible for the damage, and there's no required personal injury protection. Your own liability coverage doesn't pay for injuries to you or people in your household. Medical payments coverage does, starting at $1,000 per person.

Proof of insurance and DMV suspensions

You have to carry proof of insurance in the car and show it when police ask, when you renew your registration, and after a collision. Insurers report private vehicles to the DMV electronically (Vehicle Code § 16058), and they tell the DMV when you buy a policy or stop paying for one. If the DMV doesn't receive proof of insurance for a registered vehicle, it suspends the registration. The car then can't be driven or parked on public roads until you submit proof, which you can do online or by email.

Cancelling insurance on a car you've stopped driving still triggers that process. To avoid a suspension, file an Affidavit of Non-Use (REG 5090) before cancelling, and keep the car off public streets. If the car is still sitting when renewal comes due, you'll have to pay renewal fees or put it on Planned Non-Operation status. You can't file the affidavit after the registration has expired.

Driving uninsured

The Department of Insurance sums up the consequences plainly: if you can't show proof of insurance when asked, you'll get a ticket. If you truly have no insurance, your license may be suspended and the car can be impounded. The DMV may also require an SR 22, the California Proof of Insurance Certificate, as evidence of financial responsibility.

If coverage is hard to get or afford

California Low Cost Automobile Insurance (CLCA). This state program sells liability coverage to income-eligible good drivers at lower limits that still satisfy the law. The CLCA limits are $10,000/$20,000/$3,000, and SB 1107 left them unchanged. To qualify:

  • household income at or below 250 percent of the federal poverty level
  • a car worth $25,000 or less
  • a valid California license (AB 60 licenses are accepted)
  • age 16 or older (applicants under 18 must be legally emancipated).

Premiums vary by county and are higher if a driver aged 19 to 24 lives in the household. There's no broker fee. Details are at mylowcostauto.com or 1-866-602-8861.

California Automobile Assigned Risk Plan (CAARP). If accidents or tickets keep standard insurers from covering you, CAARP assigns you to a company. Every insurer licensed for auto in California has to accept CAARP drivers. All of them charge the same CAARP premium, with installments allowed and no broker fee. Call 1-800-622-0954.

Rules that work in your favor

  • Good Driver discount. Every insurer has to offer it. If you've been licensed for at least three years in a row and have no more than one point on your record, your rate has to be at least 20 percent lower than a non-good driver's rate at the same company.
  • Limits on cancellation. Once a policy is in force, an insurer can cancel or refuse to renew it for only three reasons: fraud or material misrepresentation, nonpayment, or a substantial increase in the hazard insured against.
  • Uninsured motorist coverage. UM bodily injury pays for injuries to you and your passengers when an at-fault uninsured driver hits you, at the same limits as your liability coverage. UM property damage covers your car up to $3,500, but only if the uninsured driver is identified. If you already carry collision, you may not need it.

Premiums and uninsured drivers

The NAIC's 2024 combined average premium for California (liability, collision, and comprehensive) was $1,690.23, and the average expenditure per insured vehicle was $1,454.27. Both jumped sharply from 2023, when they were $1,417.94 and $1,225.02. The Insurance Research Council estimated that 20.4 percent of California drivers were uninsured in 2023, ranking the state 8th highest. With roughly one driver in five uninsured, it's worth weighing UM coverage and a collision deductible waiver. That waiver covers your collision deductible when an uninsured driver is at fault.

Complaints and help

The California Department of Insurance answers many questions by phone at 1-800-927-HELP (4357). If the problem isn't resolved, you can file a Request for Assistance online or by mail through the CDI's consumer help page, and attach policies, cancelled checks, and letters. The CDI also publishes a consumer complaint study comparing insurers, and lets you check an agent's license with its License Status Inquiry.
