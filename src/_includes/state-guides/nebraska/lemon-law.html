Nebraska's lemon law at a glance

Nebraska's lemon law is short: nine sections, Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 60-2701 to 60-2709, which apply to vehicles from the 1984 model year on. When a new vehicle has a defect the manufacturer can't fix after a reasonable number of tries, the law requires the manufacturer to replace it or refund the purchase price. The Nebraska Department of Motor Vehicles calls the state's criteria "very strict" and reminds owners that ordinary warranty claims may still be available when a car doesn't qualify. The DMV notes there are two routes: informal arbitration, which doesn't require an attorney, or a lawsuit against the manufacturer.

Who and what is covered

Section 60-2701 defines a "consumer" as someone who buys a motor vehicle, other than for resale, that is normally used for personal, family, household or business purposes. Business use is included. The definition also covers anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while an express warranty is still in effect, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty under its terms.

The vehicle has to be a new motor vehicle, as defined in § 60-1401.30, sold in Nebraska. Recreational vehicles are excluded. The Attorney General's consumer protection site says the law doesn't cover vehicles sold as used, trailers, or self-propelled mobile homes. The "manufacturer's express warranty" is the manufacturer's written warranty, labeled as such.

If you bought from a dealer in another state, the DMV suggests checking with that state's attorney general about whether its lemon law covers you.

The one-year reporting window

Under § 60-2702, you have to report the defect to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer during the express warranty term or within one year after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, whichever ends first. If you report in time, the manufacturer has to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty, even if the work happens after the warranty or the one-year period ends.

There's no mileage cap in the statute. The clock runs on time alone. Service contracts, extended warranties and mechanical breakdown insurance don't lengthen your lemon law rights, according to the DMV. It describes them as paid protection that goes beyond the express and implied warranties, and says they aren't warranties at all.

The presumption: four repairs or 40 days

Section 60-2704 presumes the manufacturer had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the warranty term or the first year after delivery, whichever is earlier, either of these happens:

  • The same nonconformity was repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or authorized dealers, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle was out of service for repairs for a cumulative 40 or more days.

The warranty term, the one-year period and the 40 days are all extended for any time repair services weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The defect must substantially impair the vehicle's use and market value to you. Under § 60-2703, the manufacturer has an affirmative defense if the problem doesn't meet that standard or if it was caused by your abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations.

Certified-mail notice

The presumption never applies against a manufacturer until it has received prior written notice directly from you or on your behalf, by certified mail, and had a chance to cure the defect. Dealer repair visits alone aren't enough. The Attorney General's office points owners to the owner's manual, the warranty documents or the manufacturer's website for the address. Keep the green card or electronic return receipt with your repair orders.

Replacement or refund

If the defect can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, § 60-2703 requires the manufacturer to either replace the vehicle with a comparable one, or take it back and refund the full purchase price. The refund includes all sales taxes, license fees, registration fees and similar government charges, minus a reasonable allowance for your use. It's paid to you and any lienholder according to your interests.

The use allowance covers use by you and any earlier owner before the first report of the defect to the manufacturer, agent or dealer. It also covers any later time when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. The statute doesn't give a mileage formula for the allowance.

Informal dispute programs

Section 60-2705 has the Director of Motor Vehicles adopt standards for informal dispute settlement procedures that substantially comply with the Federal Trade Commission's rules in 16 C.F.R. part 703, as they stood on January 1, 2026. If the manufacturer has set up or joined a procedure the Director has certified, you have to use it before you can claim a refund or replacement under § 60-2703.

The Attorney General's guide says these programs are triggered by written notice from the consumer and that a decision should be made within 40 days after the arbitrator is notified of the dispute. A decision can be delayed if you leave out your name and address, the brand, the vehicle identification number, or a statement of the problem, or if you haven't first tried to resolve it directly with the manufacturer. If you aren't satisfied with the decision, you can still file a lawsuit.

Going to court

Under § 60-2706, a lawsuit has to be filed within one year after the express warranty expires or two years after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer, whichever comes first. A buyer whose warranty runs three years still faces the two-year deadline. Under § 60-2707, the court must award reasonable attorney's fees when the consumer wins. The statute doesn't provide fees for a winning manufacturer.

The Nebraska Supreme Court has addressed how these claims are tried. In Genetti v. Caterpillar, Inc. (2001), the court held that a lemon law action is an action at law, and that a buyer doesn't have to prove a precise or specific defect to show the product was defective. A lemon law claim and a breach-of-warranty claim may both go to the jury. A plaintiff who wins on both, though, must choose between the verdicts. That fits § 60-2708, which says the lemon law doesn't limit any other rights or remedies you have under other laws.

Used cars and cooling-off periods

The lemon law applies only to new vehicles. A used car sold "as is" comes without a warranty, and the seller doesn't have to make repairs afterward. The DMV and the Attorney General both say Nebraska law gives no three-day right to cancel a vehicle purchase once you've signed and paid. Complaints about a dealer can go to the Nebraska Motor Vehicle Industry Licensing Board.
