What Alaska requires

Alaska sets some of the highest minimum liability limits in the country. Under the Mandatory Motor Vehicle Insurance law, AS 28.22, every owner or driver of a registered vehicle has to carry at least the following, according to the Alaska DMV and the Division of Insurance's Consumer Guide to Automobile Insurance:

50/100/25: $50,000 bodily injury or death per person, $100,000 per accident, and $25,000 property damage.

Alaska is a fault-based state. There's no personal injury protection mandate. The at-fault driver's liability coverage pays the other party's medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering, and property damage covers the other car as well as things like fences and walls. Your own injuries and vehicle are covered only by optional coverages: medical payments, collision, comprehensive, and uninsured/underinsured motorist.

Under AS 28.22.019, you have to keep proof of insurance with you whenever you drive. That can be a copy of the policy, an insurance ID card, a self-insurance certificate, or an electronic version on your phone.

Who enforces the law

Alaska splits the job between two agencies, and the Division of Insurance says so directly. The Division of Insurance, part of the Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development, regulates insurers and agents under the Insurance Code in Title 21. It doesn't set the coverage requirement. The Division of Motor Vehicles enforces the driving-insurance mandate in Title 28. That includes license suspensions and SR-22 filings.

After a crash: the 15-day proof rule

Alaska enforces the mandate mostly after a crash. If you're in a crash involving injury, death, or property damage over $501, you have to show proof of insurance. The DMV's certificate of insurance form (Form 466) must be filed within 15 days. If you can't show coverage, the DMV suspends your driver's license under AS 28.22.041:

  • At least 90 days if you haven't had an insurance suspension in the past 10 years.
  • At least one year if you have had one or more in the past 10 years.

This suspension runs consecutively, after any other suspension, not at the same time. The DMV has to give you 30 days' notice before it takes effect, and during that time you can contest it in writing or in person. If you ask for a hearing, the suspension is on hold until the hearing is decided. There's one narrow exception: a crash that damaged only your own property, under $2,000, where you show coverage within 15 days and prove the lapse was beyond your control.

A limited license, for work or medical care only, may be possible if you've filed an SR-22, haven't had two or more of these suspensions in 10 years, and can show severe hardship.

SR-22 requirements

To get your license back after an insurance suspension, you need an SR-22 certificate of future financial responsibility plus the reinstatement fees. According to the Division of Insurance guide, an SR-22 has to stay on file with the DMV for three years after a crash in which you were uninsured. The DMV's reinstatement page requires the SR-22 to be dated within 30 days of the request. Your insurer files it.

Premiums and uninsured drivers

The NAIC's 2024 average combined premium for Alaska is $1,398.93. The Insurance Research Council estimated that 12.5 percent of Alaska drivers were uninsured in 2023, ranking the state 24th. Since the at-fault driver may have no coverage, especially on rural roads, UM coverage is worth pricing.

Buying a policy

The Division of Insurance's guide makes a point worth repeating: there's no such thing as a "full coverage" policy. If you ask an agent for full coverage, you'll usually get the state minimum liability plus collision and comprehensive. That may leave you without uninsured motorist coverage, rental reimbursement, or towing. Ask for each coverage by name and compare limits. If no company will insure you, Alaska drivers can get coverage through the state's assigned-risk plan, which is run by AIPSO (800-227-4659). Expect to pay more there.
