Colorado's required liability limits

25/50/15: $25,000 bodily injury per person, $50,000 bodily injury per accident, and $15,000 property damage.

The Colorado Division of Insurance describes liability as the only coverage the law requires. It pays for injuries to other people and damage to their property when you're found at fault. Colorado is an at-fault state, so the driver who causes the crash, and their insurer, pays. Your liability policy won't cover a car owned by you or a relative in your household unless that car is listed for liability on the policy, and it never covers intentional acts.

A $15,000 property damage limit is low by current standards. Hitting a late-model pickup or causing a multi-car crash can easily go past it, and you're personally responsible for the rest.

Coverages included unless you say no

Colorado lets you turn down two coverages, but your insurer has to include them by default:

Uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM). This pays for your own injuries when the at-fault driver has no insurance, not enough insurance, or leaves the scene. It's on your policy unless you reject it in writing. The Division estimates that 15 to 20 percent of Colorado drivers are uninsured, which makes this coverage worth keeping. A driver counts as underinsured when their liability limit won't cover everything you're legally owed.

Medical payments. $5,000 of med-pay is added unless you reject it, either in writing or the same way you applied for the policy. It covers reasonable medical costs for you and your passengers no matter who caused the crash. It pays before your health insurance and can be applied to your health plan's deductible or coinsurance. Insurers don't have to offer it on motorcycles, low-powered scooters, or off-road vehicles.

Physical damage and other optional coverage

  • Collision pays to repair your car after it hits another vehicle or an object, or rolls over. Insurers have to offer deductibles of $100 or $250, and you can choose a higher one.
  • Comprehensive covers fire, theft, vandalism, falling objects, and hail. The Division names hail, wind, and flood losses as a driver of comprehensive premiums. If you have a car loan, your lender will likely require both collision and comprehensive.
  • Uninsured motorist property damage is an option if you don't carry collision. It pays for damage an uninsured at-fault driver does to your car, but only if the two vehicles actually touched. It pays the lower of the car's actual cash value or the repair cost.

Your policy won't apply while your car is rented or leased to someone else unless you've bought coverage for that. Check this before listing your car on a peer-to-peer rental app.

Driving without insurance

Under C.R.S. § 42-4-1409, driving or allowing someone to drive your uninsured car is a class 1 misdemeanor traffic offense. After a crash, or whenever an officer asks during a traffic stop, you have to show proof of insurance. A phone screen counts, and the officer may look only at the policy, not at anything else on the device. Penalties for driving uninsured:

  • First offense: a mandatory minimum fine of $500. The court can suspend half of it if you show you've since bought insurance. The court can also order at least 40 hours of community service.
  • Second or later offense within five years: at least $1,000. Half can be suspended only if you show proof that you've bought insurance.

If you were insured but couldn't show proof at the stop, the charge is dismissed once you give the court proof of a policy that was valid at the time. The court clerk can confirm coverage through the state's uninsured motorist database.

Your rights when you file a claim

  • You choose the repair shop. Under C.R.S. § 10-4-120, an insurer can recommend preferred shops and guarantee their work, but it can't make you use them. It pays reasonable market costs and may refuse excessive charges such as inflated storage fees.
  • Diminished value isn't addressed by statute. The Division notes that no Colorado insurance law covers loss-of-value claims, so any recovery depends on case law and negotiation.
  • Claim deadlines. State law sets no fixed response time for insurers, but the Division can step in when delays become unreasonable.
  • Rates. The Division doesn't set premiums. Insurers file rates based on their own loss and expense data, and Division staff check that the filings are supported. The Division's Premium Comparison Tool and complaint index can help you shop.

What Coloradans pay

According to the NAIC's 2024 auto insurance database supplement, Colorado's combined average premium (liability, collision, and comprehensive together) was $1,835.17, compared with $1,612.81 nationwide. The NAIC's average expenditure, which also counts liability-only drivers, was $1,601.22. The Insurance Research Council estimated that 19.7% of Colorado drivers were uninsured in 2023, the ninth-highest rate in the country (as reported by the Insurance Information Institute). That's in line with the Division's own 15–20% estimate. The Division lists inflation, more complex repairs, hail and other climate losses, fraud, and distracted driving as reasons premiums keep rising.
