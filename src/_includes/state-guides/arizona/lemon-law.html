Arizona's lemon law at a glance

Arizona's new-vehicle lemon law is Title 44, Chapter 9, Article 5 of the Arizona Revised Statutes, A.R.S. §§ 44-1261 through 44-1265. It's one of the shorter lemon laws in the country, and it leaves the remedy to the courts: no state agency hears cases or runs an arbitration board. Arizona does go further than most states in one way. A neighboring article, § 44-1267, gives a narrow warranty right to buyers of used cars from dealers. It's covered at the end of this page.

Who and what is covered

The law protects a "consumer," which § 44-1261 defines broadly. It covers the buyer (other than a buyer for resale), a lessee, anyone the vehicle is transferred to while the express warranty is still running, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it. A "motor vehicle" is any self-propelled vehicle designed mainly to carry people or property on public highways. Three exclusions apply:

  • vehicles with a declared gross weight over 10,000 pounds;
  • vehicles sold at a public auction; and
  • the living quarters of a motor home. The chassis and drivetrain are covered, but the parts "designed, used or maintained primarily as a mobile dwelling, office or commercial space" are not (§ 44-1261(B)).

The coverage window

Under § 44-1262, you have to report the defect to the manufacturer, its agent, or an authorized dealer during the term of the express warranty or within two years or 24,000 miles from original delivery, whichever comes first. If you report it in time, the manufacturer has to make the repairs needed to bring the car into line with the warranty, even if the work isn't done until after the period ends.

The presumption: four repairs or 30 days

A manufacturer that can't fix a defect that substantially impairs the car's use and market value after a reasonable number of attempts must replace the car or refund the price (§ 44-1263). Arizona sets the bar higher than many states. Under § 44-1264, the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the coverage window:

  1. the same nonconformity was repaired four or more times by the manufacturer, its agents, or its authorized dealers and still exists; or
  2. the car was out of service for repairs for a total of 30 or more calendar days.

The 30-day count is extended for war, invasion, strikes, fire, floods, and other natural disasters.

Direct written notice to the manufacturer

This is where many Arizona claims fall apart. Under § 44-1264, the presumption doesn't apply against a manufacturer unless it has received direct written notice of the defect from you and has had a chance to fix it. Telling the dealer isn't enough. Send a letter to the manufacturer's customer-relations or zone office at the address in your warranty booklet. Certified mail with a return receipt gives you proof that it arrived. Describe the defect, list each repair date and the dealer that did the work, and say plainly that you want the car repaired or bought back under A.R.S. § 44-1263. If the manufacturer asks to inspect the car or try a final repair, let it. Refusing gives the manufacturer an easy defense.

Manufacturer arbitration comes first

Arizona has no state arbitration program. Under § 44-1265(A), though, if the manufacturer has or takes part in an informal dispute settlement procedure that meets every requirement of the FTC's 16 CFR Part 703 rule, you have to use it before the refund-or-replacement remedy is available. The warranty booklet names the program. File with it, keep its decision, and remember that the lawsuit deadline below keeps running while the program works.

Replacement or refund

Under § 44-1263, the manufacturer must either replace the car with a new one or take it back and refund the full purchase price. The refund includes all collateral charges, minus a reasonable allowance for your use of the car. Arizona doesn't set a mileage formula for that allowance, so it's negotiated or decided by a court. Refunds are split between you and any lienholder as your interests appear. Sales tax is handled directly. On a buyback, the manufacturer has to refund the transaction privilege tax tied to your purchase. If it replaces the car with a cheaper one, it refunds the difference in tax. The manufacturer can then claim that amount back from the Arizona Department of Revenue under § 42-1118(F), so it has no reason to hold the tax back.

The manufacturer can defend by showing that the defect doesn't substantially impair use and market value, or that it came from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes to the vehicle.

Deadline to sue and attorney fees

File suit within six months after the earlier of the end of the express warranty or two years or 24,000 miles from original delivery (§ 44-1265(B)). On a typical three-year warranty, that means roughly 30 months after delivery, or six months after you hit 24,000 miles if that comes first. A consumer who wins is awarded reasonable costs and attorney fees.

Used cars: the 15-day, 500-mile implied warranty

Arizona also protects used-car buyers, in a separate statute: A.R.S. § 44-1267. A used motor vehicle dealer can't disclaim the implied warranty of merchantability, or limit the remedies for breaking it, until midnight of the 15th calendar day after delivery or 500 miles, whichever comes first. The days the car spends broken down don't count toward the 15. The warranty is met if the car is safe under Arizona's equipment laws and substantially free of any defect that significantly limits its use for ordinary driving. It doesn't cover damage after the sale from abuse, neglect, missed maintenance, low fluids, off-road use, racing, or towing.

If the car breaks within that window, give the dealer reasonable notice and a chance to repair it. You pay half the cost of each of the first two repairs, capped at $25 per repair. The dealer's total liability is capped at the purchase price. The sale contract has to print this warranty in bold 10-point type. If it doesn't, you can void the contract. You can waive the warranty only for a specific defect that's disclosed to you under the conditions the statute sets.

Getting help

The Arizona Attorney General's Office takes consumer complaints at 602-542-5763 (Phoenix), 520-628-6648 (Tucson), or 1-800-352-8431 elsewhere in Arizona, and through the online complaint form. The main office is at 2005 N. Central Ave., Phoenix, AZ 85004. The lemon-law remedy itself is a private lawsuit. The statute makes the manufacturer pay your attorney fees if you win, so ask a lawyer how they bill a lemon case before you sign anything. Bring every repair order, your notice letter and its return receipt, and the decision from the manufacturer's dispute program.
