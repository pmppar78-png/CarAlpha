How Florida's lemon law works

Florida's lemon law is Chapter 681 of the Florida Statutes, the Motor Vehicle Warranty Enforcement Act. The Attorney General's Lemon Law Arbitration Division runs it. The law has strict timing rules and a notice requirement, so you need to know the steps before your second year of ownership ends.

What vehicles are covered

The law covers a new vehicle sold in Florida to carry people or property, including demonstrators and leased vehicles that came with a manufacturer's warranty. Recreational vehicles are covered, except for their living quarters (plumbing, furnace, generator, roof air conditioner and similar parts). It doesn't cover motorcycles, mopeds, electric bicycles, off-road vehicles, or trucks over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight.

A "consumer" is anyone who bought the vehicle for personal, family or household use (not for resale), anyone it was transferred to for those uses during the rights period, and anyone else the warranty allows to enforce it. A lessee qualifies if the lease runs a year or more and makes the lessee responsible for repairs, or if it's a lease-purchase agreement.

The 24-month rights period

The Lemon Law rights period ends 24 months after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer. There's no mileage limit. You must first report the defect to the manufacturer or its authorized service agent (usually the dealer) within those 24 months. If you do, the manufacturer has to keep repairing it even after the period ends. The defect has to substantially impair the vehicle's use, value or safety. Problems caused by an accident, abuse, neglect, or changes made by someone other than the manufacturer or its agent aren't covered.

When a car is presumed a lemon

Under § 681.104, the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if either of these happens during the rights period:

  • The same defect has been repaired at least three times, the manufacturer has had a final repair attempt, and the problem is still there.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for 30 or more cumulative days (60 for an RV), not counting routine maintenance. This can cover one defect or several.

The 30-day count can be extended for any time repairs were unavailable because of war, a strike, fire, flood or a natural disaster. These presumptions aren't the only path. The Arbitration Board must grant relief whenever a reasonable number of attempts have been made.

Two written notices you may have to send

Florida requires notice by mail to the manufacturer, and notice to the dealer doesn't count. You send it at two points:

  1. After the third repair attempt on the same problem, send written notice by registered or express mail. The Attorney General's office also accepts certified mail. This gives the manufacturer a final chance to fix the car. It has 10 days from receiving your notice to send you to a reasonably accessible repair shop, and then 10 days from when you deliver the car (45 for an RV) to fix it. If the manufacturer doesn't respond or doesn't finish the repair in time, the final-attempt requirement no longer applies.
  2. After 15 cumulative days out of service, send written notice so the manufacturer has a chance to inspect or repair the vehicle. The manufacturer must have had at least one such opportunity before the 30-day presumption applies.

The address to use should be in your warranty booklet or owner's manual, which must list the manufacturer's regional office for Florida. The Attorney General offers a Motor Vehicle Defect Notification form you can use. Keep the mailing receipt.

Keep every repair order

Each time the car comes back from warranty work, the service agent must give you an itemized, legible repair order. It must list the problem you reported, the diagnosis, the parts and labor, any test drive and roughly how long it was, the odometer reading, and the dates the car went in and came out. Check every order before you leave the shop. Keep receipts for towing, rentals and other costs related to the defect too. Florida counts them as incidental charges you can recover.

Refund or replacement, your choice

If the defect can't be fixed, the manufacturer has 40 days to either buy the vehicle back and refund the full purchase price, or replace it with an acceptable comparable vehicle. Either way, it must include collateral charges (sales tax, title charges, finance charges earned, dealer-installed items) and incidental charges. Florida gives you an unconditional right to choose the refund over a replacement. On a lease, you get back what you paid and the lessor gets the rest, and you can't be charged an early termination penalty.

The reasonable offset for use is the miles you drove up to the settlement or hearing date, times the base price on the invoice (not counting taxes and fees), divided by 120,000 (60,000 for an RV). For example, a $42,000 car driven 9,000 miles would have an offset of $3,150. If you traded in a vehicle, the trade-in counts at the allowance on the contract. If either side disputes that figure, it counts at 100% of the NADA retail value.

State arbitration comes first

You can't go straight to court. Under § 681.1095, a consumer must first take the dispute to the Attorney General's office, and then to the Florida New Motor Vehicle Arbitration Board if it's found eligible. The order depends on your manufacturer:

  • If the manufacturer has a state-certified program (such as BBB AUTO LINE, NCDS or CAP Motors) and told you about it in writing when you bought the car, you must file there first, within 60 days after the rights period ends. If that program doesn't decide within 40 days, or you're unhappy with the result, you can then go to the state board. The manufacturer can't appeal its own program's decision.
  • If there's no certified program, or the program can't hear your dispute, apply directly to the Attorney General's office.

The Request for Arbitration form must arrive no later than 60 days after the 24-month rights period ends, or 30 days after a certified program's final action, whichever is later. Mail the signed original, with copies of the documents it asks for, to Office of the Attorney General, Lemon Law Arbitration Screening, PL-01, The Capitol, Tallahassee, FL 32399-1050. Staff will screen it within 20 days. If they ask for more information, you have 30 days to send it.

If your claim is accepted, a panel of three board members hears it within 40 days and decides within 60 days of approval. Hearings are held around the state, and you can bring witnesses and a lawyer. At least one board member in each region must know vehicle mechanics. If you win, the manufacturer has 40 days to comply.

Appeals and penalties

Either side can appeal to circuit court within 30 days, and the case is heard fresh (de novo). If the court upholds your award, you also get attorney's fees, costs, and $25 a day for every day past the 40-day compliance deadline. If the court finds the manufacturer appealed in bad faith or just to harass you, it must double the total award and may triple it. If a manufacturer neither complies nor appeals, the state can seek fines of up to $1,000 a day.

If the Attorney General's office rejects your claim, you can sue directly under § 681.112. The court awards a winning consumer their losses, costs and reasonable attorney's fees. You must file within one year after the rights period ends, or within one year after the final action of the program, department or board if you used one.

Buying a returned vehicle

Under § 681.114, a manufacturer that takes back a vehicle must report its VIN to the state within 10 days. Nobody may knowingly sell or lease that vehicle again, including one bought back under another state's law, without clearly disclosing the defect. The manufacturer must also warrant the fix for 1 year or 12,000 miles. If you're shopping for a used car, ask for the vehicle history and look for that disclosure.

Contact

Call the Lemon Law Hotline at 1-800-321-5366 (850-414-3500 from outside Florida) for the forms, a list of certified manufacturer programs, or questions about the process. Dealers must give you the state's written statement of lemon law rights, which lists this number, when you buy the car.
