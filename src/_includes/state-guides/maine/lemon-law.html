The Maine Lemon Law at a glance

Chapter 203-A of Title 10, which § 1161-A names "the Maine Lemon Law," requires a manufacturer to replace or refund a vehicle it can't bring into line with its express warranty. The Attorney General's office also runs a free arbitration program that manufacturers must take part in. According to the Attorney General's Lemon Law guide, the law covers new or used cars, motorcycles, vans, trucks and RVs bought or leased in Maine that are still under an express warranty.

Who and what is covered

Under the definitions in § 1161, a "consumer" includes:

  • Anyone who buys a vehicle for a purpose other than resale
  • A lessee
  • Anyone the vehicle is transferred to while an express warranty still applies
  • Anyone else the warranty's terms allow to enforce it

Government entities aren't consumers under the law, and neither are businesses that register three or more vehicles. A "motor vehicle" is any motor-driven vehicle designed to carry passengers or property on public highways and sold or leased in Maine. Vehicles used mainly for commercial purposes with a gross vehicle weight of 8,500 pounds or more are excluded. A "manufacturer" includes an importer, a distributor, or anyone named as the warrantor on an express written warranty.

When you must report the problem

Under § 1163(1), the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty. You must report the defect during whichever of these ends first:

  • The term of the express warranties
  • Three years after the vehicle was first delivered to a consumer
  • The first 18,000 miles of operation

The AG's guide explains that the mileage is the vehicle's total odometer reading, not just the miles you drove. As long as you reported the defect in time, the duty to repair continues even if the repairs happen after the period ends. The warranty term, the 18,000 miles, the three years and the 15-day out-of-service count are all extended for any time repair service isn't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

How many repair attempts count

A manufacturer must replace or refund the vehicle when, after a reasonable number of attempts, it still can't fix a defect or combination of defects that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, safety or value. Under § 1163(3), the number of attempts is presumed reasonable in any of these situations, within the warranty term or the protection period, whichever ends first:

  • The same problem has been repaired three or more times and still exists.
  • The same problem has caused a serious failure of the brakes or steering and has been repaired at least once.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs of any defect or combination of defects for a total of 15 or more business days.

The AG's guide notes that the law doesn't list which defects qualify. Problems that are annoying but don't substantially affect the use, safety or value of the vehicle aren't enough. You don't have to wait an unreasonable amount of time for repairs, but you do have to give the manufacturer a chance unless it agrees the defect can't be fixed.

The written notice and final repair

Under § 1163(3-A), once the repairs have failed, you must notify the manufacturer or the authorized dealer in writing that you want a refund or replacement. After a serious brake or steering failure, you can send this notice after a single repair attempt. The manufacturer then has seven business days after it receives the notice to make a final repair, at a facility reasonably accessible to you. That final attempt doesn't pause the deadline for the manufacturer to provide an arbitration hearing.

Two rules soften the notice requirement. Under § 1163(6), you only have to give the manufacturer written notice if the warranty or owner's manual clearly says so and gives the name and address to send it to. Under § 1163(6-A), written notice to the authorized dealer also counts, and the dealer must pass it on to the manufacturer right away. The AG's office publishes a sample notice letter and says the State Lemon Law Arbitration Program won't accept your application without this notice.

Refund or replacement

The manufacturer may offer a comparable replacement vehicle, but you can turn it down and take a refund instead. A refund must include:

  • The full purchase price, or for a lease the lease payments made so far, including any finance charges paid
  • All collateral charges, such as sales tax, registration fees and similar government charges
  • Reasonable costs for towing, storage and alternative transportation while the vehicle couldn't be driven because of the defect

The manufacturer can subtract a reasonable allowance for use. Under § 1161(4), that allowance is capped at the lower of two amounts. The first is one-third of the IRS business mileage rate, applied to the mileage on your arbitration application plus your use beyond 20,000 miles. The second is 10% of the purchase price. The AG's guide adds that attorney's fees, lost time and lost wages aren't part of the refund. Refunds go to you and any lienholder according to your interests at the time. For a lease, they go to the lessor and lessee. Under § 1168, a lease ends once the vehicle is found not to conform and is returned, and the lessee owes no further lease charges.

State-certified arbitration

Under § 1169, every manufacturer must take part in state-certified arbitration if you request it and the state accepts your application. You must request it within three years of the vehicle's original delivery or within the express warranty term, whichever ends first. The arbitrators are neutral and chosen by the Attorney General's office, and each side can reject one proposed arbitrator. The written decision is due within 45 days after the AG's office receives your completed request, or 5 days later if the arbitrator orders an independent evaluation of the vehicle. The arbitrator can also award you reasonable fees for a mechanic or engineer who prepared a notarized report or testified for you.

The AG's program is free. A $1 fee, collected by dealers on each new vehicle sale, helps fund it, and a matching $1 on used vehicle sales supports the state's consumer mediation service. Before you apply or send documents, the AG's office asks you to email lemon.law@maine.gov or call 207-626-8848.

After the decision

If you win, the manufacturer has 21 days after receiving the decision to deliver the refund or replacement or to appeal to Superior Court. It can ask the AG's office for more time to deliver a replacement if it has good cause. An appeal by either side must be filed within 21 days in the Superior Court of the county where the sale happened, and the court hears the case from scratch. If the court upholds an award to you, you can recover up to $25 a day for each day after you returned the vehicle that it was out of use, unless the manufacturer gave you a comparable vehicle free of charge. A consumer who wins on appeal must be awarded reasonable attorney's fees and costs, and the court must double the total award if the manufacturer's appeal was frivolous or had no reasonable basis.

If the manufacturer neither complies nor appeals, a consumer who sues to enforce the decision is entitled to at least twice the actual award. The only exceptions are a failure beyond the manufacturer's control or a written agreement with you. Under § 1169(10), not delivering or appealing within 21 days is also prima facie evidence of an unfair trade practice. If you lose, you can try the manufacturer's own dispute program, if you haven't already, or appeal to Superior Court within 21 days.

Other rules worth knowing

  • Manufacturer programs: Under § 1165, if a manufacturer has a dispute procedure that fully complies with 16 CFR Part 703, you must first use either that procedure or state arbitration before the refund and replacement rules apply. The requirement is satisfied 40 days after you notify the program, or sooner if it finishes its work first.
  • No waivers: Under § 1162, any agreement that waives or limits these rights is void, and the law doesn't take away rights you have under other laws.
  • Defenses: Under § 1164, a manufacturer can defend a claim by showing the defect doesn't substantially impair the vehicle, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized changes made by someone other than the manufacturer or its dealers.
  • Unfair trade practice and fees: A violation of the chapter is prima facie evidence of an unfair or deceptive trade practice (§ 1166), and a court may award attorney's fees to a consumer who wins (§ 1167).
  • Records: If you ask, the manufacturer or dealer must give you all repair records and reports on your vehicle, including inspection, diagnosis and test-drive reports, plus technical bulletins for your make and model.
  • Resale disclosure: A returned vehicle can't be resold without a written disclosure that it was returned under the law and how it failed to conform. Its title is branded under 29-A M.R.S. § 670. A vehicle surrendered in an arbitration settlement must carry a notice when it's first offered for retail sale.
  • Dealers: Dealers aren't liable under this law, except for written warranties they made themselves.

For recall and defect questions, the AG's guide points to NHTSA at 1-888-327-4236.
