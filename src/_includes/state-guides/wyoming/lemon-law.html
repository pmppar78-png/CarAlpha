Wyoming's lemon law

Wyoming's lemon law is one section of the statutes, Wyo. Stat. Ann. § 40-17-101, titled definitions, express warranties and duty to make warranty repairs. It sets a repair duty for new vehicles, a presumption for when repairs have gone on long enough, and a refund or replacement remedy. It also lets an injured consumer sue for attorney's fees.

Who and what is covered

Under § 40-17-101(a)(i), a "consumer" is anyone who:

  • Buys a motor vehicle with an express warranty, other than for resale
  • Receives the vehicle by transfer while an express warranty is in effect
  • Is entitled under the warranty's terms to enforce it

A "motor vehicle" is any self-propelled vehicle under 10,000 pounds unladen weight that is sold or registered in Wyoming, except vehicles moved solely by human power. The warranty the law refers to is the manufacturer's written warranty, labeled as such, for a new motor vehicle, including any terms or conditions that must be met before the manufacturer's obligations can be enforced.

The duty to repair

Under § 40-17-101(b), if a new vehicle doesn't conform to all applicable express warranties, report the nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer within one year after the vehicle was originally delivered to you. The manufacturer, agent or dealer must then make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity with the warranties. Those repairs have to be made even after the one-year period runs out, as long as the report came in time.

When a reasonable number of attempts is presumed

Under § 40-17-101(d), a reasonable number of repair attempts is presumed if, within one year after original delivery, either of these happens:

  • The same nonconformity has been subject to repair more than three times by the manufacturer, its agents or its authorized dealers, and it still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative total of 30 business days.

The text of subsection (d) includes the phrase "whichever is later," although it names one period.

The periods in subsection (d) are extended by any time the vehicle couldn't reasonably be repaired because of war, invasion, an act of terror, civil unrest, a strike, fire, flood or natural disaster, under § 40-17-101(j).

Written notice to the manufacturer

Under § 40-17-101(h), the presumption can't be used against a manufacturer unless it first received direct written notification from you or someone acting for you and had a reasonable opportunity to cure the defect. A report to a dealer under subsection (b) is a separate step. Keep a copy of the letter and proof of when the manufacturer received it.

Refund or replacement

Under § 40-17-101(c), if the manufacturer, its agents or its authorized dealers can't conform the vehicle to the warranty after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must do one of two things. The defect has to be one that substantially impairs the use and fair market value of the vehicle to you.

  • Replace it with a new or comparable vehicle of the same type and similarly equipped.
  • Accept return of the vehicle and refund you and any lienholder, as your interests appear, the full purchase price including all collateral charges, less a reasonable allowance for your use.

The use allowance is defined in § 40-17-101(a)(iii). It covers use of the vehicle before you first reported the nonconformity to the manufacturer, agent or dealer. It also covers any later period when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. Time the vehicle spent in the shop doesn't count against you.

Manufacturer defenses

Under § 40-17-101(g), the manufacturer has an affirmative defense to a claim in either of these situations:

  • The alleged nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use and fair market value.
  • The nonconformity results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration of the vehicle by a consumer.

Informal dispute settlement

Under § 40-17-101(f), the refund and replacement remedy in subsection (c) isn't available to a consumer who hasn't exhausted the manufacturer's informal dispute settlement procedure. That rule applies when such a procedure exists and complies with the applicable federal statute and regulation. The federal regulation for warranty dispute programs is 16 CFR Part 703, issued under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act.

Part 703 sets these requirements for a qualifying program:

  • It can't charge consumers a fee to use it.
  • It must be insulated from the manufacturer so that the manufacturer doesn't influence decisions or staff.
  • No member deciding a dispute can be a party to it, an employee or agent of a party, or someone who is or may become a party to a lawsuit about the product, apart from the role of deciding disputes.
  • When one or two members decide a dispute, none can have direct involvement in the manufacture, distribution, sale or service of any product. With three or more members, at least two-thirds must have no such involvement.
  • It must normally decide a dispute within 40 days of being notified, and the decision may include repair, replacement, refund, reimbursement of expenses or other remedies.
  • Its decisions aren't legally binding on anyone, although the manufacturer must act in good faith in deciding whether to follow them. A decision is admissible as evidence in a later lawsuit.
  • It must be audited at least once a year, with the audit report sent to the Federal Trade Commission.

Under Part 703, the manufacturer has to disclose the program on the face of the written warranty, including its name and address or a telephone number consumers can use without charge. It also has to disclose any requirement that you use the program first. Either party can get access to all records of its own dispute, and copies at reasonable cost.

For federal Magnuson-Moss claims, § 703.5(i) treats the requirement to use the program first as satisfied 40 days after the program is notified of the dispute, or when it finishes its duties, whichever comes sooner. Section 40-17-101(f) uses its own wording, exhausting the procedure, and doesn't cross-reference that rule.

If you take a dispute directly to the manufacturer, § 703.2(e) requires it to decide within a reasonable time whether and how far it will satisfy you. Its answer must include the program disclosures. Program meetings to hear and decide disputes are open to observers on reasonable terms, and the program keeps each dispute's records for at least four years.

Going to court

Under § 40-17-101(k), any consumer injured by a violation of the section can bring a civil action to enforce it and recover reasonable attorney's fees from the manufacturer that issued the express warranty. Under subsection (e), the section doesn't limit your rights or remedies under any other statute.
