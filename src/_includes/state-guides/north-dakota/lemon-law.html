North Dakota's lemon law

North Dakota's lemon law is in N.D.C.C. §§ 51-07-16 through 51-07-22, part of the chapter on miscellaneous trade practices. It requires a manufacturer to repair a new passenger vehicle that doesn't conform to its express warranty, and to replace it or refund the price if a defect can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts. The full chapter is published as a PDF on the Legislative Branch website.

Who and what is covered

Under § 51-07-16, a "consumer" is someone who buys or leases a passenger vehicle, other than for resale or re-lease, that's normally used for personal, family or household purposes. The definition also includes anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while an express warranty is still in effect, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it. So a second owner who takes over the car during the warranty term can use the law.

A covered "passenger motor vehicle" is a passenger vehicle as defined in § 39-01-01, or a truck with a registered gross weight of 10,000 pounds or less, sold or leased in North Dakota. House cars, the code's term for motor homes, are excluded. Because the law covers vehicles used for personal, family or household purposes, a vehicle bought mainly for business use may fall outside it.

The repair duty

Section 51-07-17 applies when you report a nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer during the express warranty term or the year after original delivery, whichever ends first. The manufacturer then has to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into conformity, even if the work happens after that period ends. That earlier-date rule matters: with a three-year warranty, the window for reporting under the lemon law closes one year after delivery.

When a "reasonable number of attempts" is presumed

Section 51-07-19 presumes the manufacturer has had enough chances if either of these happened within the express warranty term or one year after delivery, whichever is earlier:

  • The same nonconformity has been subject to repair more than three times and still exists, which in practice means a fourth repair visit hasn't fixed it, or
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repair for a cumulative 30 business days or more.

The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30-day count are all extended for any period when repairs aren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster. The statute doesn't set a mileage limit for the presumption.

The presumption doesn't apply against the manufacturer unless it received direct notice from you, or someone acting for you, and had a chance to cure the defect. Send the manufacturer written notice at the address in your warranty booklet or owner's manual.

Substantial impairment

The refund or replacement remedy covers a defect or condition that substantially impairs the vehicle's use and market value. Under § 51-07-18(2), it's an affirmative defense that the problem doesn't substantially impair use and market value, or that it resulted from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations by a consumer.

Refund or replacement

If the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer can't fix the defect after a reasonable number of attempts, § 51-07-18(1) requires the manufacturer to replace the vehicle with a comparable one or take it back and refund the full purchase price, including all collateral charges. Refunds go to you, any lessor and any lienholder as their interests appear.

The deduction for use

The refund can be reduced by a reasonable allowance for your use of the vehicle. The allowance is capped at the lesser of 10 cents per mile driven or 10% of the purchase price. It counts use before your first report of the problem and use during later periods when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair.

For example, on a $40,000 purchase with 6,000 qualifying miles, 10 cents a mile works out to $600, which is less than the $4,000 that 10% of the price would come to. The deduction couldn't exceed $600.

Leased vehicles

Section 51-07-18.1 splits a lease refund. You, the lessee, get back all payments made to the lessor, including cash payments, security deposits and any trade-in allowance, less the reasonable allowance for use. The lessor receives its actual purchase cost minus your payments, plus freight and installed accessories if applicable, plus 5% of its purchase cost. That 5% replaces any early termination charges in the lease. When the vehicle is returned, the lease ends and no early termination penalty can be charged.

Informal dispute programs come first

Under § 51-07-18(3), if the manufacturer has set up or participates in an informal dispute settlement procedure that substantially complies with the Federal Trade Commission's rules at 16 C.F.R. part 703, or takes part in a consumer and industry appeals, arbitration or mediation board whose decisions bind the manufacturer, you have to use it before you can claim a refund or replacement. If you ask for an oral presentation, the hearing must take place in the state where you live. The Attorney General will, on application, determine whether a dispute program qualifies.

The deadline to sue

Section 51-07-21 requires a lemon law lawsuit to be filed within six months after the earlier of:

  • The end of the express warranty term, or
  • Eighteen months after original delivery.

For a vehicle with a warranty longer than 18 months, that means the case has to be filed no later than 24 months after delivery. Keep that date in view while a claim is in a manufacturer's dispute program.

Choosing the lemon law

Section 51-07-20 makes this an exclusive remedy. If you elect to proceed under the lemon law, you can't pursue any other remedy arising from the same facts. You may want to talk with a lawyer about how a lemon law claim compares with a federal Magnuson-Moss or a state-law warranty claim before you choose.

Building your file

Because the presumption depends on repair counts and days out of service within a short window, get a dated repair order each time the vehicle goes in, even when the shop can't find the problem. Each one should state your complaint, the arrival date and the date the vehicle was ready. Keep copies of the purchase or lease contract, the warranty, your written notice to the manufacturer and anything you receive from a dispute program.

Resale of returned vehicles

Under § 51-07-22, a vehicle returned under the lemon law can't be sold or leased in North Dakota unless the manufacturer gives the new buyer the same express warranty, running at least 12,000 miles or 12 months after resale, whichever is earlier. The buyer must also receive a separate statement, signed by the manufacturer and the buyer in 10-point capital letters, saying the vehicle was returned because warranty defects weren't repaired within a reasonable time under North Dakota law. A returned vehicle shipped to another state for resale or lease requires full disclosure of the reasons for return. A violation is a class B misdemeanor.
