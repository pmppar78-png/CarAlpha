Minnesota Statutes section 325F.665

Minnesota's new motor vehicle lemon law is Minn. Stat. § 325F.665. It requires manufacturers to repair warranty defects. When they can't, after a reasonable number of tries, they must replace the vehicle or refund it. Before you can sue for a refund or replacement, you generally have to use the manufacturer's arbitration program, which every manufacturer selling here must run in Minnesota. The law doesn't limit any other rights you have, and it doesn't make dealers liable.

Who and what is covered

You're a consumer if you bought or leased a new motor vehicle, other than for resale or sublease, and use it for personal, family or household purposes at least 40% of the time. Someone it's transferred to for those purposes while the express warranty is in effect is also covered. A lease counts if it runs more than four months, whether or not you can buy the vehicle at the end. Licensed ambulance services are covered for the ambulance chassis or van.

A motor vehicle here means:

  • a passenger automobile, including pickups and vans
  • the self-propelled chassis or van portion of a recreational vehicle sold or leased in Minnesota
  • the chassis or van portion of an ambulance

The living area of a motor home isn't part of the definition. The law applies to manufacturers that distribute at least ten new vehicles a year to dealers.

Two years or the warranty, whichever ends first

The manufacturer, its agent or an authorized dealer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the express warranty. That duty applies if you reported the problem during the warranty term or within two years of the original delivery, whichever ends first. The repairs have to be made even if they happen after that period ends. The statute doesn't set a mileage limit, but a warranty that expires by mileage closes the window then.

Four repairs, 30 business days, or one brake or steering failure

A reasonable number of attempts is presumed in any of these situations within the same window:

  • The same nonconformity has been repaired four or more times and still exists.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a total of 30 or more business days.
  • A nonconformity causes a complete failure of the braking or steering system that's likely to cause death or serious injury, has been repaired at least once, and still exists.

The four-repair and 30-day presumptions apply only if the manufacturer, its agent or its dealer received written notice from you at least once and had a chance to fix the defect. A dealer or agent that gets your notice must send it to the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested. The warranty term, the two years and the 30 days are extended while repairs aren't available because of war, invasion, strike, fire, flood or other natural disaster.

There's a three-year extension. If you first reported the problem while the warranty was in effect, the repair attempts that make up the reasonable number can happen within three years of delivery, and you can still get a refund or replacement.

At the time of purchase or lease, the manufacturer must give you a separate notice in capital letters. It says you may be entitled to a refund or replacement, that you must first notify the manufacturer or dealer in writing and allow a repair, and that you can use the manufacturer's consumer arbitration program.

What a refund includes

If the defect substantially impairs the vehicle's use or market value and can't be fixed, the manufacturer must replace it with a comparable vehicle or take it back and refund the full purchase price. You can reject a replacement and insist on the refund. The refund includes:

  • options and modifications arranged, installed or made by the manufacturer or dealer within 30 days after delivery
  • sales or excise tax, license fees and registration fees
  • towing and rental costs from warranty repairs
  • the dealer's stated trade-in value of your old vehicle, plus whatever else you paid

The manufacturer may subtract a reasonable allowance for your use. It's capped at the lesser of 10 cents a mile or 10% of the purchase price, and it counts only use during periods when the vehicle's use and market value weren't substantially impaired. The tax refund is reduced in the same proportion as the use allowance. You must get an itemized statement, and the refund goes to you and any lienholder on the registrar's records. If the tax isn't stated separately, or the manufacturer doesn't apply for its own tax refund within a year, the Department of Public Safety may refund the tax to you directly.

The manufacturer can defend a claim by showing the problem doesn't substantially impair use or market value. It can also show the problem came from abuse, neglect, or modifications by someone other than the manufacturer or its dealer.

Leased vehicles

A lessee has the same rights as a buyer, except that the remedy is a refund; a replacement isn't offered. The vehicle goes back to the manufacturer and the lease ends. You get back what you actually paid on the lease, plus the charges listed above, minus the use allowance. The lessor gets the original purchase price plus early termination costs, capped at 15% of that price, less what you paid.

The required arbitration program

Every manufacturer doing business in Minnesota must run or join an informal dispute settlement mechanism located in the state that complies with 16 C.F.R. part 703. The program has to follow these rules:

  • It must give you and the arbitrators information about the lemon law, as directed by the Attorney General.
  • It can't receive documents unless both parties had them beforehand. You may ask for a postponement to review anything new.
  • You can make an oral presentation in Minnesota, or agree to a decision on documents or by phone. If you choose documents only, manufacturer and dealer representatives can't take part in deciding.
  • You can have the manufacturer's claim that a problem is "within specifications" appraised by a technical expert you choose and pay for.
  • If the manufacturer knows of a technical service bulletin on the problem, it must provide it at a reasonable cost.
  • Any fee charged to you can't exceed the conciliation court filing fee in that county, and either side may bring a lawyer.

The decision isn't binding unless the parties agree it is. Either side can take it to district court for a new trial. A manufacturer that disagrees must file within 30 days of receiving the decision, or the court will confirm it on request. If a court finds a party appealed in bad faith, the other side gets three times its actual damages, plus costs and attorney fees.

Going to court

A consumer harmed by a violation can sue to enforce the law and recover costs and reasonable attorney fees. The Attorney General can also sue manufacturers. A lawsuit must be filed within three years of the original delivery. If you applied to arbitration within those three years and are unhappy with the result, you have six months after the final decision.

Returned vehicles

A vehicle returned under this law, or a similar law elsewhere, can be resold or re-leased in Minnesota only with the same express warranty the first buyer got. That warranty may be shortened to 12,000 miles or 12 months after the resale, whichever comes first. It also needs a written disclosure that it was returned for an uncured nonconformity. A vehicle returned for a complete brake or steering failure can't be resold in Minnesota at all.
