The Motor Vehicle Quality Assurance Act

New Mexico's lemon law is the Motor Vehicle Quality Assurance Act, NMSA 1978 §§ 57-16A-1 to 57-16A-9, enacted in 1985. It makes a manufacturer repair a new vehicle that doesn't meet its express warranty, and replace or buy back the vehicle when repairs fail. A 2003 amendment added a short implied warranty for used vehicles sold by dealers. The full text is on the state's NMOneSource site in Chapter 57. The act doesn't set up a state hearing board, so a claim that isn't settled goes through the manufacturer's dispute program, if it has a qualifying one, and then to court.

Covered vehicles and buyers

Section 57-16A-2 defines a "motor vehicle" as a passenger vehicle, including a car, pickup, motorcycle or van, normally used for personal, family or household purposes, sold and registered in New Mexico, with a gross vehicle weight under 10,000 pounds. A "consumer" is a buyer who didn't purchase for resale, anyone the vehicle is transferred to while an express warranty still applies, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it. The Court of Appeals held in Jones v. GMC (1998) that a buyer who doesn't use the vehicle for personal, family or household purposes isn't a consumer under the act.

A "manufacturer" includes anyone who builds, assembles, imports or distributes vehicles as a regular business. The consumer definition speaks of purchasers and transferees and doesn't address leases, so a lessee should read the warranty and the lease terms closely.

Report the problem in the first year

Under § 57-16A-3(A), you have to report the nonconformity to the manufacturer, its agent or its authorized dealer during the express warranty term or within one year after the original delivery, whichever ends first. When the report is made in time, the manufacturer has to make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty. The act has no mileage limit, so a high-mileage first year still counts.

Four repairs or 30 business days

Section 57-16A-3(C) presumes the manufacturer has had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the warranty term or the first year after delivery, whichever ends first:

  • The same uncorrected nonconformity has been subject to repair four or more times by the manufacturer, its agents or its authorized dealers and still exists, or
  • The vehicle has been in the possession of the manufacturer, an agent or a dealer for repair for a cumulative 30 or more business days, not counting down time for routine maintenance the manufacturer prescribes.

The warranty term, the one-year period and the 30 days are all extended for any time repair service isn't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

Written notice to the manufacturer

The presumption can't be used against a manufacturer until it has received direct written notice from you or on your behalf and has had a chance to cure the defect. The statute also puts a duty on the manufacturer: it has to tell you in writing, in the warranty or a separate notice, that this written notice is required before you can use the act's remedies. Send the letter to the address in the warranty booklet, keep a copy, and use a delivery method that gives you proof of receipt. Keep every repair order, since they show the dates the vehicle was in and out of the shop.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer, agent or dealer can't make the vehicle conform after a reasonable number of attempts, and the defect substantially impairs its use and market value to you, § 57-16A-3(B) gives the manufacturer two choices:

  • Replace it with a comparable vehicle, meaning an identical or reasonably equivalent one.
  • Refund the full purchase price plus all collateral charges, which § 57-16A-2 defines to include taxes, license, title and registration fees and other government charges tied to the purchase.

Either way, a reasonable allowance for use is deducted. It's the amount attributable to your use before you first reported the problem, plus any later period when the vehicle wasn't out of service for repair. Refunds go to you and any lienholder as their interests appear.

The manufacturer's defenses

Section 57-16A-4 lists the affirmative defenses a manufacturer can raise: the nonconformity doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use and market value; it resulted from abuse, neglect or unauthorized modifications or alterations; or the claim wasn't filed in good faith. Any other defense allowed by law is also available.

Manufacturer dispute programs

Under § 57-16A-6, if the manufacturer has set up or takes part in a fair and impartial informal dispute procedure that substantially complies with the Federal Trade Commission rules in 16 C.F.R. part 703, the refund and replacement remedy isn't available to you until you've used that procedure first. The Attorney General may investigate and decide whether such a program is fair and conforms with the federal rules. The warranty booklet should say whether a program exists and how to start it.

Filing deadline and fees

Section 57-16A-8 requires a lawsuit to be filed within 18 months after the vehicle was first delivered. If you used a manufacturer's dispute program, the deadline is 90 days after the panel's final action, if that's later. Under § 57-16A-9, a consumer who wins is entitled to reasonable attorney fees and court costs from the manufacturer. A consumer who loses and brought the case for frivolous reasons or in bad faith can be ordered to pay the manufacturer's fees and costs.

Section 57-16A-5 makes you choose your path. If you enforce the act, you can't also pursue the Uniform Commercial Code remedies in §§ 55-2-602 through 55-2-608, such as rejecting the vehicle or revoking acceptance.

Resale of returned vehicles

A vehicle returned because it couldn't be fixed under this act or a similar law in another state can't be resold in New Mexico unless the manufacturer gives any prospective buyer full written disclosure of why it was returned. Under § 57-16A-7.1, a manufacturer, agent, dealer or used vehicle dealer that replaced or repurchased a vehicle, or was ordered to, has to attach a written notice saying so before offering it for resale. A buyer can sue anyone who removes that notice, unless the buyer got written notice of the buyback before the sale was completed.

The used-vehicle warranty

Section 57-16A-3.1 covers used vehicles sold by a used motor vehicle dealer, meaning a seller of four or more used vehicles in the previous 12 months, excluding banks, insurers and a few others. The dealer can't disclaim the implied warranty of merchantability until midnight of the 15th calendar day after delivery or 500 miles, whichever comes first. The warranty is met if the vehicle runs substantially free of a defect that significantly limits its use for ordinary driving on public roads, and it doesn't cover damage after the sale from off-road use, racing, towing, abuse, neglect or missed maintenance.

If the warranty is breached, give the seller reasonable notice within 30 days and a chance to repair. You pay half of each of the first two repairs, up to $25 per repair. The dealer's sales agreement must print that rule in bold type, and a sale without it is voidable at your option. A seller who isn't a dealer must hold the title in its own name before trying to sell.
