The District's Automobile Consumer Protection Act

The District's lemon law is the Automobile Consumer Protection Act of 1984, codified at D.C. Code §§ 50-501 to 50-509. Three features stand out. A single failed repair of a safety defect can be enough. You have to go through a District arbitration board before you can sue. And the same chapter includes disclosure rules for used cars sold by dealers.

What's covered

The law covers passenger vehicles that are manufactured for sale, offered for sale, sold, or registered in the District. Buses sold for public transit, motorcycles, motor homes, and motorized recreational vehicles are excluded (§ 50-501). A car counts as "new" during its first 18,000 miles or first two years after delivery to the original purchaser, whichever comes first. Protected consumers include the buyer, a lessee, anyone the car is transferred to while the warranty is in effect, and anyone else entitled to enforce the warranty.

The defect has to significantly impair the car. Under the statute, that means it makes the car unreliable or unsafe for normal use, or lowers its resale value below the average for comparable vehicles. The Attorney General's lemon law consumer alert gives examples such as problems with the engine, transmission, drive shaft, cooling system, electrical system, or fuel system.

Reporting the problem

Under § 50-502(a), you must report the defect to the manufacturer, its agent, or an authorized dealer within the 18,000-mile or two-year period. If you tell the dealer, the dealer has seven days to forward written notice to the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested. The manufacturer must fix the problem for free, even if the repairs happen after the period ends. The OAG notes you don't have to go back to the dealer who sold you the car. Any authorized dealer will do, but follow the repair instructions in your warranty.

When a car is presumed to be a lemon

Under § 50-502(d), the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts if, within the coverage period:

  • the same non-safety defect has been repaired four or more times and is still there
  • the same safety-related defect has been repaired once or more and is still there
  • the car has been out of service for repairs for a total of 30 days or more.

A "safety-related defect" is one that reduces the driver's ability to control the car in normal operation, or that creates a risk of fire, explosion, or another life-threatening malfunction. The 30-day count is extended for any time repairs weren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood, or another natural disaster.

Refund or replacement, your choice

If the defect can't be fixed, the manufacturer must, at your option, either give you a comparable replacement or take the car back and refund the full purchase price, including sales tax, license and registration fees, and similar government charges. The mileage deduction is small. The manufacturer can deduct no more than 10 cents per mile, and only for miles beyond the first 12,000. It can also deduct for damage that isn't normal wear and isn't caused by the defect.

For example, if you return a car with 15,500 miles, only 3,500 miles count, so the deduction can't be more than $350. Return it at 11,000 miles and there's no mileage deduction at all. According to the OAG, the use allowance doesn't apply if you take a replacement instead.

The manufacturer can defend by showing the defect doesn't significantly impair the car, or that it came from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications (§ 50-502(c)).

The Board of Consumer Claims Arbitration

Before you can get a refund or replacement in court, you must first file a claim with the Board of Consumer Claims Arbitration (§ 50-502(f)). You can sue only if the Board rejects the case or you reject its decision. Section 50-503 sets out how the Board works:

  • It has seven mayoral appointees, including two District attorneys, two people trained in arbitration, a consumer advocate, and a member with experience in manufacturing or sales.
  • Within five business days of receiving your written claim, it decides whether the claim qualifies and notifies the manufacturer. It can reject claims that a majority finds frivolous, fraudulent, or outside its authority.
  • Cases go to three arbitrators, or to one attorney arbitrator if both sides agree. You must be notified at least five days before the hearing. Hearings are public and electronically recorded.
  • You can testify, call and cross-examine witnesses, present evidence, and bring a lawyer. The Board can issue subpoenas, and the arbitrators can hire independent technical experts.
  • Arbitrators can award lemon-law relief, relief under other laws, and reasonable attorney fees, and can charge the manufacturer the costs of arbitration.
  • A written decision is due within 60 days of accepting the case, with a deadline for compliance.

Either side can ask Superior Court to enforce the decision. Either side can also petition the court within 20 days of receiving the decision to vacate it and get a new trial. If the court grants a new trial for reasons other than a flaw in the arbitration, the Board's decision is admitted as evidence and presumed correct. That makes a strong case before the Board especially valuable.

The code still places the Board in the Department of Consumer and Regulatory Affairs. That agency was split in 2022, and its consumer protection work now belongs to the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP).

Disclosure, waivers, and buybacks

Manufacturers and dealers must tell buyers in writing about their rights under this law for any vehicle sold or registered in the District. Any contract term that waives or limits those rights is void, and the rights pass to anyone who later owns the car (§ 50-504). A returned car must be reported to the city so the title can note the return, and a dealer reselling it must disclose that in the sales contract before you sign. The OAG says to look for the words "manufacturer's buyback."

Used cars from dealers

Under § 50-505, a dealer can't offer a used car for sale without first telling you in writing about any material mechanical defect it knows of, and about any fire, water, collision, or other damage that cost more than $1,000 to repair. The dealer must also tell you whether it inspected the car. "Known" covers information the dealer learned from its own inspection, a previous owner, an auction salesperson, or any other source. A dealer that breaks this rule faces a fine of $300 to $1,000 for a first offense, and up to $5,000 plus possible loss of its license for repeat offenses. You can recover damages, but these claims also go to the Board first.

Deadline and practical steps

You must file any lawsuit under this chapter within four years of the original delivery date (§ 50-507). The law adds to your other legal rights and doesn't take any away. The OAG recommends keeping every repair order and making sure each one fully describes the defect. Keep a dated log of problems and conversations, and put complaints in writing.
