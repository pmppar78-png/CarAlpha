The Motor Vehicle Warranty Enforcement Act

Mississippi's lemon law is the Motor Vehicle Warranty Enforcement Act, Miss. Code Ann. §§ 63-17-151 through 63-17-165, in effect since July 1, 1985. The Mississippi Motor Vehicle Commission posts the full text of the Act. In its findings, the Legislature calls a car a major consumer purchase and says a defective one creates a hardship. It also treats a franchised dealer as the manufacturer's agent. The Act's aim is to have good-faith warranty complaints resolved within a set time, and to give buyers a path to a replacement or full refund when a new vehicle can't be fixed. It doesn't limit any other rights you have under Mississippi law.

Who and what the Act covers

A consumer is someone who bought a motor vehicle, not for resale, and uses it mainly for personal, family or household purposes. The definition also includes anyone the vehicle is transferred to for those purposes while an express warranty still applies, and anyone else the warranty lets enforce it.

A motor vehicle is a powered vehicle sold in Mississippi and used on the state's public roads to carry people or property. Demonstrators and lease-purchase vehicles count if the manufacturer issued a warranty as a condition of the sale. The Act excludes:

  • vehicles that run only on tracks
  • off-road vehicles
  • motorcycles and mopeds
  • electric personal assistive mobility devices
  • parts and components the motor home maker added to or assembled on a motor home

The Act enforces the manufacturer's written express warranty, meaning a written promise that materials or workmanship are defect-free or will perform at a set level for a set time. Implied warranties aren't included in that definition. They're still available under other law, since the Act doesn't limit other remedies.

The one-year window

Under § 63-17-157, you must report the problem to the manufacturer or its agent, such as the dealer, during the express warranty or the first year after the original delivery, whichever ends first. Once you report it in time, the manufacturer must make the repairs needed to bring the vehicle into line with the warranty, even if the work happens after the window closes. The statute doesn't set its own mileage limit. A warranty that ends by mileage before the year is up, though, ends the window too.

The window can be extended. If you reported a warranty problem and it still hasn't been repaired when the warranty or the one-year period runs out, § 63-17-159(4) allows that period to be extended.

When repairs count as a reasonable number

A reasonable number of repair attempts is presumed if, within the warranty or the first year, whichever ends first, either of these happens:

  • The manufacturer or its agent has tried to repair substantially the same nonconformity three or more times, and the problem continues.
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs of the nonconformity for 15 or more working days in total since delivery.

Downtime for routine maintenance in the owner's manual doesn't count toward the 15 days. The 15 days can be extended for any time repair service isn't available because of conditions outside the manufacturer's control.

The problem has to be a defect or condition that impairs the vehicle's use, market value or safety to you.

Written notice and the final repair

Before you can use the Act's remedies, you or your representative must notify the manufacturer in writing that the vehicle needs repair. The manufacturer has to list its zone or regional service office addresses in the owner's manual, which is where to send the letter. The process then works like this:

  1. The manufacturer must immediately tell you about a reasonably accessible repair facility.
  2. After you deliver the vehicle there, the manufacturer has ten working days to bring it into conformity with the warranty.
  3. If you tell the manufacturer the vehicle still isn't fixed, it must tell you whether it has an informal dispute settlement procedure and give you a copy of the Act. It doesn't have to repeat that notice if it already gave it to you.

Your letter should identify the vehicle and VIN, describe the problem, list the repair dates and days out of service, and ask for a final repair. Keep a copy and proof it was received, along with every repair order.

Replacement or refund

If the manufacturer or its agent can't fix the problem after a reasonable number of attempts, § 63-17-159(1) requires the manufacturer to let you choose:

  • Replacement with a comparable vehicle, meaning an identical or reasonably equivalent one, that's acceptable to you.
  • Refund of the full purchase price, plus all reasonably incurred collateral charges. The manufacturer takes title to the vehicle.

Collateral charges are costs beyond the manufacturer's sticker price. They include dealer preparation, undercoating, transportation, towing, replacement rental cars and title charges. The purchase price is the cash price you paid. If you financed the vehicle through a retail installment contract, it's the cash sale price defined in § 63-19-3.

Either way, the manufacturer subtracts a use allowance of 20 cents per mile you drove. As an example, at 12,000 miles that's $2,400. Refunds are paid to you and to any lienholder of record, according to their interests.

The manufacturer's defenses

A manufacturer can defend a claim by showing any of these:

  • The problem doesn't impair the vehicle's use, market value or safety.
  • The problem came from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modifications or alterations by the consumer.
  • The claim wasn't filed in good faith.
  • Any other defense the law allows.

Under § 63-17-161, a consumer whose claim a court finds was filed in bad faith, solely to harass, or with no real legal or factual issue must pay the manufacturer's court costs caused by that claim.

Manufacturer dispute programs

Mississippi doesn't run a state lemon-law arbitration board. Under § 63-17-163, however, if a manufacturer has an informal dispute settlement procedure that fully complies with the FTC rules at 16 C.F.R. part 703, you must use it before you can claim a refund or replacement under the Act. That requirement doesn't apply if the manufacturer failed to tell you the procedure exists.

Filing deadlines and attorney fees

A lawsuit under the Act must be filed by the earlier of one year after the express warranty expires or 18 months after the original delivery. If you used a manufacturer's dispute program, you have 90 days after the panel's final action instead. If you finally prevail, the court may award your reasonable costs and expenses, including attorney fees based on actual time spent. Violations of the Act are also subject to the remedies in the state's consumer protection law, Title 75, Chapter 24, of the Mississippi Code.
