The structure of Kentucky's lemon law

Kentucky's new-vehicle lemon law is KRS 367.840 to 367.846, part of the state's consumer protection chapter. The General Assembly created it in 1986, extended it to leases in 1998 and last amended the core section, KRS 367.842, in 2012. A separate set of sections, KRS 367.860 to 367.870, has required manufacturers since 1983 to offer buyers an informal dispute resolution system. The two work together. A lemon-law dispute over a refund or replacement has to go through one of those dispute systems before it can go to court.

KRS 367.840 tells courts to construe the law liberally. It lists three purposes: holding manufacturers accountable for certain nonconformities, limiting how many repair attempts and how much time a manufacturer gets, and requiring a refund or an acceptable replacement "in as expeditious a manner as possible" when repairs fail.

Who and what qualifies

Under the definitions in KRS 367.841, a "buyer" is any resident who buys, contracts to buy or leases a new motor vehicle in Kentucky. For a lease, the buyer can be the lessor, the lessee or both. A "new motor vehicle" is one that's never been titled and is held by the manufacturer, a distributor or a franchised dealer. Manufacturers include new conversion van manufacturers.

The law covers self-propelled vehicles that must be registered for highway use, except for:

  • Vehicles substantially altered after the first sale from a dealer to an individual
  • Motor homes
  • Motorcycles and mopeds
  • Farm tractors and other farm production machinery
  • Vehicles with more than two axles

KRS 367.846 applies the law to vehicles bought after July 15, 1986, and leased after July 15, 1998.

A "nonconformity" is a failure to conform to the manufacturer's written warranty that substantially impairs the vehicle's use, value or safety. Kentucky includes safety in that test, so a defect that makes the vehicle unsafe can qualify even if it doesn't greatly affect its value.

The 12-month, 12,000-mile window

The protection period is the first 12,000 miles of operation or the first 12 months after delivery, whichever ends first. If the manufacturer or its agents can't fix the nonconformity after a reasonable number of attempts during that time, KRS 367.842(1) says the buyer "shall report the nonconformity, in writing, to the manufacturer." Send that letter to the manufacturer itself, not just the dealer, and keep a copy along with proof that it was delivered.

When enough attempts are presumed

Within the same 12-month or 12,000-mile period, the law presumes that a reasonable number of attempts were made if:

  • The same nonconformity, defect or condition has been repaired four or more times by the manufacturer and still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service because of repairs to that same problem for a total of at least 30 calendar days

Both tests are tied to a single problem. Repairs to several unrelated defects aren't combined to reach four attempts or 30 days. The 30-day period is extended by a reasonable time when parts or supplies can't be had because of war, invasion, civil unrest, fire, flood or natural disaster.

Replacement or refund, at the buyer's option

When the manufacturer can't correct a defect that substantially impairs use, value or safety within the period, the buyer chooses between a comparable replacement vehicle and a refund of the full purchase price. The statute defines that price to include:

  • The amount paid for the vehicle
  • Finance charges
  • All sales tax
  • License and registration fees and similar government charges
  • All collateral charges

The manufacturer deducts a reasonable allowance for use, which KRS 367.841 limits to use directly attributable to the buyer, excluding any time the vehicle was out of service because of the nonconformity. Refunds go to the buyer and any lienholder as their interests appear on the state's title records. If there's a loan, it must be paid off in full, including finance and other charges, before the exchange or refund, unless the lender agrees to move its lien to the replacement vehicle.

Defenses the manufacturer can raise

A manufacturer can defend a claim by proving either of these:

  • The problem doesn't substantially impair the vehicle's use, value or safety
  • The problem results from abuse, neglect, or unauthorized modification or alteration by the buyer

The required dispute resolution step

Under KRS 367.842(4), disputes over a refund or replacement must be resolved through a dispute system set up under KRS 367.860 to 367.870, or under the FTC's rules at 16 CFR part 703. You must use that system before you can seek relief in court.

KRS 367.865 requires each manufacturer's system to be impartial, accessible and fast, and to cost the buyer nothing. The manufacturer can't refuse to arbitrate a dispute that arose in the first two years or 25,000 miles of ownership, whichever comes first. That's longer than the lemon-law presumption period. You're entitled to an oral hearing unless you agree in writing to a decision based on documents alone. At least 51% of the panel must have no involvement in making, distributing or selling vehicles.

The decision binds the manufacturer. It binds you only if the manufacturer has chosen a system that's binding on all buyers. In that case, you must sign a bold-faced disclosure warning that you're choosing the system instead of going to court before the dispute can proceed. Under KRS 367.867, a federal 16 CFR 703 program satisfies the state requirement only if it gives each party the right to an oral hearing.

Going to court

A buyer who suffers an ascertainable loss of money or property because of a violation can sue under KRS 367.220, the consumer protection act's private-action section, as provided in KRS 367.843. A lemon-law action must be filed within two years after the vehicle was originally delivered. A court may award reasonable attorney's fees to a winning plaintiff. Any agreement that waives, limits or disclaims your lemon-law rights is void, and the law doesn't limit remedies available under other laws.

The statute protects franchised dealers. Nothing in it makes a dealer liable or gives a buyer a claim against one, and KRS 367.844 bars manufacturers from trying to shift that liability to dealers. Your claim is against the manufacturer.

The Attorney General's role

Under KRS 367.845, a manufacturer's failure to comply is unlawful, and the Attorney General can enforce the law and charge the costs to the manufacturer. The Attorney General can also monitor manufacturers' dispute systems and review their complaint records.
