California's lemon law: the Song-Beverly Act and the Tanner presumption

California's protection for defective new cars sits inside the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, mainly Civil Code § 1793.2. The section most people mean by "the lemon law" is § 1793.22, the Tanner Consumer Protection Act, which sets out when a car is presumed to be a lemon. The core rule is simple. If the manufacturer or its dealers can't fix a warranty defect after a reasonable number of tries, the manufacturer has to promptly replace the car or pay you back. You pick which. The law says outright that you can't be forced to take a replacement.

Which vehicles are covered

The Attorney General's consumer guide explains that the law covers most new vehicles bought or leased in California that are still under the manufacturer's new-vehicle warranty. Section 1793.22(e) fills in the details:

  • Personal use: any new vehicle bought or used mainly for personal, family, or household purposes.
  • Small business use: a new vehicle under 10,000 pounds gross weight, bought or used mainly for business, by a person or company with no more than five vehicles registered in California.
  • Dealer-owned cars and demonstrators sold with a manufacturer's new-car warranty.
  • Motor homes: the chassis, the cab, and the drive portion are covered. The living quarters aren't, though they fall under the general warranty rules in § 1793.2(d)(1).
  • Leases: lessees count as buyers (§ 1793.2(d)(2)(D)).

Motorcycles and off-highway vehicles that aren't registered under the Vehicle Code are excluded. The AG also notes a special rule for full-time active-duty service members. If you were stationed or living in California when you bought or leased, you're covered even if the car was bought or registered in another state.

When a car is presumed to be a lemon

The Tanner presumption applies when one of these happens within 18 months of delivery or 18,000 miles, whichever comes first:

  • A defect likely to cause death or serious bodily injury has been repaired two or more times and still isn't fixed, and you've notified the manufacturer directly at least once.
  • The same defect has been repaired four or more times and you've notified the manufacturer directly at least once.
  • The car has been out of service for warranty repairs for a total of more than 30 calendar days since delivery. The days don't have to be in a row. The limit is extended only for delays truly beyond the manufacturer's control.

The direct-notice requirement for the two-repair and four-repair counts applies only if the manufacturer clearly disclosed it in the warranty or owner's manual. If it did, send the notice to the address given there. The presumption is rebuttable, so the manufacturer can try to disprove it. It isn't the only way to qualify, either. The AG stresses that these are not requirements. A car can still be a lemon after fewer repairs, or outside 18 months, as long as the warranty defect substantially impairs the car's use, value, or safety and wasn't caused by unauthorized or unreasonable use.

A related rule in § 1793.2(b) says warranty repairs generally have to be completed within 30 days unless you agree in writing to a longer time.

What a refund or replacement includes

Restitution (refund) under § 1793.2(d)(2)(B) is the actual price paid or payable, including transportation charges and factory-installed options. Add-ons installed by the dealer or by you aren't included. The refund also covers collateral charges such as sales tax, license and registration fees, and other official fees. On top of that come incidental damages under § 1794, which include reasonable repair, towing, and rental car costs you actually paid.

Replacement under § 1793.2(d)(2)(A) has to be a new vehicle substantially identical to yours, with full new-car warranties. The manufacturer also pays the sales tax, license, registration, and other official fees on the replacement, plus the same incidental damages.

The mileage offset. The manufacturer can deduct for your use of the car, but only for the miles driven before you first brought it in for the problem. The formula is the price paid (including transportation and factory options) times the miles before that first repair visit, divided by 120,000. If you drove 6,000 miles before the first visit on a $48,000 car, the offset would be $2,400. Miles driven after the first repair visit don't count against you.

State-certified arbitration

Many manufacturers run arbitration programs certified by the Department of Consumer Affairs' Arbitration Certification Program (ACP). The ACP says these programs are free to consumers and much faster than court. To be certified under § 1793.22(d), a program must:

  • Follow the FTC's informal dispute rules in 16 CFR Part 703.
  • Issue decisions that bind the manufacturer if you accept them, but not you.
  • Require the manufacturer to carry out an accepted decision within 30 days.
  • Order a refund or replacement figured the same way the statute does.
  • Provide, at no cost to you, an inspection by an independent automotive expert if the arbitrator asks.
  • Keep manufacturer and dealer staff from arguing the merits unless you get to take part too.

Whether you have to use arbitration depends on the manufacturer. If it has a certified program and told you about it in writing in time, you have to try that program before you can rely on the Tanner presumption in court. You aren't bound by a decision you reject, and you can still sue afterward, including in small claims court. The arbitration decision can be used as evidence. Time spent in arbitration also pauses the deadline for filing suit, from when you file the complaint until the decision or, if you accept it, the manufacturer's deadline to comply.

Not every manufacturer has a certified program. For those that don't, the ACP points buyers to the New Motor Vehicle Board's Consumer Mediation Services Program at (916) 445-1888. The ACP site also flags changes to lemon-law procedures under AB 1755 and SB 26, which apply to manufacturers that opted in. Check the ACP's list of opted-in manufacturers before you file, because the steps can differ.

Lemon buybacks on the used market

Under § 1793.22(f), a car returned under California's lemon law, or a similar law in another state, can't be resold unless three conditions are met. The defect has to be clearly disclosed, it has to be fixed, and the manufacturer has to give the new buyer a written one-year warranty against that defect. The AG adds that resold buybacks must be identified as a "lemon law buyback" and carry a lemon sticker on the door. If one is sold "as is" without proper disclosure, the buyer may still have rights under the lemon law.

Practical steps

  • Get a repair order every time the car is in the shop, even when the dealer says it "couldn't duplicate" the problem. The order should show the dates in and out and the mileage.
  • Check your owner's manual for a direct-notice requirement and address, and send that notice in writing.
  • Keep receipts for rentals, towing, and out-of-pocket repairs. They're recoverable as incidental damages.
  • Read the warranty booklet to see whether your manufacturer has a certified arbitration program. That tells you whether arbitration comes before court.
