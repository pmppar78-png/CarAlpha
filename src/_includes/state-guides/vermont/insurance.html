Vermont's insurance requirement

Section 800 of Title 23 sets Vermont's financial responsibility rule. No owner of a vehicle that has to be registered, and no operator who has to be licensed or hold a learner's permit, may drive or let someone drive the vehicle on Vermont highways without an automobile liability policy or bond in effect. The policy has to carry at least these limits:

  • $25,000 for one person killed or injured
  • $50,000 for two or more people killed or injured
  • $10,000 for damage to property in any one crash

In shorthand, that is 25/50/10. As an alternative, an owner can file evidence of self-insurance of $115,000 with the Commissioner of Motor Vehicles, kept up in a form the Commissioner prescribes. The Commissioner may also require evidence of financial responsibility before a vehicle inspection under § 1222.

Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage

Vermont builds uninsured and underinsured motorist protection into every liability policy. Under 23 V.S.A. § 941(a), no liability policy for a vehicle registered or principally garaged in Vermont can be delivered or issued unless it covers people insured under it who are legally entitled to recover from owners or operators of uninsured, underinsured or hit-and-run vehicles. The coverage applies to bodily injury, sickness, disease and death, and to property damage.

The required limits are higher than the liability minimum. Section 941(c) sets uninsured motorist coverage on new or renewed policies at no less than $50,000 for one person and $100,000 for two or more people. When your liability limits are above those numbers, your uninsured motorist limits match your liability limits unless you, as the policyholder, direct otherwise.

The property damage part pays up to $10,000 per claim, subject to a $150 deductible. That deductible doesn't apply if you have other direct damage coverage on your vehicle, such as collision. In that case, the uninsured motorist property coverage pays your collision deductible, and the collision coverage handles the rest of the claim up to its limits.

A vehicle counts as underinsured to the extent that its liability limits are lower than your uninsured motorist limits, or payments to other people injured in the crash have reduced the available liability insurance below your limits. A vehicle whose insurer becomes insolvent within a year after the crash can also be treated as uninsured.

Section 941(b) also requires every liability policy to cover liability for bodily injury or death within Vermont or elsewhere in the United States and Canada.

Finding out the other driver's coverage

After a crash, § 941(g) gives you a way to learn what coverage applies. If you reasonably claim a right to recover damages and send a written request to an insurer that may be liable for the claim, the insurer has 30 days to give you a statement, signed by an authorized agent, naming the insurer and the insured and listing the limits of liability coverage.

Carrying proof of insurance

Under § 800(c), every operator of a vehicle required to be registered must have proof of financial responsibility while driving on Vermont highways. You can show that proof on a phone or other portable electronic device, and doing so doesn't by itself give an officer consent to look at anything else on the device.

If you're cited for not having proof, you won't be convicted as long as you send or produce, within seven business days after the stop, proof of coverage that was in effect at the time of the stop.

Penalties

Driving without the required coverage brings a civil penalty of up to $500 under § 800(b), and the violation is a traffic violation under chapter 24 of Title 23. Failing to carry proof while you drive is a separate violation under § 800(d), with a civil penalty of up to $100.

When the Commissioner requires proof of future responsibility

Section 801 lists the situations in which the Commissioner requires proof of financial responsibility, at the same 25/50/10 limits, before you can keep driving. They include:

  • A conviction for a death caused by careless and negligent operation or by reckless driving
  • A violation of § 1201 of Title 23, or a suspension under § 1205
  • Leaving the scene of a crash that injured a person or property without stopping to help
  • Operating a vehicle without the owner's consent, or driving after a suspension, revocation or refusal of a license
  • An unpaid Vermont court judgment for damages from a crash based on a violation of Title 23
  • Involvement as the operator in a crash causing injury, death, or property damage of $3,000 or more in total

For several of these, the requirement doesn't apply if you show that a standard automobile liability policy at the required amounts was in effect at the time.

Under § 802, if you don't furnish the proof within 20 days after the Commissioner mails you notice, the Commissioner suspends your license, or your right to drive in Vermont if you're unlicensed or a nonresident, until you do. Section 804 says the proof comes from an insurer or surety company authorized in Vermont, which files a certificate with the Commissioner. That policy can't be canceled without 15 days' notice to the Commissioner.

Once you've had to file, § 803 bars license renewal unless the proof stays on file, and your license is suspended on the date the insurance expires or ends. The Commissioner releases you from the requirement one year after the crash, conviction or judgment under § 809. That period is three years for a death caused by careless or reckless driving or for a second or later § 1201 conviction, and if you were suspended, the clock runs from your reinstatement eligibility date.

What coverage costs

The NAIC's 2024 Average Premium Supplement, Table 5, lists a combined average of $1,039.63 in Vermont for liability, collision and comprehensive. The Insurance Research Council's estimate puts Vermont's share of uninsured drivers at 11.8%, 29th in the nation.
