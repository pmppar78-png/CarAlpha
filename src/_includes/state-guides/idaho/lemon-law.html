Idaho's lemon law in brief

Idaho's lemon law is Title 48, Chapter 9 of the Idaho Code, titled "New Motor Vehicle Warranties — Manufacturer's Duty to Repair, Refund or Replace." It covers new vehicles bought or leased with a manufacturer's written warranty. If a defect can't be fixed after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must replace the vehicle or refund what you paid. The Attorney General's lemon law pamphlet walks through the process for owners, dealers and manufacturers. It also says the law doesn't limit your warranty rights or your remedies under other laws.

Idaho has no state-run arbitration board. Disputes go first to the manufacturer's own arbitration program, which every manufacturer doing business here must offer within the state, and then to district court if necessary.

Which vehicles qualify

Under § 48-901, you're a "consumer" if you buy or lease a new vehicle for personal business use or for personal, family or household purposes, as long as it isn't for resale or sublease. Anyone the vehicle is transferred to for the same purposes while the express warranty is still running also counts. A lease must run longer than four months. The vehicle must be sold or licensed in Idaho. The law excludes:

  • Motorcycles and farm tractors
  • Trailers
  • Any vehicle with a gross laden weight over 12,000 pounds

The Attorney General's pamphlet adds ATVs to that list. The pamphlet also lists what the lemon law won't help with: a dealer's false statements (the Consumer Protection Act may cover those), trouble making your payments, and buyer's remorse. A "manufacturer" is any company that distributes at least 10 new vehicles a year to dealers.

Report problems early

The manufacturer's duty to repair, in § 48-902, applies to any defect you report while the express warranty is in effect, within two years of the original delivery, or before the odometer reaches 24,000 miles, whichever comes first. Once you've reported the defect within that window, the repair obligation continues even after the warranty or the two years run out.

The Attorney General gives an example of how quickly that window can close. A driver who puts 24,000 miles on a car in six months and first reports a defect in month seven is no longer covered by the lemon law, even with five years of factory warranty left. The window is extended for any time repairs aren't available because of war, invasion, a strike, fire, flood or another natural disaster.

The reasonable-attempts presumption

Under § 48-903, the manufacturer is presumed to have had a reasonable number of attempts when, within the warranty, two-year or 24,000-mile period:

  • The same defect has been repaired four or more times and still exists
  • The vehicle has been out of service for repairs for a cumulative 30 or more business days
  • A defect causing complete failure of the braking or steering system, likely to cause death or serious bodily injury, has been repaired at least once and still exists

In every case, the manufacturer gets at least one opportunity to repair the vehicle before the presumption applies.

The four-repair and 30-day presumptions work against the manufacturer only after it, its agent or its dealer has received written notice from you, or on your behalf, at least once and has had a chance to fix the defect. A dealer that receives your notice must forward it to the manufacturer by certified mail, return receipt requested.

Idaho also leaves room for a late fix to fail. If you first reported the defect during the express warranty, you can still get a refund or replacement when the reasonable number of attempts happens any time within three years of delivery.

Writing the notice letter

Several failed repairs don't guarantee a buyback by themselves. The Attorney General advises sending a written notice that explains the defect in detail, says your vehicle is a lemon, and demands a refund or replacement under Idaho's lemon law. Send it by certified mail with a return receipt. If you send it to the dealer, send a copy to the manufacturer as well. Under § 48-903(7), the warranty guide must include a capitalized notice explaining this requirement and your right to use the manufacturer's arbitration program.

Refunds, replacements and the use deduction

If you win, the manufacturer must either give you a comparable replacement vehicle or take the vehicle back and refund what you paid, including the value of any trade-in. The refund is capped at 105% of the manufacturer's suggested retail price. That cap includes factory options and anything the dealer added within 30 days of delivery. The manufacturer must also refund sales or excise tax, license and registration fees, and towing and rental costs from warranty repairs. You get an itemized statement of every amount refunded.

The use deduction can't be more than the miles you drove before the arbitration hearing multiplied by the purchase price and divided by 120,000. For a $36,000 vehicle with 10,000 miles on it, that works out to $3,000. If the manufacturer offers a replacement, you can turn it down and insist on a refund instead. If the manufacturer doesn't break out the sales tax separately or claim a tax refund within one year, the State Tax Commission may refund the tax directly to you.

Leased vehicles get a refund only, under § 48-904. You receive the unused share of your down payment (the down payment divided by the lease's length in months, times the months left after arbitration), plus the other charges you actually paid. Your lease payments count as the use allowance. The leasing company gets its early termination charges and the vehicle's residual value. Your down-payment refund and the leasing company's payment together can't exceed 105% of the original MSRP.

The manufacturer can refuse a buyback if the defect doesn't impair the vehicle's use or market value, or if it was caused by abuse, neglect or unauthorized changes made by anyone other than the manufacturer or its dealers.

The manufacturer's arbitration program

Section 48-906 requires every manufacturer doing business in Idaho to run or join an informal dispute settlement program located in the state that meets federal rules (16 CFR part 703). You generally have to use it before suing for a refund or replacement, unless the manufacturer lets you go straight to court. The rules guarantee you several things:

  • Information about Idaho's lemon law for you and the arbitrator
  • The right to see, and comment on, every document the arbitrator considers
  • An in-person presentation in Idaho, unless you agree to decide the case by phone or on documents alone, in which case manufacturer and dealer representatives can't take part in the decision
  • A chance to have your own technical expert evaluate a manufacturer's claim that the vehicle is "within specifications"
  • Copies of known technical service bulletins for your problem, at reasonable cost
  • The right to bring an attorney, although attorney fees for arbitration aren't recoverable

Any fee you're charged can't exceed the conciliation court filing fee in the county where the arbitration takes place.

The decision isn't binding unless both sides agree in advance. Under § 48-907, either party can move the case to district court for a new trial within 30 days of receiving the decision. If no one does, a party can ask the court to confirm the decision. The written decision may be used as evidence later. If a court finds that either side removed the case in bad faith, for example to stall or with a frivolous claim, it must award the other side three times its actual damages plus costs and attorney fees.

Going to court

You can sue under § 48-909 and recover costs and reasonable attorney fees. A lawsuit must be filed within three years of the original delivery. If you used arbitration within that time and you're unhappy with the result, you have three months from the final decision to take it to court. The Attorney General can also treat violations as breaches of the Idaho Consumer Protection Act.

Resold lemons

A returned vehicle, including one returned under another state's law, can be resold or re-leased in Idaho only with a separate written notice in capital letters saying it was returned for an uncured warranty defect. The manufacturer must also provide its original warranty for at least 12 months or 12,000 miles after resale. A vehicle returned for an unrepaired, complete brake or steering failure can't be resold in Idaho at all.
